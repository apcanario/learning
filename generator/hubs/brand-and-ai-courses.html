Frame

The brand is the method, and the method is the record

Working name to be decided. "I get consumed by something, build a syllabus, go on the journey, and record it so I don't forget it." Karate was season one. The half marathon, the weight loss and the history course are the next journeys; building with AI is the fourth. Plurality is the register, not the hook: each piece leads with one journey and the breadth shows across pieces. ADHD is public under his name (decided 27 Oct 2026); everything else gets a pre-mortem before first disclosure. Failures stay in the record.

Pick

Journeys, recorded. Shōmen is one path off it, not the engine. Leaving corporate is not on the table for 24 months; this is a build alongside Infios, measured quarterly.

  • Generalist brands die on variety and live on a repeatable format.
  • The ADHD-creator space sells relatability; this has rigour.
  • Every existing output already fits: vlog, syllabi, journal, pcblueprint.

Override if: after six pieces in the format nobody can describe the channel back to him in one sentence. Then the format is wrong, not the breadth.

  • Cost: 5–7 h/week; €0–300 in gear; an editor only after the cadence has held for six months.
  • Horizon: 18–36 months to 1,000 YouTube subscribers at a bi-weekly cadence; a brand that moves Shōmen revenue is a 24-month question at best.
  • Where the research pushes back: the data says season one is one journey in one format, and karate is the thin niche where a 10–20K channel is visible. History and AI are a year-two layer by the format-drift evidence. His call.

Superseded on 28 Oct 2026 by the A9, A2 and A6 units, after Audit 1 found 23 of 91 claims failing and the deliverables missing; kept as the record. Research run 27 Oct 2026 across five tracks (YouTube mechanics; Instagram and TikTok; platform strategy; retention and conversion; niche landscape). It stands as the written material for A2, A6 and A9 and feeds A4 and A7. Most figures are correlational and vendor-published; the text says so where it applies. Gaps: no measured calendar-time-to-milestone data, no short-form→long-form conversion rate, no primary newsletter churn, no Portuguese-language RPM or SEO data; the YPP February 2027 threshold is cited via AIR, not verified against YouTube Help.

With 5–7 hours a week, the evidence says build on YouTube long-form (one 10–25 minute episode every two weeks, plus one or two Shorts cut from it), hold a weekly email list from day one, treat Instagram as a low-cost relationship channel fed by the same material, and stay off TikTok entirely. The most robust finding across every dataset in this research is not about platform choice at all: it is that consistency on one platform for 2–3 years beats breadth, that strategy switches mid-stream are the documented loser, and that the overwhelming majority of channels that fail simply stop publishing (83% of sub-1K YouTube channels uploaded nothing in a 360-day window) (vidIQ Research). At a bi-weekly cadence the realistic timeline is roughly 2–3 years and 40–70 videos to 1,000 subscribers, another 1–2 years to 10,000, and no documented solo creator in any adjacent niche reached 50,000 in under four years. Median early views will be in the low hundreds and that is normal, not a signal to change course. Retention is driven by a repeated format and predictable schedule, not by topic breadth, which is a direct warning against spreading five journeys across a single channel in year one. Conversion from audience to buyer runs at 1–3% of an engaged, owned list for a paid product and roughly 0.4–1.2% of social followers per merch drop, so Shōmen sales from an audience under 10,000 will be a few orders per drop, not a business line. Portugal adds very little (no karate or running creator of scale, platform payouts largely unavailable) and English is the right call; the karate niche itself is thin above the top three channels, which is the single most under-exploited opening in the whole picture. Much of the quantitative material here is correlational and vendor-published, and the report flags it as such throughout.

Growth is per-video recommendation, not subscriber push, on every platform

The mental model to drop first is "followers see my posts." YouTube's Senior Director of Growth, Todd Beaupré, said in September 2026 that subscribers skip roughly 90% of uploads in their feed, that suggested videos rather than the subscriptions feed are the biggest source of views from subscribed channels, and that ranking is assembled per viewer per session from watch history, context, time of day and device (PPC Land). The system judges individual videos, not channel averages, and YouTube says explicitly there is no "penalty box" for breaks and no permanent damage from one weak video (Search Engine Journal). That has two practical consequences for a bi-weekly publisher: a gap between uploads is not punished by a mechanism, and the only levers that matter are per-video packaging (click-through rate on impressions) and per-video satisfaction (average view duration, satisfaction surveys). YouTube's own definition of clickbait is high CTR plus low average view duration plus fewer impressions than expected, and such videos are recommended less (YouTube Help). Half of all channels sit at a 2–10% impressions CTR; vendor heuristics put a healthy target at 4–6% CTR and 30–45% average percentage viewed on a 15–30 minute video (vidIQ). The widely repeated folklore of "test audiences of 10–100 viewers" and "layered rollouts" has no official source; YouTube has only confirmed that early impressions go to diverse audiences including people who never watched the channel.

Instagram works on the same logic with different weights. Adam Mosseri named watch time, likes and sends as the top three ranking signals in January 2025, with sends weighted more for reach to non-followers and likes more for followers (Social Media Today). Reels and the newer Trial Reels (shown only to non-followers, requires 1,000 followers) are the only formats built to reach strangers; Stories reach followers only; carousels get a "second chance" resurfacing to followers who swiped past (Lia Haberman). The reach a small account can expect is modest: in Socialinsider's brand dataset, a 1–5K account averages 580 views per Reel and 993 per carousel, and annual follower growth at that tier fell from 38% in 2024 to 22% in 2025 (Socialinsider). Creator accounts engage roughly three times brand accounts in Metricool's data, so the absolute numbers are a floor, but the direction (halving growth rates year on year) is the point. TikTok's only official explanation of the For You feed dates from 2020 and states that follower count and past hits are "not direct factors" (TikTok Newsroom); measured 2025 data shows 1–5K brand accounts averaging just 350 views per video while growing followers 150% a year, an arms race in which output rose 40% and views per video fell 23% (Socialinsider). Across all three, the honest summary is that growth comes from the occasional piece the system pushes to non-followers, not from steady per-post reach to the people who already follow.

Email is the one channel where the follower count means something, and the numbers back that up: beehiiv reports a 41.24% average open rate across 65,000 newsletters in 2025 against a 3.23% click rate (beehiiv). Patreon's 2025 survey found only 24% of users' Instagram time, 32% of YouTube time and 38% of TikTok time is spent on creators they already follow (Net Influencer); the survey is company-sponsored, but a four-in-ten open rate against single-digit feed reach is a different order of magnitude regardless of method. The catch is that a standalone list has no discovery engine. Substack says recommendations plus its app drive 50% of all new subscriptions on the platform (On Substack), and beehiiv says newsletters using its Recommendations grew 2.75x faster, so a list started before any social audience exists is defensible only if it lives on a network with built-in discovery.

Bi-weekly long-form means two to three years to 1,000 subscribers

The benchmark data is unusually good on YouTube and almost nonexistent elsewhere, so this section is YouTube-first. vidIQ's study of 448,413 channels created in the first half of 2025 puts the median 30-day view count of a new long-form channel's first upload at 180, rising to 272 by the tenth, with the best of the first ten reaching a median of 1,948; 57.8% of long-form channels got at least one of their first ten videos past 1,000 views and 30.8% got one past 10,000 (vidIQ Research). These are survivor numbers (channels that quit before ten uploads are excluded), so the true median is lower. Reaching 1,000 subscribers is not rare in the abstract (40.6% of all channels with any subscriber have crossed it) but it is rare in any given year: of 32.9 million sub-1K channels, 1.5% crossed 1,000 in the 360 days to September 2026, and the share depended heavily on volume, with 1.86% for channels adding 1–11 videos, 8.65% for 12–51, and 30.77% for 52+ (vidIQ Research). A bi-weekly cadence (about 26 videos a year) sits in the middle band. Among channels that did reach 1,000, roughly 68% had uploaded 40 or more videos (vidIQ). No large-sample study reports calendar time to milestones; the only durations available are a seven-channel anecdotal sample showing 5–26 months to 1K and 29–36 months to 10K (Social Video Playza), which is illustrative, not evidence.

Two things cut against the pessimism. First, each additional upload compounds: median growth rises monotonically with uploads per month across 10.2 million channels, and the biggest single step is from "occasional" to "repeatable" (vidIQ Research). Moving from bi-weekly to weekly roughly doubles the growth rate in that data (the 1–3/month tier versus 4–7/month), which is the strongest argument for finding a way to a weekly rhythm even if episodes get shorter. Second, back-catalogue discovery is real on YouTube in a way it is not on TikTok; one practitioner estimate puts the crossover point where old videos out-earn new uploads at around month six and 24–26 videos (Humble&Brag), and YouTube itself says videos can gain most of their audience a month or more after upload. The one case in the notes of an unusually fast year one (an assistant professor who published 88 videos in 52 weeks and reached 20K subscribers) shows what weekly-plus output with an existing professional network can do, and also that it took 1.7 videos a week, which is outside a 5–7 hour budget (Sidika Tunc Candogan).

Newsletter benchmarks need to be read with suspicion. beehiiv's headline for 2025 launches is a median of 482 subscribers in month one and 8,314 by the end of year one (beehiiv), but that cohort is self-selected and survives only if it kept sending on a platform that sells growth tools. Every first-person part-time account in the research lands far lower: 100 subscribers after four months and 2,000 at twelve (The Creator Playbook); 2,500 after three years alongside a corporate job and family (The Introverted Networker); 42,000 after three years full-time, with the first 1,000 described as "the slowest" (Amy Suto). Plan on the lower range: a few hundred in year one, low thousands by year three, with growth concentrated in the back half.

One home platform wins; the losers switched mid-stream

No controlled study compares "one platform first" against "everywhere at once" for solo creators, and the report should not pretend one exists. What the large-sample data shows consistently is that consistency on a platform beats breadth and that platform-specific formats do not transfer. Buffer's analysis of 52 million posts found that accounts skipping a week fell below their own baseline growth (a "no-post penalty"), that top-decile accounts post more often and more consistently, and that format performance varies enough by network that content working on one often fails on another (Buffer). AIR Media-Tech's study of 18,000 English-language YouTube channels found that the "transitional" cohort that shifted its Shorts/long-form mix by more than 30 points mid-year had the lowest median subscribers in every niche, below both stable strategies (in Entertainment at 100K–1M: 51,600 versus 72,400 and 73,600) (AIR Media-Tech). AIR is a vendor selling audits and says the data is correlational and quality-uncontrolled; the direction is still the most useful single finding for an ADHD creator, because the failure signature documented across every stalled case in the notes (no niche, cadence swinging from five posts a week to zero, overcommitting, burnout from doing everything at once) is the pivot pattern, not the volume pattern (The Creator Playbook; Amy Suto). Amy Suto's only flat months came from publishing more often than she could sustain, and her rule of picking a cadence you can hit "on your worst week, not your best one" is the right frame for a 5–7 hour budget.

The case for a second format on the same platform is stronger than the case for a second platform. The same AIR dataset finds a 25–40% Shorts share of uploads correlates with more median subscribers in Fitness (+41%), Business (+39%) and Education (+37% at small size), while long-only wins in Gaming, Beauty and Entertainment, and long-form performance weakens above roughly 55% Shorts (AIR Media-Tech). Metricool's 800,000-video sample points the same way: channels posting about five Shorts a month averaged 46% more views per long-form video than channels posting none, and the benefit vanished above 35 Shorts a month (Metricool). Nobody has shown that Shorts subscribers go on to watch long-form; the one Shorts-to-long-form conversion figure cited anywhere (0.04% to 0.5%) is unsourced. Treat Shorts as a cheap top-of-funnel bet that costs an hour per episode, not as distribution for the long-form. The same logic kills TikTok as a feeder: the funnel from TikTok or Reels to YouTube has no measured conversion rate in any source, cross-linking is structurally weak (only 2.7% of TikTok creators with 10K+ followers link a YouTube account) (Click Analytic), and YouTube's own short-form surface is far more generous to small accounts in brand data (15,160 views per Short at under 5K followers versus 350 on TikTok and 625 on Reels) (Socialinsider). Add that Portugal is excluded from TikTok's Creator Rewards Program (Quasa) and that the EU's February 2026 DSA preliminary findings may force a redesign of the recommender in Europe (Crowell & Moring), and TikTok is a third platform with its own native format, no monetisation, regulatory product risk and no evidence of feeding the home channel.

Time budgets settle the rest. Vendor rules of thumb put long-form editing at 30–60 minutes per finished minute (Tasty Edits), so a 10–12 minute episode is roughly 2 hours of scripting and filming plus 4–6 hours of editing spread over two weeks, with two or three Shorts cut from it in another hour; that is 5–6 hours a week and fully consumes the budget. A writing-first track (one weekly newsletter at 2–3 hours, two or three derived LinkedIn or Instagram posts at 1–1.5 hours, 15 minutes a day on comments) also costs 5–6 hours. Running both is 10–12 hours. Justin Welsh's template brings a newsletter issue down to 45 minutes only because it recycles his own best social posts (Justin Welsh); for a video-first creator the equivalent is a short email per episode that carries the video, one thing learned, and a question, which is 30–45 minutes rather than an original essay. Instagram fits in the residual time only as a derivative: a 3–5 post per week rhythm roughly doubles follower growth versus 1–2 in Buffer's 2.1 million post regression (+0.26%/week versus +0.12%) (Buffer), but each post must come from footage and stills already shot for the episode or it is a second production line. LinkedIn deserves a mention it rarely gets in creator advice: Buffer's 2025 data has it as the highest median engagement network (~6.2%), personal profiles out-engage company pages 2.60% to 1.60%, carousels average 1,451 impressions against 606 for video, and links in the post body cost 27% of impressions (Metricool). For a senior corporate professional it is the one network where learning-in-public and athlete-discipline content lands on an audience that already respects the day job; the risk literature on employer conflict is about politics and posting on company time, not about side projects, and side projects "tend to be more acceptable to employers than strong personal stances" (Globe and Mail).

Audiences stay for a repeated format, and leave when the creator stops or drifts

Retention has a thin but consistent evidence base. YouTube's own guidance for growing "Regular" viewers (the tier that returned six or more times in the past year, introduced in July 2025) is to create content "more consistent and similar in topic or format," to build series from topics that already worked, to publish regularly and to respond to comments (YouTube Help). AIR's 18,000-channel study adds that channels on a predictable schedule earned more views per subscriber than channels at the same volume posting on random days, that going dark for 14+ days three or more times a year reduced views per video, and that in Health & Fitness the median subscriber count peaked at 2–5 videos a month (172,000) and fell sharply at 10–20 a month (77,700), with the same 2–5 peak in Education (AIR Media-Tech). Bi-weekly sits inside that band. Third-party guidance treats a Regular-viewer share above 10% as excellent for a new channel, with 20–40% the range for established ones, and warns the metric only becomes meaningful after about 20 uploads (Vidpros); these are not YouTube-published benchmarks. The format evidence from comparable creators is by example rather than by study, but the examples agree: Goal Guys (560K, bounded fitness and productivity goals since 2015), Mike Boyd (2.7M, compressed skill challenges), Nick Bare's episodic Hyrox Prep series and Hellah Sidibe's run streak are all repeated, bounded pursuits, and discovery in each case came from one breakout video while the season structure kept people coming back (Social Blade; yespress.io; Tatler Asia). "Athens 2026, then half marathon 2027" maps directly onto that pattern; the five journeys in the brief do not, at least not in year one. The best examples all started as a single, specific, documented pursuit and widened later.

The community mechanics with measured deltas are small and specific. Patreon's 2022 census of 13,000 creators found those with a named community had 4–8% higher monthly retention, and daily promoters earned 75% more than monthly promoters (Net Influencer). beehiiv's data says newsletters that recommend others are 32x more likely to be recommended back (beehiiv). Discord and Circle communities have no published retention data for solo creators. Email churn is widely quoted at 20–30% a year but every figure found is unsourced aggregator material; the one measured benchmark, a 0.15% unsubscribe rate per send (EmailListValidation), compounds to 7–8% a year at weekly cadence, so the rest of any decay is disengagement rather than opt-out.

The honesty question has a real answer and a real cost. A 2026 structural-equation study of 407 followers found that expertise and trustworthiness, not attractiveness, predict the parasocial relationship that in turn predicts purchase intention (Hidayat et al., Frontiers in Communication); that is the mechanism by which openness about ADHD and mental health builds an audience that buys. The same bond is what the Administrative Science Quarterly 2026 study of 54 successful creators calls "dysfunctional entanglement": hypersensitivity to comments and metrics, distress at algorithm changes and a desire to quit, which the creators who recovered managed by delaying comment reading, depersonalising critique and anchoring in self-set standards (PsyPost). The New Media & Society study of creator burnout found the fear "If I take a break, I'm going to get punished by the algorithm" is pervasive and largely unfounded on YouTube, which says it does not over-weight unpredictive history (Cornell Chronicle). For the audience side, the notes found no study quantifying over-disclosure risk; the men's-mental-health and ADHD audience that exists prefers long-form talking formats (ADHD Chatter at 408K with 83K average views per video at about 1.2 uploads a week; HealthyGamerGG at 3.49M with 91% of its audience on YouTube) over relatability Shorts, whose biggest exemplars show flat growth or a deliberate retreat from the format (OutlierKit; CreatorDB).

Conversion runs at one to three percent of an owned list

The buyer numbers are low and should be planned as such. Substack's official guidance is that 5–10% of free subscribers go paid; an independent aggregation of hundreds of publication reports puts the median at 3%, with only 20% of publications above 5% (Really Good Business Ideas), and a named AI newsletter reports a steady ~1% (121 paid of 13,413 free) (Artificial Ignorance). Practitioner guidance for mini-courses sold to an engaged email list is 2–3% of subscribers per launch (Teachery). Patreon's free tiers convert to paid at roughly 0.7% a month by Sacra's estimate (Sacra), and Patreon reports $52 a year per fan and $110 per paying member (Net Influencer). Merch is the weakest-sourced figure: an aggregator attributes 0.4–1.2% follower purchase conversion at a $48.50 average order to Spring and Fourthwall benchmarks it does not link (VoxBooster). Applied to 5,000 engaged followers that is 20–60 orders per drop, and the one athlete-apparel case with a hard number (Alpha/AC1, ~125K Instagram followers) discloses only a 67% returning-customer rate, which points to retention of buyers rather than conversion of followers as the lever for a small gi brand (Startland News). The existence proof for creator-led gi sales is Jesse Enkamp's Seishin, built on a 1.6M-subscriber audience over roughly fifteen years (WKF); it is a proof that the path exists, not a template for a 1K channel.

Platform-native money is close to zero at this scale and in this country. YouTube's Partner Program needs 1,000 subscribers and 4,000 watch hours for ad revenue, rising to 8,000 hours from 1 February 2027 (AIR Media-Tech, citing YouTube Help; verify before relying on it), and once there the median RPM is $1.23 in Health & Sport against $10.22 in Education & Science across 300 managed channels (AIR Media-Tech), which means the ancient-history thread would earn roughly eight times the karate thread per view if it ever had views. Instagram Gifts was rolled out to 22 EU states but not Portugal as of the last Meta announcement found (November 2023) (Meta for Creators), and TikTok's Creator Rewards excludes Portugal. Brand deals are the realistic small-creator income: compiled rate cards put a nano (1–10K) Instagram post at $50–500 and a micro (10–100K) post at $500–5,000 (ContentGrip), and a Brazilian kata athlete with 85K Instagram followers lists $240 for a post and story (OpenSponsorship). The wider income distribution is the sobering frame: more than half of surveyed creators earn under $15,000 a year (Influencer Marketing Hub), only 28% of European creators with a brand deal work full-time, and 23% of those men earn under €500 a month (Kolsquare).

Karate is the thin niche; running and ADHD are the crowded ones

The adjacent categories differ enormously in crowding, and that should shape what the channel is "about" when a stranger first sees it. Running is dense: the English-language top tier runs from Total Running Productions at 807K through fifteen channels above 100K to a long tail at 13–70K (FeedSpot), and the verified mid-tier over-35 or comeback stories (Mark Lewis 399K, Hellah Sidibe 294K, Ben Felton 157K, Ben Parkes) all began as one-person training logs with a personal stake and took four to six years, with Lewis losing 1,000 subscribers in the 28 days to late October 2026, a hint that the mature channels are plateauing (vidIQ). ADHD is denser still, with How to ADHD at 1.9M and a dozen channels above 100K (FeedSpot); the one under-served angle is the late-diagnosed high achiever, validated by exactly one large case, Alex Partridge's ADHD Chatter, which reached around 100,000 monthly downloads within about a year (National Diversity Awards). Learning-in-public is dominated by Mike Boyd and Goal Guys but no verified creator does self-taught ancient history as a documented pursuit. Build-in-public with AI is saturated on X in generic entrepreneur spaces and is a text-platform category, not a video one (SoftwareSeni).

Karate is the opening. The WKF has about 818K YouTube subscribers and will stream all major competitions on YouTube from 2026 (FrancsJeux); Jesse Enkamp is at 1.62–1.67M and adding 20–30K a month while his Instagram is flat to declining (vidIQ; CreatorDB); and below those two the FeedSpot karate list drops to TEAM KI at 117K, Shotokan Karate at 52K and then a tail under 40K (FeedSpot). A channel at 10–20K subscribers would sit inside the top ten for karate on YouTube. No over-35 karate athlete documenting a competitive comeback was found anywhere, and the audience is adult, male and US/UK-heavy, which matches an English-first creator. The category is narrow, so it caps at perhaps one to two million addressable people rather than the tens of millions in running, but a thin niche with a clear leader and an empty mid-tier is where a bi-weekly channel can become visible fastest, and it is the niche the gi brand sells into.

Portugal subtracts more than it adds, and the subtraction is small. The country has 7.59M YouTube and 6.35M Instagram users, essentially saturated (DataReportal), but no fitness, running or karate YouTuber of scale; the top Portuguese running influencers live on Instagram at 4–40K followers and six of the top eleven already post in English, with the biggest Portuguese-language runner at 14.6K (Modash). A Porto-based "AI leader and multi-sport" profile already exists at 6.2K followers, which shows the persona is viable and small. Brazil is the real Portuguese-language pool (150M YouTube users, Portuguese a top-five YouTube language) but its fitness space is bodybuilding channels at 1.3–3.9M in Brazilian Portuguese (HypeAuditor); an English-first European creator would not capture it without a separate PT-BR track, which is a second production line. The right use of Portuguese is as texture (a line, a caption, an occasional PT-subtitled episode around a Portuguese event), not as a strategy. No data was found on Portuguese-language RPM or on AI Overview impact on Portuguese search, both of which are gaps.

Recommendation: YouTube bi-weekly plus a weekly email, Instagram as residue, no TikTok

For 5–7 hours a week, the mix the evidence supports is a single YouTube channel publishing one 10–20 minute episode every two weeks, with two or three Shorts cut from each episode (keeping Shorts at 25–40% of uploads), a short weekly email on a network with built-in discovery (Substack or beehiiv) that carries the episode in alternate weeks and a 300-word note in the others, and Instagram fed only from already-shot material at two or three posts a week with Stories for the people already there. TikTok gets nothing. LinkedIn gets one or two derived text posts a week only if the hours exist after the other three, with the newsletter link in the comments rather than the body. The budget breaks down as roughly 4–5 hours per fortnight on the episode (script, film, edit), 1 hour on Shorts, 30–45 minutes a week on the email, 30 minutes a week on Instagram and 15 minutes a day on comments; it is tight and any week it cannot be met, the email and comments survive and the episode slips, never the reverse. The first season is one journey (Athens 2026 into the half marathon 2027), told in a repeated format, with ADHD and mental health as the honest through-line rather than a separate topic, and ancient history and AI held back until the channel has a Regular-viewer share worth measuring (about 20 uploads in). Expect 150–300 views per episode in year one, a few hundred email subscribers, and 1,000 subscribers somewhere between month 18 and month 36; review the strategy at twelve months and not before, because the data says the channels that change course in month six are the ones that end up smallest.

Override this if any of the following hold. If after 20 episodes the per-video average percentage viewed on 10–20 minute videos sits below 25% and CTR below 3% at 1,000+ impressions, the problem is packaging or format, not the platform, and the fix is a shorter, tighter episode, not a platform move. If the weekly email consistently takes more than an hour, drop it to fortnightly rather than dropping it. If Shōmen becomes the priority rather than the audience, the mix inverts: Instagram becomes the home (it is where karate athletes and buyers are, where 39% of creators list a public email against 4.6% on TikTok (Click Analytic), and where brand collaborations are priced), YouTube becomes a bi-monthly flagship, and seeding product through 50–100K-follower karate athletes at a few hundred dollars a post replaces organic growth as the channel. And if the EU forces a redesign of TikTok's recommender or the karate audience visibly migrates to short-form, the Shorts share can rise toward the 40% ceiling without a second platform; the data never supports adding a third native format.

Conclusion

The research reframes the question. "Which platform" matters less than three things the data agrees on: a cadence you can hit on your worst week, a format strangers can recognise in one thumbnail, and refusing to pivot for at least a year. Every stalled creator in the notes broke one of those; every one who reached the mid-tier held all three for longer than felt reasonable. The hard constraint is not the algorithm but the budget, and at 5–7 hours the arithmetic forbids two production lines, so the real decision is the order in which the five journeys get told, not whether they all get told at once.

The less obvious implication is that the thinnest niche is the strategic one. Running and ADHD are where the audience is, but karate is where a 10K channel is visible, where the gi brand sells, where attention is measurably shifting to long-form YouTube, and where nobody is doing the comeback story. The other threads are what will make the channel a person rather than a category once it has an audience to carry them; the evidence on format drift says they are a year-two problem. What the research could not supply is any measured data on calendar time to milestones, on short-form-to-long-form conversion, on newsletter churn, or on Portuguese-language economics, so the numbers above are planning ranges built from correlational, mostly vendor-published samples, and the first twenty episodes are the only dataset that will actually settle them.

A9 · What to expect: benchmarks

Every number in this unit was checked against its source on 28 October 2026. Platform rules, thresholds and live channel counts carry that date unless another is given.

1 · What these numbers are, and what they cannot tell you

Every figure below is a base rate for other people's channels. Three properties of the sources decide how much weight each one can carry.

Most large samples are vendor data. The biggest YouTube datasets come from vidIQ, Metricool and AIR Media-Tech, which sell tools or services to creators and draw on their own indexes or client bases. vidIQ's own methodology for its uploads study says the sample "is not random" and is not a census of YouTube, and that its comparison groups were not matched on starting subscribers, channel age, resources or quality (vidIQ, uploads-to-1,000 study, 11 Sep 2026). Every cadence and growth relationship in this unit is correlational. Where a figure is vendor data it is labelled as such.

Several samples are survivors. vidIQ's first-ten-videos study excludes every channel that "gave up before publishing ten same-format videos in their first 90 days" (vidIQ, First 10 Videos, 28 Jul 2026). Its study of how many videos channels had when they reached 1,000 subscribers counts only channels that got there, so it has no denominator and cannot give a chance of success at any upload count (vidIQ, 9 Jun 2025). Survivor samples overstate what a typical new channel sees.

No large-sample study reports calendar time to a milestone. None of the datasets found gives months from launch to 1,000, 10,000 or 50,000 subscribers. Every "months to" figure in this unit is one of two things: upload counts converted to months at his cadence (26 uploads a year, the arithmetic shown), or anecdote from named or unnamed creators, labelled as anecdote.

One mechanism frames how to read all of it. YouTube ranks videos per viewer and per video, and subscribers are a weak channel for views. Todd Beaupré, YouTube's senior director of growth and discovery, said in a Creator Insider interview published 1 September 2026 that "probably all your videos have about maybe a 10% or less click-through rate" in the subscribers traffic source, and that "even your best videos, 90% of the time, your subscribed audience isn't deciding to watch"; he described the subscriptions feed as small next to the suggested list on the watch page (PPC Land, 2 Sep 2026). That is a secondary report of an interview, with no sample behind the 10%. In an earlier interview Beaupré said YouTube aims "to not overemphasize historical data if that data isn't particularly predictive of future video performance", and that a weak video does not irreversibly harm a channel (Search Engine Journal, 4 Mar 2024). The consequence for this unit: subscriber count is a lagging stock that summarises past reach, and the per-video numbers (views, click-through rate, percentage viewed) are the ones that move first. The checkpoints at the end read those first.

2 · Year one, per video

Views. vidIQ tracked 137,355 long-form channels created between January and June 2025 that posted at least ten long-form uploads in their first 90 days. The median 30-day view count was about 180 for the first upload, 211 for the fifth and 272 for the tenth; the median of each channel's best video among its first ten was 1,948. Of those channels, 57.8% had at least one of the first ten past 1,000 views and 30.8% had one past 10,000 (vidIQ, First 10 Videos, 28 Jul 2026). Two corrections apply before he uses these. The cohort posted ten videos in 90 days, about one and a half times his cadence: at 26 uploads a year his tenth video lands around week 18. And the cohort excludes channels that quit early, so the true medians are lower. Read the other way, 42.2% of channels that did keep posting (100 minus 57.8) had no video past 1,000 views in their first ten. That outcome is common among channels that continued, so on its own it says nothing about whether to stop.

Click-through rate. YouTube's own guidance is that "half of all channels and videos on YouTube have an impressions CTR that can range between 2% and 10%", and that new videos or channels, or videos with fewer than 100 views, "can see an even wider range" (YouTube Help, Impressions and click-through rate). The same page says YouTube recommends a video when it is relevant to the viewer and its average view duration suggests viewers find it interesting, and that a high CTR combined with low view duration and lower-than-expected impressions can indicate clickbait. YouTube publishes no "good" CTR target. Vendor CTR targets of 4–6% circulate widely (A5's brief carries one), but none found carries a dataset, so this unit uses none of them.

Average percentage viewed (APV). vidIQ publishes target bands it labels "vidIQ guidance", without a sample: 50–70% for videos under 5 minutes, 40–55% for 5–15 minutes, 30–45% for 15–30 minutes, 25–35% above 30 minutes (vidIQ, average view duration, reviewed 21 Jul 2026). For a 10–20 minute episode, the 5–15 and 15–30 bands bracket it. These are vendor heuristics and are labelled that way in the deliverable.

Regular viewers. YouTube replaced the returning-viewers metric in July 2025 with three segments, and said the new ones are "not a direct equivalent" of the old metric (Search Engine Journal, 2 Jul 2025). YouTube defines casual viewers as having "watched your channel at least once per month for one to 5 months in the past year", and regular viewers as having "watched a video on your channel at least once per month for more than 6 months in the past year" (YouTube Help, Understand new, casual and regular viewers); its tips page phrases the threshold as returning "for at least six months in the past year" (YouTube Help, New, casual and regular viewers tips). The segment counts months with a view, not visits. Two consequences follow. A channel cannot have Regular viewers until it has existed for six or seven months, so the metric is empty for the first two quarters by construction. And YouTube publishes no benchmark for the Regular share; the share benchmarks in circulation were written for the retired returning-viewers metric and do not transfer. The checkpoints record his Regular count as his own baseline and judge only its direction.

3 · From 0 to 1,000 subscribers

The best single dataset is vidIQ's study of 32,926,088 channels that had fewer than 1,000 subscribers and at least one video, compared with themselves 360 days later. Of those, 500,409 crossed 1,000, about 1.52% (the page gives the totals; the percentage is my division). The share depended on how many videos the channel added in the year (vidIQ, 11 Sep 2026):

Videos added in 360 days Channels Crossed 1,000
None (no net change in video count) 27,360,211 0.20%
1–11 3,423,276 1.86%
12–51 1,259,198 8.65%
52 or more 883,403 30.77%

That first row is 83.1% of the sample showing no net change in video count over the year. vidIQ warns that this "does not prove a channel published nothing", since uploads can be offset by removals or changes in visibility. Video counts include Shorts, long-form and livestreams, and the 52-or-more group does not prove a weekly schedule.

A bi-weekly channel adds 26 videos a year and sits in the 12–51 band: about one channel in twelve in that band crossed 1,000 within the year. A weekly channel sits in the 52-plus band at 30.77%. The groups were not matched, so the gap is an association: channels able to post weekly may also have more time, money or existing audience.

vidIQ's earlier study of 5,330,249 channels created within the previous three years that reached 1,000 subscribers (all vidIQ users) gives the upload count at the moment they crossed. 68% had 40 or more videos; 9.9% had fewer than ten; the largest single group, 30.89%, had 150 or more (vidIQ, 9 Jun 2025). Converted at 26 uploads a year (my arithmetic): video 40 arrives around month 18, video 70 around month 32, and video 150 around month 69. So the 37.1% of channels that crossed with 40–149 videos would, at his cadence, have taken between about one and a half and nearly six years, and the 30.89% that needed 150 or more would have taken longer than that.

The median growth rates show why time alone does not get a channel there. Among the channels that added videos in the 90 days to 20 July 2026 (11,752,034 in all; 3,332,098 in the three sub-1,000 bands), the median monthly gain was 0.5 subscribers in the 1–99 band, 1.4 in the 100–499 band and 3.7 in the 500–999 band (vidIQ, Subscriber Benchmarks 2026, 20 Jul 2026). A channel at the median of the smallest band gains about six subscribers a year. Channels that reach 1,000 are therefore not median channels growing steadily; they are channels where a few uploads outperform, which matches the first-ten study, where the best of the first ten had about ten times the views of the first.

Two anecdotes bracket the range and neither is evidence. A YouTube coach, Dexxter Clark, lists seven of his own and clients' channels, with 1,000 subscribers reached at 5, 7, 18, 18, 22 and 26 months where his table gives a value; the page is undated and its prose and table disagree on three channels (Social Video Plaza). At the fast end, an assistant professor posted 88 long-form videos in her first year from 28 July 2023, about 1.7 a week, and ended the year above 20,000 subscribers and 600,000 views with $300 or more in revenue; her first video drew 164 views (Sidika Tunc Candogan). That cadence is outside a 5–7 hour week.

For scale: 40.6% of the 61,186,246 channels with at least one public subscriber have 1,000 or more (vidIQ, Subscriber Benchmarks 2026). The milestone is common across all channels that have ever existed, and rare in any single year for any one channel.

4 · From 1,000 to 10,000

The stock figures come from the same snapshot: 40.6% of channels with a subscriber have at least 1,000 and 7.90% have at least 10,000, so about 19% of channels past 1,000 are also past 10,000 (vidIQ, Subscriber Benchmarks 2026; the ratio is my division). That is a cross-section of all channels at one moment and is not a conversion rate; it mixes channels of every age.

The median growth rate in the 1,000–9,999 band, across 6,192,753 channels that added videos in the 90 days to 20 July 2026, was 13 subscribers a month, or 0.51% (vidIQ, Subscriber Benchmarks 2026). Compounded at 0.51% a month, 1,000 becomes 10,000 in about 38 years (my arithmetic). The median rate is therefore not a route anyone takes; the channels that cross 10,000 are in the upper tail.

Cadence moves that tail. vidIQ's frequency study of 10,210,278 channels that already had 1,000 subscribers (April 2025 to March 2026, Shorts and long-form counted together) found median monthly growth of 0.89% in views and 0.10% in subscribers for channels posting 1–3 times a month, against 1.32% and 0.39% for channels posting 4–7 times (vidIQ, Upload Frequency Study, 8 Jul 2026). Moving from the bi-weekly tier to the weekly tier goes with about 1.5 times the view growth and 3.9 times the subscriber growth (my division). At the bi-weekly tier's median of 0.10% a month, a 1,000-subscriber channel gains about one subscriber a month. vidIQ states the relationship is correlational and that larger, healthier channels may simply have the resources to post more.

Metricool's 2026 YouTube report (799,718 videos from 71,177 accounts in its dataset, published 29 July 2026) gives the only tier-transition rates found: comparing 2025 with 2026 (the report does not define the window), 16.01% of accounts in its 2,000–10,000 band moved up a tier, the highest of any band, against 9.59% of accounts under 2,000, 7.01% at 10,000–100,000 and 2.11% at 100,000–1,000,000; 10.08% overall (Metricool, YouTube Trends 2026). This is vendor data from accounts using Metricool, not a random sample. The same report found that channels under 2,000 subscribers saw long-form views rise 267% year on year while average view duration fell more than 58%, which the report calls "thinner" views.

Durations exist only as anecdote. In the Social Video Plaza table, the four channels that reached 10,000 did so at 29, 31, 32 and 36 months, which is 9 to 14 months after each crossed 1,000 (my subtraction from the table) (Social Video Plaza). Those four are the survivors of a small, self-reported, undated sample. No sourced figure gives the typical months from 1,000 to 10,000, and the expectation sheet says so.

5 · From 10,000 to 50,000

No dataset isolates this band. What exists:

  • vidIQ's 10,000–99,999 band, 1,756,249 channels that added videos in the 90 days to 20 July 2026, had a median gain of 109 subscribers a month, or 0.48% (vidIQ, Subscriber Benchmarks 2026). Compounded, 10,000 to 50,000 at that rate takes about 28 years (my arithmetic): again the median describes no actual path.
  • In the same snapshot 1.30% of channels have 100,000 or more against 7.90% with 10,000 or more, so about 16% of channels past 10,000 are past 100,000 (my division). vidIQ publishes no 50,000 line.
  • Metricool's tier-up rate for 10,000–100,000 accounts is 7.01% over its 2025–2026 comparison period (Metricool).
  • In the Social Video Plaza anecdote, the same four channels went from 10,000 to 20,000 in 3, 5, 10 and 12 months (Social Video Plaza).

The documented creator cases are survivors, chosen because they succeeded, and none of the sources found dates their 10,000 or 50,000 milestones:

  • Hellah Sidibe started a run streak on 15 May 2017; his first video, "why I run every day", went up on day 163 and was an instant hit, and his HellahGood channel had 276,000 subscribers when CNN profiled him in September 2022 (CNN via LocalNews8, 22 Sep 2022). FeedSpot lists the channel at 294,000 on 12 October 2026 (FeedSpot, running channels). Read across the two sources, that is about 18,000 subscribers in the four years after the channel took roughly five years to reach 276,000.
  • Ben Felton began posting training videos during lockdown (the article gives no year), furloughed from work as a chef, as "simply something to do" (Gazette, undated); Ben Is Running had 157,000 subscribers on 12 October 2026 (FeedSpot).
  • Mark Lewis's channel was created on 11 September 2018 and had 399,000 subscribers and 514 videos on 27 October 2026, having lost 1,000 subscribers between 30 September and 27 October 2026 (vidIQ, channel stats).

Those cases show one-person training channels in an adjacent niche sitting at six figures six to nine years after they started. They do not show when any of them passed 10,000 or 50,000, so the months for this band stay unsourced.

What 10,000 to 50,000 means depends on the niche. On FeedSpot's karate list, ranked by a popularity score that weighs subscribers, views and uploads, positions eight to ten on 2 July 2026 had 20,500, 16,100 and 16,500 subscribers (FeedSpot, karate channels). In karate, the lower half of this band is already top-ten territory. Which niche leads each piece is A2's question.

6 · The owned list and the first money

Email list. beehiiv reports that newsletters launched on its platform in 2025 had a median of 482 subscribers in month one and 8,314 at the end of year one, across a platform base of 65,000-plus newsletters and 28 billion emails, with a 41.24% average open rate and a 3.23% click rate; median time to first dollar for 2025 launches was 66 days (beehiiv, The State of Newsletters 2026, 6 Jan 2026). This is platform-published data on a self-selected cohort using a platform that sells growth tools. Three documented cases, all anecdotes; the two written alongside other work land far lower:

  • The Creator's Playbook reached 100 subscribers about three months after a July 2024 launch and 2,000 at twelve months (a promotional line on the same page says 3,000; the main text says 2,000), with posting that swung from five times a week to none, and an explicit note that doing everything at once "burned me out" (The Creator's Playbook, 30 Jul 2025).
  • The Introverted Networker reached 2,500 over three years from a first issue in July 2022, written alongside "a full-time job, a family, and very limited hours" (The Introverted Networker, charts as of 28 May 2025).
  • Amy Suto reached 42,000 in three years, says "the first 1,000 subscribers are the slowest", and lists publishing more often than she could sustain as the cause of her only flat months (Amy Suto, 4 Jul 2026).

YouTube ad revenue. Today the Partner Program's ad tier needs 1,000 subscribers plus either 4,000 qualified watch hours in 12 months or 10 million qualified Shorts views in 90 days (YouTube Help, YPP overview, checked 28 Oct 2026). From 1 February 2027, new applicants need 1,000 subscribers plus "8,000 qualified watch hours in the last 365 days, or 20M qualified Shorts views in the last 90 days"; existing members keep their status; the fan-funding tier stays at 500 subscribers with 3,000 watch hours or 3 million Shorts views (YouTube Help, Changes to the YouTube Partner Program; announced 10 August 2026 per PPC Land, 12 Aug 2026). A channel starting in November 2026 will in practice enter under the 8,000-hour rule: YouTube says entry thresholds for new creators change on 1 February 2027, without saying whether qualifying or applying decides. From the same date a YPP channel counts as active only with 1,000 qualified watch hours in 365 days, 1 million qualified Shorts views in 90 days, or 2 long-form videos or 5 Shorts uploaded every 90 days; the bi-weekly plan clears the upload test.

What 8,000 hours means at his format (my arithmetic): a 12-minute episode at vidIQ's 40–55% APV band holds a viewer for 4.8 to 6.6 minutes, so 8,000 hours is about 72,700 to 100,000 long-form views in 365 days, or about 2,800 to 3,850 views per episode across 26 episodes. The first-ten medians in §2 are 180 to 272.

What the money is worth once there: AIR Media-Tech's study of 300 managed channels (3,595 monetised channel-months, May 2025 to May 2026) found a median RPM of $1.23 in Health & Sport (interquartile $0.72–1.53; small channels $0.76) and $10.22 in Education & Science (AIR Media-Tech, RPM by niche, 1 Jul 2026). That is vendor data from AIR's own managed channels. At the Health & Sport median, 100,000 views earn about $123 (my arithmetic).

7 · The share of followers who ever buy

No source found measures the share of a creator's followers who ever buy anything. Every available figure is per offer, per month, or a ratio of two totals at one moment, and most come from email lists or paid tiers, not from social followers. The brief's question can only be answered with proxies, and the proxies vary in quality.

Platform-official and documented cases (meet the standard):

  • Substack says: "We tend to see 5-10% of free subscribers convert to paying subscriptions, with 10% being a rate to aim for" (quoted in Really Good Business Ideas, 4 Sep 2026). This is the platform's own claim, without a dataset.
  • Artificial Ignorance, an AI newsletter, had 13,413 free and 121 paid subscribers on 1 January 2025, about 0.9%, which the author calls a "consistent ~1%" (Artificial Ignorance, 11 Feb 2025).
  • beehiiv cites Generalist World at 43,000 subscribers and 700-plus paying members, about 1.6% (my division) (beehiiv, 6 Jan 2026).
  • Patreon's company-sponsored State of Create survey (1,000 US creators and 2,000 US fans via NewtonX, fieldwork August 2024) reports average annual income of $52 per fan and $110 per paying member (Net Influencer, 21 Feb 2025).

Below the standard (reported here, kept out of the deliverable):

  • An aggregation of hundreds of publicly shared Substack reports over about five years puts the median paid conversion at 3%, with 20% of publications above 5% (Really Good Business Ideas, 4 Sep 2026). The dataset is paywalled and cannot be checked.
  • Teachery's course guidance says to expect "about 2-3% of your email audience to purchase your mini-course", and only if the audience is engaged and has heard from you regularly for months (Teachery). That is per offer, practitioner guidance, with no dataset.
  • Sacra's analyst page gives Patreon 60 million free members and about 400,000 free members converting to paid each month, without a percentage and without dating the 60 million (Sacra). Dividing the two gives about 0.67% a month; that ratio is mine and ties two figures the page does not tie to one period.
  • Merch: VoxBooster gives 0.40–1.20% of social followers buying physical merchandise and a $48.50 average order, attributed variously across its text and tables to Linktree, Spring, Shopify, Fanjoy and Fourthwall, with no study linked (VoxBooster, 22 Aug 2026). This is the figure most relevant to Shōmen and the weakest sourced in the unit. It does not enter the deliverable; A7 deals with Shōmen's numbers.

What the sound figures support: on an owned list, the documented cases sit around 1–2% paying at a given moment, and the platform's own claim is 5–10%. For social followers buying merchandise, there is no figure that meets the standard.

8 · The plateau, and why most quit there

Where the stall sits. The largest group in any dataset is the channel that has stopped adding videos: 83.1% of sub-1,000 channels showed no net change in video count over 360 days, and they crossed 1,000 at 0.20% (vidIQ, 11 Sep 2026). The first plateau is below 1,000, and its most common form is a channel with no new uploads.

Growth slows with size. Median monthly subscriber growth falls from 1.26% in the 1–99 band to 0.51% at 1,000–9,999, 0.48% at 10,000–99,999, 0.30% at 100,000–999,999 and 0.24% above a million (vidIQ, Subscriber Benchmarks 2026). Metricool's tier-up rate falls from 16.01% at 2,000–10,000 to 7.01% and then 2.11% (Metricool). Mature channels stall too: Mark Lewis lost 1,000 subscribers in the four weeks to 27 October 2026 (vidIQ), and HellahGood added about 18,000 in the four years after September 2022 (from the two sources in §5). In percentage terms every channel is on a flattening curve; in absolute terms the curve can still rise.

Why creators say they stop. No study found records the subscriber count or month at which creators quit, so "most quit at the plateau" is an inference from where the stall concentrates, not a measured fact. Surveys give reasons, with the usual weaknesses (self-report, sponsor interest):

  • Billion Dollar Boy, a creator agency, surveyed 1,000 US and UK creators (published 7 July 2025): 52% said they had experienced burnout as a direct result of their career and 37% were actively considering leaving; creative fatigue (40%), demanding workloads (31%) and constant screen time (27%) were the leading causes, and among those who had burned out, financial instability ranked as the most severe (55%) (Billion Dollar Boy, 7 Jul 2025). Agency survey.
  • Linktree surveyed almost 2,000 creators (November 2024): 59% said producing consistent content is a struggle, 55% named algorithm unpredictability as a barrier, and 70% earned under $49,000 in the past year (Passionfruit, Nov 2024). Vendor survey.
  • In Patreon's company-sponsored survey, 78% of creators said burnout affects their motivation and 75% said platforms punish creators who are not constantly publishing (Net Influencer, 21 Feb 2025). YouTube's growth team rejects that belief for its own platform: Search Engine Journal reports Beaupré dismissing the idea of a penalty box for breaks or falling views (§1 source), though AIR's correlational data below links repeated gaps to fewer views per video.

The peer-reviewed work describes the mechanism. Nguyen and Duffy, in New Media & Society (7 July 2026; 58 creator-authored pieces, 62 news accounts, 78 interviews), found burnout normalised and often "unspeakable", with the line "If I take a break, I'm going to get punished by the algorithm" a recurring theme (Cornell Chronicle, 8 Jul 2026). Pillemer, Harrison, Murphy and Park, in Administrative Science Quarterly (54 highly successful creators, 74 interviews), describe "dysfunctional entanglement": metrics become the main source of validation, underperformance causes distress, and many creators express a desire to quit; those who recovered did so by distancing from audience input (for example waiting a day before reading comments), depersonalising critique and anchoring in their own standards (PsyPost, 20 Feb 2026). That sample is creators with very large audiences, not small channels.

The money behind the decision. More than half of 3,000-plus creators surveyed by Influencer Marketing Hub earn under $15,000 a year (Influencer Marketing Hub, Aug 2025). Of 783 European creators with 5,000-plus followers and at least one brand deal (France, Germany, Italy, Spain, the UK and the Nordics, May–June 2025), 28% work full-time and 23% of the men earn under €500 a month (Kolsquare, 7 Jul 2025). Portugal is not in that sample.

Pivots. A radical change in format mix mid-stream is associated with worse outcomes. AIR Media-Tech's study of 18,000 English-language channels (March 2025 to March 2026) found that 1,247 channels which shifted their Shorts share by more than 30 points had lower median subscribers than stable strategies; in Entertainment at 100,000–1,000,000 monthly views, 51,600 against 72,400 and 73,600 (AIR Media-Tech, 28 May 2026). AIR states it cannot separate the pivot causing the decline from struggling channels being the ones that pivot. Going dark for 14 or more days three or more times a year is associated with fewer views per video, and a predictable schedule with more views per subscriber than the same volume on random days (AIR Media-Tech, 8 Jun 2026). Both are vendor, correlational, and drawn from channels far larger than his.

Put together, the documented sequence runs: per-video numbers far below expectation, subscriber growth that looks flat at the median, a belief (contradicted by YouTube) that a missed week is punished, metrics read as a verdict on the person, then either a cadence collapse or a pivot. The checkpoints below are built against each step: they judge the system by uploads before subscribers, compare per-video numbers with the base rates above, read Studio on fixed dates only, and allow format changes inside the channel before any change of platform.

Deliverable 2 · Quarterly checkpoints

How to run them. A quarter is 13 weeks, which at bi-weekly cadence is 6 or 7 uploads. Quarter one starts on the day of upload one. If upload one goes out in the week of 3 November 2026 (the start date in A3's brief), the reviews fall on the dates below; if not, shift every date by the same number of days. Each review is one sitting on that date. Studio and follower counts are read for the checkpoint on that day and not between; comments are answered in the daily comments slot without opening Studio. This extends the distancing strategy from the ASQ study (§8), whose example is not reading comments for 24 hours after posting, from comments to metrics and from a day to a quarter; that extension is mine, untested. Every review is written into the record, misses included.

The same seven reads every quarter.

  1. Uploads published this quarter against plan (7 in quarters 1, 3, 5, 7; 6 in quarters 2, 4, 6, 8).
  2. Hours logged per week against the 5–7 hour budget, and how many weeks went over.
  3. Median 30-day views across uploads at least 30 days old; the single best upload so far.
  4. Impressions CTR for each upload at least 7 days old.
  5. APV for each upload, against vidIQ's band for its length.
  6. Subscribers: total and net gain this quarter; Regular viewers (from quarter 3).
  7. Watch hours in the last 365 days against 8,000; email list size; paid members or orders per offer, if any offer ran.

The four verdicts. Each one names the evidence it rests on. The thresholds are his own decision rules, set from the base rates; no source sets them.

  • Continue: 6 or more uploads in the quarter and hours inside budget, whatever the subscriber count. Basis: rows 5–7 of the sheet; subscriber count lags per-video performance (§1).
  • Change format (packaging, structure or length, same channel, same cadence): 6 or more uploads, and CTR below 2% on three or more of the quarter's uploads, or APV below its vidIQ band on three or more. Basis: YouTube recommends on relevance plus view duration and judges each video on its own (YouTube Help 7628154; Search Engine Journal, Mar 2024); a shift of more than 30 points in Shorts share is associated with lower median subscribers (AIR, May 2026, correlational, cause unresolved), so a format change leaves the Shorts share where it was.
  • Cut the load: hours over 7 in three or more weeks of the quarter. The episode gets shorter before the cadence slips. Basis: going dark 14+ days three or more times a year is associated with fewer views per video (AIR, Jun 2026, vendor); Amy Suto's only flat months came from publishing more than she could sustain (anecdote).
  • Stop or rebuild the system: fewer than 3 uploads in two consecutive quarters. Three a quarter annualises to 12, the floor of the 12–51 band; below it the base rate is the 1–11 band, 1.86% a year. Basis: row 5. A stop is recorded with its reason and the numbers at the time.

No quarter before quarter 8 uses subscriber count as grounds to stop. Basis: row 6; at his cadence the 40-upload line that 68% of 1,000-subscriber channels had passed arrives around month 18.

3 · What converts them

Three conversions matter, and they rest on evidence of very different quality.

Stranger to viewer. On YouTube this is per-video recommendation: impressions, click-through and view duration, judged video by video. A6 explains the mechanics; A9 §1 and §2 give the base rates. Nothing about the audience profile changes it. The audience converts on a title and a thumbnail that promise their pursuit, delivered by a video that holds them.

Viewer to owned list. No source found measures the share of YouTube viewers or Instagram followers who join a creator's email list. That rate is unsourced, and the allocation plans around no number for it. What is documented is that newsletter platforms supply subscribers from inside their own networks. Substack said recommendations and its app drove 50% of all new subscriptions and 25% of new paid subscriptions (On Substack, 22 Feb 2024); in October 2024 it said the app drove 3 million subscriptions in the past month and recommendations 2 million, and that app discovery was generating "about the same number of subscriptions as all social media sources combined" (On Substack, 31 Oct 2024). beehiiv says newsletters using its Recommendations "grow 2.75x faster, and those that recommend others are 32x more likely to be recommended back" (beehiiv, 5 Jan 2026). Both are platform claims without published method. The practical consequence: for a list that starts at zero, the platform's network is the only measured source of subscribers, and a call to action inside an episode is an unmeasured second one.

Follower or subscriber to buyer. The rates (how many of a list pay, how many followers ever buy) are A9 §7, and A7 applies them to Shōmen. What this unit adds is what this audience is documented to pay for.

  • They buy on creator recommendation, at least occasionally. PYMNTS surveyed 3,196 US consumers, weighted to the US adult population (fieldwork 10–15 January; the page gives no year; published March 2025): 72% of millennials and 58% of Gen X made at least one influencer-recommended purchase in a year, and 12% of all consumers did so more than six times (PYMNTS Intelligence, Mar 2025). An older Pew survey (6,034 US adults, July 2022) found 33% of social media users aged 30 to 49 had bought something after seeing an influencer post about it (Pew Research Center, 21 Nov 2022). Both are self-report.
  • They spend on the gear their pursuit needs. Running USA's 2024 Global Runner Survey (more than 7,400 runners, 94% American, June–July 2024, sponsored by Active) found 24.9% spend more than $400 a year on running shoes and 24.7% spend $301–400, so about half spend more than $300 (my sum); on apparel 29% spend $100–200 and 21% $201–300 (SGB Media, 21 Oct 2024). This is a reporter's summary of a sponsored survey. No equivalent exists for karate equipment.
  • They say they would pay for long-form. Patreon's fans were more likely to pay for long-form content (49%) than short-form (29%) (Patreon, State of Create). Company-sponsored, US-only, a stated preference.
  • Structured, educational products are where creators report growth. Kajabi's survey of 1,717 creators (December 2024 to January 2025) reported digital-download sales up 20% and educational content up 14%, against brand deals down 52%, affiliate income down 36% and platform payouts down 33% (Net Influencer, 17 Apr 2025, reporting Kajabi's 2025 State of Creator Commerce; the three declines also in Silicon UK, 17 Apr 2025). These are creators reporting their own revenue changes on a platform that sells course tools; it says nothing about who the buyers were.
  • Affiliate commission on gear is small per sale. Amazon.es, the Amazon store that serves Portugal, pays 4.0% in Deportes y Fitness, 6.0% on clothing (Ropa y Accesorios) and on shoes (Zapatos, Bolsos y Carteras) and 5.0% on books (Amazon.es Associates fee table; no effective date shown). A €120 pair of running shoes earns €7.20 at 6% (my arithmetic). Whether a Portuguese resident can join the Amazon.es programme was not confirmed.

Why an audience trusts one creator enough to buy has peer-reviewed evidence from a different population: in a 2026 cross-sectional survey of 407 Indonesian women who follow beauty influencers, perceived expertise and trustworthiness predicted the parasocial bond that predicted purchase intention, while attractiveness did not predict the bond but was directly related to purchase intention (Hidayat, Rahmat and Adilla, Frontiers in Communication, 2026). A4 treats it with the disclosure policy.

Put together, the sourced picture is that runners spend on the equipment running consumes (one sponsored, mostly US survey) and that this audience, on weaker evidence, would pay for long-form or structured guidance from someone it trusts. Every source behind that sentence is American or global, and none is Portuguese. The share of his own audience that will pay for anything is unknown until an offer runs and is measured per A9's checkpoints.

4 · Why YouTube long-form compounds

The mechanism is stated by YouTube. Its help centre says "It's common for viewers to start showing more interest in old videos" and that "Many viewers don't watch videos in chronological order or decide what to watch based on when a video was published"; its recommendation system "aims to deliver the right videos to the right viewers, regardless of when that video was uploaded" (YouTube Help, performance FAQ, answer 141805). A separate page says "publish time is not known to impact a video's long-term performance" (YouTube Help, YouTube's recommendation system, answer 16533387). PPC Land's report of Todd Beaupré's September 2026 interview adds, in its own paraphrase, that some videos "accumulate the bulk of their audience a month or more after publication" (PPC Land, 2 Sep 2026). A video is a standing entry in the recommendation pool for as long as it satisfies viewers. A catalogue of 26 episodes a year is 26 entries that keep drawing impressions.

The vendor data shows the same shape. In vidIQ's 2025 cohorts, median 30-day views on long-form rose from about 180 on the first upload to about 272 on the tenth across 137,355 channels, while the Shorts cohort of 367,243 channels went from about 1,362 to about 1,344, which vidIQ describes as "no incremental growth, in fact a slight decline over time" (vidIQ, First 10 Videos, 28 Jul 2026). The sample excludes channels that did not publish ten same-format videos within their first 90 days, so it is a cohort posting about weekly or faster (he reaches ten uploads in about 18 weeks; my arithmetic), and it is observational. A practitioner model goes further: Humble&Brag estimates that by month six, with 24 to 26 videos, back-catalogue views start to exceed the views on new uploads, but its own assumptions are one video a week and about 450 first-week views each (Humble&Brag, 9 Mar 2026). That is a model, without a measured dataset, and at his cadence video 24 arrives around month 11 and video 26 at month 12 (my arithmetic).

For Instagram and TikTok no source found measures how a post's views accumulate over months. Socialinsider and Buffer report per-post and per-week figures. Whether a Reel or a TikTok compounds is therefore unsourced, and the allocation treats both as producing value in the week they are posted.

The money favours long-form per view. YouTube pays creators 55% of net revenue from Watch Page ads and 45% of the revenue allocated to them from Shorts Feed ads (YouTube Help, YouTube partner earnings overview, answer 72902). Alphabet's chief executive said on the Q3 2025 earnings call (29 October 2025) that "In the U.S., Shorts now earn more revenue per watch hour than traditional in-stream on YouTube" (PPC Land, 6 Nov 2025, reporting the call). That is YouTube's revenue per hour watched. A Short is watched for seconds and an episode for minutes, so even at equal revenue per hour, revenue per view stays far higher on long-form (my inference; no source found gives creator revenue per view for the two formats side by side). Long-form watch hours are also what count toward the Partner Program's watch-hour route; the thresholds, the February 2027 change and the RPM figures for his niches are in A9 §6.

Cadence: bi-weekly sits at the low edge of the best-performing band. AIR Media-Tech's 12-month snapshot of 18,000 English-language channels in 11 niches (US, UK, Canada, Australia) found that in Health & Fitness the median channel posting 2–5 videos a month had 172,000 subscribers (82 channels) against 77,700 for channels posting 10–20 (63 channels); in Education the 2–5 band also peaked, at 116,000. Channels on a predictable schedule got more views per subscriber than channels posting as many videos on random days (AIR Media-Tech, 8 Jun 2026). AIR calls this correlational, and its channels are far larger than his. Bi-weekly is about 2.2 long-form videos a month (my arithmetic), inside the band at its bottom edge.

Shorts as the second format: the two datasets disagree at his volume. AIR's Shorts study (18,000 channels, March 2025 to March 2026, at least 40 videos) found channels mixing Shorts had more median subscribers than long-form-only channels in Fitness (41% more), Business (39%) and Education (37% at smaller sizes), put "the optimal Shorts ratio" at 25% to 40% of total uploads, and found Shorts "crowding out" long-form above 55% (AIR Media-Tech, 28 May 2026). Metricool's 799,718 videos from 71,177 accounts found channels posting around five Shorts a month averaged 8,789 views per long-form video, about 46% above the global average of 5,985, and that at 35 or more Shorts a month performance fell back to roughly the level of channels posting none (Metricool, YouTube Trends 2026, 29 Jul 2026). Both are vendor and correlational. At 2.2 episodes a month, AIR's 25–40% share means 0.7 to 1.4 Shorts a month, while Metricool's peak sits near five (my arithmetic). One Short per episode gives two a month and a 50% share, under AIR's 55% line and between the two findings. That is the allocation's choice, and neither dataset tested it. It also means the "2–3 Shorts per episode" in A3's brief would put Shorts at 67–75% of uploads, above AIR's crowding line (my arithmetic); A3 should take the one-Short figure from here.

6 · What the hours buy

The budget is 5 to 7 hours a week, 10 to 14 a fortnight. Most components have no measured time cost; where one exists it is cited, and the rest are the allocation's own estimates, which the hours log in A9's checkpoints (read 2) will correct.

  • Long-form editing runs "30–60 minutes per finished minute" of video by a vendor rule of thumb with no stated method (Tasty Edits, updated 17 Sep 2026). A 10-minute episode is 5 to 10 hours of editing; a 12-minute episode is 6 to 12. At the top of that range the edit alone fills a fortnight. The allocation therefore sets the episode at about 10 minutes and plans on the low end, 5 hours. A5's brief aims lower (a 12-minute episode in under 4 hours); the plan keeps 5 until that workflow is in use.
  • A short weekly email can be written in 45 minutes with a four-step formula, by Justin Welsh's own account, picking topics from his best-performing social posts (Justin Welsh, 6 Apr 2024). That is an anecdote from a full-time creator; here the episode supplies the topic.
  • Instagram frequency matters less than consistency. Buffer's fixed-effects analysis of 2.1 million posts from 102,000 accounts found weekly follower growth of 0.12% at 1–2 posts a week and 0.26% at 3–5, each relative to weeks with no posts (Buffer, 13 Aug 2025). Vendor data, feed posts only. Two posts a week cut from the episode's footage sits in the lower band at the lowest cost.
  • Replies to comments are on YouTube's own list of ways to grow Regular viewers, alongside publishing regularly and making content "more consistent and similar in topic or format" (YouTube Help, answer 13615784). The allocation gives them fixed slots, which is also the distancing practice A9 §8 takes from the ASQ study.
  • The cadence has to survive the worst week. Amy Suto's rule is to pick a cadence you can hit "on your worst week, not your best one" (Amy Suto, 4 Jul 2026); that is an anecdote. AIR's association between going dark for 14 or more days three or more times a year and fewer views per video is in A9 §8. Both point to the same cut order: shrink the derivatives and the episode, hold the fortnightly date.

1 · YouTube

1.1 Browse, suggested and search are three different machines

YouTube Analytics sorts every view by where it started. The definitions matter, because the three large sources work differently (YouTube Help, Traffic sources):

  • Browse features: traffic from Home, Subscriptions, Watch Later, trending/Explore and other browsing features.
  • Suggested videos: traffic from suggestions next to or after other videos, and from links in video descriptions.
  • YouTube search: traffic from search results.
  • Smaller sources: channel pages, playlists, notifications and emails to subscribers, external sites and apps that embed or link the video, the Shorts feed, and direct or unknown.

Browse and suggested are a pull, ranked per viewer. Beaupré, YouTube's senior director of growth and discovery, said in 2024 that creators picture YouTube pushing videos out to people when the process runs the other way, with viewers pulling, and that discovery "is focused more on individual videos" than on channels (Search Engine Journal, 4 Mar 2024). In a Creator Insider interview published 1 September 2026 he said every viewer gets a ranking of their own, and that the main place subscribers watch a channel they follow is the suggested list on the watch page, with the subscriptions feed small by comparison (PPC Land, 2 Sep 2026). In the same interview he put subscriber click-through in the subscribers row at "maybe a 10% or less", a hedged figure with no sample behind it. A subscriber is therefore one input to a per-viewer prediction; the subscription does not deliver the video.

Search is a query match. YouTube's own page on search names three elements, whose importance "may vary by search type" (YouTube Help, How YouTube search works):

  • relevance: how well the title, tags, description and video content match the query;
  • engagement: signals such as the watch time of a particular video for a particular query;
  • quality: signals of which channels show expertise, authoritativeness and trustworthiness on a topic.

The page says results may also use the viewer's search and watch history, and that YouTube does not accept payment for organic placement. It says nothing about channel size or recency.

How big each source is for a channel like his is unmeasured. The only large split found is Metricool's 2026 study of 799,718 videos from 71,177 accounts (published 29 July 2026): the Shorts feed carried more than 61% of organic views, subscribers 11%, YouTube search 9%, related videos 5.77%, playlists 5.05% and channel visits about 3% (Metricool, YouTube Trends 2026). That is vendor data from Metricool's users, dominated by Shorts volume, and it is not a long-form or small-channel split. No source found gives the traffic mix of a small long-form channel, so his own Studio split is the only figure he can use, and it is a baseline to watch, with nothing to compare it against.

What the mechanics imply for a journey channel (my reading, from the definitions above): an episode can be built to be found by a query (the title and description carry the words a stranger would type, and the video holds that searcher), or built to be picked by the per-viewer prediction on Home and the watch page (packaging and retention). The two are scored on different evidence, so the traffic-source report is where he learns which kind each episode turned out to be.

1.2 Click-through rate and retention: the two numbers YouTube says it uses

YouTube's help page on impressions states the rule both ways: it recommends a video when the video is relevant to the viewer and its average view duration shows viewers find it interesting; a video with "high CTR but low average view duration and lower than expected Impressions" carries the clickbait signature (YouTube Help, Impressions and click-through rate). The same page sets out how to read CTR:

  • Impressions CTR is how often viewers watched after seeing a registered impression on YouTube. Impressions on external websites and end screens are not counted, and some views do not start from a thumbnail impression at all.
  • Half of all channels and videos sit between 2% and 10%. Videos or channels less than a week old, or with fewer than 100 views, can range more widely still.
  • Videos with few impressions tend to have higher CTR because they reach a narrower, more loyal audience; a video on the homepage will naturally have lower CTR.
  • Compare CTRs between videos over the long term, bear traffic sources in mind, and avoid checking CTR immediately after upload; small variations are not cause for immediate action.

YouTube publishes no "good" CTR. The "4–6% is good" target that circulates in vendor posts has no dataset behind it and is not used in this course (Audit 1 erratum). The 2–10% range is context only (A9 row 2).

YouTube's own testing tool chooses on watch time. Test & Compare lets a channel upload up to three titles, thumbnails or title-thumbnail pairs; the test is optimised "for overall watch time over other metrics, like click-through-rate", takes a few days to two weeks, runs from YouTube Studio on a computer only, and needs advanced features enabled; if the result is inconclusive the first variant uploaded stays (YouTube Help, A/B test titles & thumbnails). That is the platform stating, in a product, which of the two numbers it values when they conflict.

Retention is read against his own videos. Studio's typical-retention line compares a video with the channel's 10 latest videos of similar length, or with all YouTube videos of similar length; Intro is the share of the audience still watching after the first 30 seconds; spikes mark rewatched or shared moments, dips mark abandoning or skipping; these highlights need a video of at least 60 seconds with at least 100 views (YouTube Help, Measure key moments for audience retention). The only external bands for average percentage viewed found for this unit are vidIQ's, which it labels "vidIQ guidance" and publishes without a sample (A9 row 3; vidIQ, updated 21 Jul 2026).

Regular viewers are a loyalty count, and YouTube says they do not drive reach. Regular viewers watched a video on the channel at least once a month for more than six months of the past year; casual viewers did so for one to five months; and YouTube states that these segments have no effect on reach or monetisation (YouTube Help, Understand new, casual and regular viewers). YouTube's tips for growing the casual and regular segments are content "more consistent and similar in topic or format", a series built from topics that already worked, regular publishing and responding to comments, with no effect sizes given (YouTube Help, New, casual and regular viewers tips). The count is empty for the first six months by construction and has no benchmark (A9 row 4).

1.3 Why long-form compounds, and how far the evidence goes

"Compounds" here means a published long-form episode keeps earning views and watch hours after its launch week. Four platform-side facts support the mechanism:

  1. An old video stays eligible. Beaupré said YouTube tries not to over-weight a channel's history where it does not predict how the next video will do, and that a weak video followed by a strong one should not hold the strong one back; the article also reports him saying older videos can gain traction when audience interest renews (Search Engine Journal, 4 Mar 2024). PPC Land's report of the 2026 interview says some videos gather most of their audience a month or more after publication; it gives that without a direct quote (PPC Land, 2 Sep 2026).
  2. Search matches a query, whenever it is typed. Relevance and per-query engagement (§1.1) are properties of the video and the query; nothing on YouTube's search page ties them to upload date (YouTube Help 16090438). That an evergreen query keeps sending viewers is my inference from the page, not a measured figure.
  3. Only public long-form builds watch hours. The Partner Program counts "qualified watch hours gained from long-form videos you've set public"; Shorts-feed watch time, private, unlisted and deleted videos do not count (YouTube Help, YPP overview). From 1 February 2027 the ad-revenue threshold for new applicants is 8,000 qualified watch hours in the last 365 days (YouTube Help, Changes to the YouTube Partner Program; A9 row 11). Every public episode adds to that rolling total for a year; unlisting or deleting one removes it.
  4. The screen favours length. YouTube said in February 2024 that viewers watch more than 1 billion hours of YouTube on TVs every day (YouTube Official Blog, 20 Feb 2024). Beaupré in 2026 attributed the rise of longer videos to people watching on television, with recommendations following that demand (PPC Land, 2 Sep 2026). In Metricool's 2026 sample, long-form average view duration was 2.51 minutes against about 16 seconds for Shorts (Metricool).

Against that, the vendor data shows the launch window still dominates: in Metricool's sample videos usually peak in the first three days and about 83% of interactions arrive within ten days (Metricool, 29 Jul 2026). That sample is Shorts-heavy and counts interactions; it is still the only timing figure found. No dataset measures what share of a small long-form channel's views come from its back catalogue. So the mechanism is sourced and its size is not, and the metrics sheet below measures it on his own channel (row Y7).

Breaks. YouTube's position is that the system does not punish a channel for a break or a weak video (SEJ, above). AIR Media-Tech's study of 18,000 English-language channels across 11 niches and four size segments, with its primary analysis on the 100,000–1,000,000 monthly-views segment (published 8 June 2026) found that channels going dark for 14 or more days three or more times a year got fewer views per video, and that channels on a predictable schedule got more views per subscriber than channels posting the same volume on random days; it gives no effect sizes and states the data is correlational (AIR Media-Tech, 8 Jun 2026). The two do not contradict each other: YouTube describes the mechanism, AIR describes what happened to channels that missed weeks, which may reflect the reasons they missed them. AIR's channels are far larger than his. The cadence figures by upload band (weekly against bi-weekly: about 1.5 times the view growth and 3.9 times the subscriber growth, on channels already past 1,000) are in A9 row 9.

2 · Instagram

2.1 Reels and Explore show strangers; Feed and Stories serve followers

Instagram's own explanation (updated 31 May 2023) says each part of the app runs its own ranking system (Instagram, Instagram Ranking Explained):

Surface What it shows (Instagram, 2023) What it predicts
Feed posts from accounts you follow, recommended accounts and ads whether you spend a few seconds, comment, like, share, tap the profile photo
Stories people the viewer has chosen to follow, plus ads whether you tap into, reply to or skip a story
Explore mostly people the viewer does not follow likes, saves, shares
Reels "the majority of what you see is from accounts you don't follow" whether you reshare, watch to the end, like, open the audio page

That table is the brief's claim in Instagram's own terms: Reels (and Explore) are where a non-follower meets the account; Stories reach followers only; Feed is mostly followers with recommendations mixed in.

Mosseri updated the signals in January 2025: watch time, likes and sends are the three that matter most; likes weigh slightly more for connected content and "sends are slightly more important for unconnected content"; and in Insights creators should watch average watch time, likes per reach and sends per reach (Social Media Today, 22 Jan 2025). "Unconnected" is not defined in the article; it is read here as content shown to people who do not follow the account.

Two follower-side and stranger-side mechanics are sourced to Instagram or to reports of what its head said:

  • Carousels get a second pass at followers. In Lia Haberman's summary of what Mosseri said in 2024, the algorithm gives a carousel "a second chance after it's posted, resurfacing it to followers who previously swiped by"; the words are Haberman's, not a transcript of Mosseri ( Lia Haberman, ICYMI, 14 Oct 2025).
  • Trial Reels test on strangers. A trial Reel is "shown to non-followers first"; followers do not see it in Feed or the Reels tab (it can still reach them through a DM share); engagement metrics appear after about 24 hours; the creator can share it to everyone at any time, or set it to share automatically if it performs well on views in the first 72 hours (Meta, Trial reels, 10 Dec 2024, updated 26 Jun 2025; Instagram Creators, 10 Dec 2024). Both pages refer eligibility to the Help Center without stating it. The 1,000-follower requirement in the earlier research has no source on either page (Audit 1 erratum); it does have one elsewhere: Mosseri said in July 2025 that trial reels are available to creators with at least 1,000 followers and a public account (Social Media Today, 13 Jul 2025, executive statement reported second-hand; no later change found).

Which format reaches further at small sizes is disputed. Metricool and HypeAuditor's Instagram Content Playbook (700 million posts from 28 million influencer accounts, January–June 2025, medians) reports that Reels have the highest reach in every follower tier (Metricool press release, 14 Oct 2025). Socialinsider's brand dataset says the opposite for views per post: at 1,000–5,000 followers in 2025, Reels averaged 580 views, carousels 993 and images 417 (Socialinsider, Instagram benchmarks). The populations differ (influencer accounts against business accounts), as do the measures (median reach against average views). For a personal account, the platform's own description (Reels mostly reach non-followers) and the influencer dataset agree; the brand dataset is the dissent. His own Insights split of views by followers and non-followers settles it for his account (row I1).

2.2 Reach is rented

"Rented" means the platform decides, and can change, who sees the work, how it is counted, and whether a viewer can leave. Each of those has happened on Instagram and is documented.

Recommendation can be withdrawn without touching followers. Instagram's original-content guidelines: an account that mostly posts unoriginal content "may not be seen in recommendations to new audiences"; it regains eligibility when most of its recent Reels, photos and carousels are original over a 30-day period; content shown to existing followers is not affected. Unoriginal includes reposts of others' work, screenshots even with credit, and minor edits such as borders, watermarks, speed changes or a credit line (Instagram Creators, Original content guidelines). On 30 April 2026 Instagram extended the rule from Reels (where it had applied since 2024) to photos and carousels, said aggregators "will no longer be eligible for recommendations across Instagram", pointed creators to Account Status to check and appeal, and said 75% of recommendations in the US now come from original posts (Instagram Creators, Rewarding original creators, 30 Apr 2026). The 2023 ranking page already listed low-resolution or watermarked Reels, and Reels already posted on Instagram, among the reasons for less distribution (Instagram Ranking Explained). For him: posting mostly reposts of training partners' videos can take the account out of recommendations to new audiences; a clip of his own that carries another app's watermark stays eligible but, on Instagram's 2023 account, gets less distribution.

The exits are narrow. Instagram does not allow links in ordinary captions (Social Media Today, Nov 2024), though since March 2026 Meta has been testing clickable caption links for Meta Verified subscribers (Engadget, 12 Mar 2026); Story link stickers are, in Social Media Today's reading, not reach-restricted "though they are only distributed to your followers" (Social Media Today, 25 Nov 2024). For a stranger who finds a Reel on an account without Meta Verified, the bio link is the remaining exit (his reading of the two sources; no source states it).

The scoreboard is redefined by the landlord. In August 2024 Instagram made Views the primary metric across Reels, posts and Stories, counted repeat views, moved impressions out of Insights, and added a Followers and Non-followers split of views (Social Media Today, Aug 2024; Metricool, 14 Aug 2024). In January 2025 it added a Reels view rate (share continuing past three seconds) and views over time against the creator's usual (TechCrunch, 29 Jan 2025); in August 2025 it replaced view rate with skip rate, the share of views from people who "decided to skip your reel during those first 3 seconds", and added a retention chart (Social Media Today, 24 Aug 2025). A metric he tracks today may not exist under the same name in a year; the metrics sheet records the definition next to each number for that reason.

Little attention goes to followed creators. In Patreon's company-sponsored State of Create survey (1,000 US creators and 2,000 US fans, via NewtonX, August 2024), only 24% of users' Instagram time went to creators they follow, against 32% on YouTube and 38% on TikTok, and 53% of creators said followers were harder to reach than five years earlier (Net Influencer, 21 Feb 2025). That is self-report from a company selling the alternative.

2.3 How much reach a small account rents

The best small-account figures are brand data. Socialinsider's sample (published 20 February 2026, updated 22 October 2026; its "2026" columns are 2025 values relabelled; 131,955 business accounts and 7,032,675 posts under 5,000 followers) gives the 1,000–5,000 tier 580 views per Reel, 993 per carousel and 417 per image, and annual audience growth falling from 38.0% in 2024 to 22.0% in 2025; the 5,000–10,000 tier fell from 35.0% to 20.3% (Socialinsider). Personal accounts may do better on interaction: in the Metricool and HypeAuditor data, influencer posts get "approximately 3 times more likes and comments than brand accounts" (Metricool, 14 Oct 2025). That multiple is for likes and comments; no source gives one for views or growth, so the brand figures are the only sourced floor.

Frequency is the one lever with within-account evidence. Buffer's study of about 2.1 million feed posts from 102,000-plus accounts (published 13 August 2025) used a fixed-effects model comparing each account's weeks with itself: against weeks with no posts, weekly follower growth rose 0.12% in weeks with 1–2 posts, 0.26% with 3–5, 0.44% with 6–9 and 0.66% with 10 or more; reach per post was about 12% higher at 3–5 posts a week than at 1–2. Stories were excluded, and results were not split by account size (Buffer, How often to post on Instagram). Vendor data on Buffer's users. How many of his 5–7 hours go to Instagram is A2's allocation.

3 · Substack and his own site

3.1 What "owned" means, exactly

On Substack the list belongs to the writer, who will "always own your readers’ email addresses" and can export them at any time, including subscribers who joined through the app (Substack Support). One part is not portable. As of Substack's FAQ of 18 August 2025, readers in the iOS app can pay through Apple's in-app purchase; the writer still gets their email address, but "Apple does not transfer billing relationships between platforms", so a paid subscriber who bought in the iOS app has to re-subscribe if the publication moves; Apple takes 30% in the first year and 15% after, and Substack's 10% is charged on the web price (Substack, In-app payments FAQ, 18 Aug 2025). Owned, on Substack, means the addresses; the payment relationship can sit with Apple.

3.2 Slow, and where new readers come from

How slowly lists grow is in A9 §6 and row 13: beehiiv's self-selected 2025 cohort at a median of 482 subscribers in month one and 8,314 at twelve months, against documented part-time cases at about 2,000 after a year and 2,500 after three.

Discovery on a newsletter platform is itself run by the platform. Substack said in February 2024 that its recommendations and app together drove "50% of all new subscriptions" and a quarter of new paid subscriptions across the network (On Substack, 22 Feb 2024); no newer official split was found, and the figure is network-wide; no per-writer split is given. beehiiv reports that newsletters using its Recommendations grew 2.75 times faster on average and that newsletters recommending others were 32 times more likely to be recommended back (beehiiv, State of Newsletters 2026, 6 Jan 2026); platform-published, on beehiiv's own users.

One documented case shows the gap between network attention and subscribers. Amy Suto spent six months going all-in on Substack Notes: Notes brought 1,459 free subscribers against 8,366 from onboarding and recommendations in the same window, and a Note with 15,800 likes and 28,055 clicks produced 378 free subscribers and no paid ones (Amy Suto, 14 Jul 2026). Anecdote, one writer, who reports 42,000-plus subscribers over three years.

So the owned channel has a rented front door: the list is his, and a large share of new arrivals on Substack come through Substack's own recommendation system.

3.3 What the inbox numbers mean

Email reaches the people on the list in a way no feed does, and the open rate overstates by how much. beehiiv's average across 65,000-plus newsletters and 28 billion emails in 2025 was a 41.24% open rate and a 3.23% click rate; the same report says Apple's Mail Privacy Protection "has made the traditional open rate less reliable, as it auto-loads images" (beehiiv, 6 Jan 2026). Apple's own description: the feature "downloads remote content in the background by default" and stops senders learning when or how often a message was opened (Apple, Mail Privacy Protection). GetResponse's 2024 benchmarks (4.4 billion messages from senders with at least 500 contacts, calendar 2023) give an average 39.64% open rate, 3.25% click-through, 0.15% unsubscribe rate and spam complaints below 0.01%, and also attribute part of the rise in opens to Apple's auto-opens (GetResponse, Email Marketing Benchmarks 2024). Both are vendor averages across every sender on each platform; neither isolates creator newsletters. The consequence for reading: clicks and unsubscribes are counted events; opens are partly a machine loading images.

3.4 His own site

A personal site is the one place no platform ranks, and it is a weak discovery channel in 2026. Ahrefs compared 300,000 keywords in Google Search Console desktop data between December 2023 and December 2025: where an AI Overview appears, position-1 click-through fell 58% against its forecast, from 7.3% to 1.6% raw; informational keywords without an AI Overview fell from 7.6% to 3.9% (Ahrefs, 4 Feb 2026). Ahrefs sells SEO tools; it cites Seer Interactive, Authoritas and Kevin Indig finding declines of 47–65%. No data was found for Portuguese-language queries. The site's sourced uses are narrower: it holds the written record, and a YouTube video embedded there shows in Studio as External traffic (YouTube Help 1714921).

4 · What carries across, and where the evidence stops

On all three platforms, a new viewer arrives through a system the creator does not control: YouTube's per-viewer prediction and query match, Instagram's recommendation surfaces, Substack's recommendations and app. What differs is what the creator keeps afterwards. YouTube keeps the video eligible and its watch hours counting for a year; Instagram keeps followers, whose share of attention is small and who are served mainly on Feed and Stories, the surfaces built for them; email keeps the address, exportable, with an inbox that reports clicks reliably and opens unreliably.

Gaps that the deliverable cannot fill with a sourced number, and does not try to:

  • the traffic-source mix of a small long-form YouTube channel;
  • the share of views a small channel's back catalogue earns;
  • reach or views benchmarks for personal Instagram accounts under 10,000 followers (only brand data and a likes-and-comments multiple);
  • skip-rate, sends-per-reach or bio-link click benchmarks of any kind;
  • the share of a single Substack writer's subscribers that come from the network;
  • anything Portugal-specific on these mechanics.

Each of those rows in the metrics sheet records his own baseline and judges direction only.

Deliverable 1 · Per-platform playbook

Checked 28 October 2026. Each rule names the evidence it rests on. Rules marked (his rule) are decision conventions set from that evidence; no source sets them. Hours per platform are A2's allocation; cadence is the course goal's (one long-form episode every two weeks); verdicts (continue, change format, cut the load, stop) are A9's quarterly checkpoints.

YouTube · the home channel

What it rewards. Per-video relevance plus watch time, judged per viewer; a query match in search. Basis: YouTube Help 7628154, 16090438; Beaupré (SEJ 2024; PPC Land 2026).

  1. Decide each episode's entry door before scripting. Search-shaped: the title and description carry the words a stranger would type for the problem the episode solves. Browse-shaped: the title and thumbnail sell the story to a person who has never heard of him. Basis: search relevance is title, tags, description and content against the query (YouTube Help 16090438); browse and suggested rank per viewer (PPC Land 2026).
  2. Make the first 30 seconds deliver what the title and thumbnail promised. Basis: Studio's Intro metric measures exactly that window (YouTube Help 9314415); high CTR with low average view duration is YouTube's clickbait signature and gets fewer impressions (YouTube Help 7628154).
  3. Run Test & Compare on every episode with two or three title or thumbnail variants, and accept its pick. Basis: it chooses on watch time share over CTR, over a few days to two weeks; desktop Studio only, advanced features required (YouTube Help 16391400).
  4. Judge packaging only on uploads at least seven days old, by traffic source, across several uploads. Basis: wider CTR range in the first week and under 100 views; compare over the long term; traffic source changes CTR; small variations are not grounds for action (YouTube Help 7628154).
  5. Keep every published episode public. No deleting or unlisting weak ones. Basis: only public long-form earns qualified watch hours (YouTube Help 72851); a weak video does not lastingly harm the channel and older videos can regain traction (SEJ 2024).
  6. Hold one recognisable format per season, and build series from episodes that already worked. Basis: YouTube's own tips for casual and regular viewers (YouTube Help 13615784).
  7. After a missed fortnight, publish the next episode on its normal date and leave the gap. Basis: Beaupré disputed that channels are penalised for breaks (SEJ 2024, the article's paraphrase; "penalty box" is SEJ's term); repeated gaps of 14+ days are associated with fewer views per video in vendor data (AIR, 8 Jun 2026, correlational, larger channels), so gaps are counted at each checkpoint. (his rule: no catch-up doubles; two episodes in one week break the 5–7 hour budget that A9's "cut the load" verdict protects)
  8. Do not rely on subscribers to deliver an episode. No subscribe appeals as a reach strategy. Basis: the suggested list is the top place subscribers watch, and Beaupré put the subscribers-row CTR at around a tenth or less (PPC Land 2026, hedged, no sample). (his rule for subscribe appeals)

Instagram · the rented shop window

What it rewards. Watch time, likes and sends; Reels and Explore for non-followers, Feed and Stories for followers; original material only for recommendations. Basis: Instagram Ranking Explained (2023); Mosseri (SMT, Jan 2025); Original content guidelines (2026).

  1. Use Reels for strangers, carousels and feed posts for followers, Stories for followers only. Basis: Reels and Explore mostly show accounts the viewer does not follow; Stories show people the viewer follows; carousels get a second pass at followers who swiped by (Instagram 2023; Mosseri via Haberman).
  2. Post only material he shot or made, never another app's export with its watermark, and never a repost of someone else's clip. Basis: unoriginal content (reposts, screenshots, watermarks, speed changes, credit-only edits) can remove an account from recommendations to new audiences, regained only when most posts over 30 days are original (Original content guidelines; Instagram, 30 Apr 2026); watermarked Reels get less distribution (Instagram 2023).
  3. Build each Reel's first three seconds as its opener, and judge Reels on skip rate and sends per reach before likes. Basis: skip rate is the share of views skipped in the first three seconds (SMT, Aug 2025); sends weigh slightly more for unconnected content (Mosseri, Jan 2025).
  4. Test risky openers as Trial Reels, with auto-share on. Basis: shown to non-followers first, hidden from followers' Feed and Reels tab, metrics after about 24 hours, automatic sharing on 72-hour views (Meta, Dec 2024, updated Jun 2025). Available from 1,000 followers on a public account (Mosseri via SMT, Jul 2025); below that, post the opener as an ordinary Reel.
  5. Route anyone leaving Instagram through the bio link; use Story link stickers for followers only. Basis: no caption links; Story links reach followers only (SMT, Nov 2024).
  6. If non-follower views fall to near zero across a month of original posts, open Account Status before changing anything else. Basis: Account Status shows recommendation eligibility and allows appeal (Instagram 2023; Instagram, 30 Apr 2026). (his rule for the trigger)
  7. Feed post count is set by A2's hours. What the data says if hours allow: within-account weekly follower growth +0.12% at 1–2 posts a week, +0.26% at 3–5 (Buffer, Aug 2025, vendor, not size-segmented).

Substack and own site · the owned archive

What it rewards. Nothing algorithmic once a reader is on the list; on the way in, the platform's recommendation network. Basis: Substack Support; On Substack (Feb 2024); beehiiv (Jan 2026).

  1. Export the subscriber list on every A9 checkpoint date and keep the file outside Substack. Basis: Substack says the writer can export at any time (Substack Support). (his rule for the schedule)
  2. Turn on Substack recommendations and recommend a small number of publications he actually reads. Basis: recommendations plus the app drove 50% of new subscriptions network-wide (On Substack, Feb 2024); on beehiiv, publications that recommend others are 32 times more likely to be recommended back (beehiiv, Jan 2026, platform-published).
  3. Send paid-tier offers as web and email links. Basis: app-store purchases keep the billing relationship with Apple and cost 30% in the first year; web checkout inside the iOS app is shown only to US readers, so a reader in Portugal subscribing in the iOS app sees only in-app purchase, while desktop, mobile-web and email-link subscriptions are unaffected (Substack IAP FAQ, Aug 2025).
  4. Read clicks, replies and unsubscribes; do not make a decision on open rate. Basis: Apple's Mail Privacy Protection loads content in the background (Apple); beehiiv and GetResponse both say opens became less reliable.
  5. Do not plan on Notes for paying readers. Basis: one documented case, a viral Note with 378 free and no paid subscribers (Amy Suto, Jul 2026, anecdote).
  6. Keep the own site as the archive and the canonical link; do not budget hours for search discovery. Basis: position-1 click-through down 58% where AI Overviews appear (Ahrefs, Feb 2026, vendor); embeds count as External traffic in YouTube (YouTube Help 1714921).

2 · What his plan measures

claude.ai shows him no prices. It meters him against limits, and Anthropic publishes the rules but almost no rates.

Plans and prices (USD; the pricing page shows no euro prices). Pro is $20 a month billed monthly, or $17 a month billed annually ($200 up front). Max is $100 a month for five times Pro's usage per session and $200 for twenty times (Claude, Plans & pricing; Claude Help Center, About Claude's Max plan usage).

How usage is counted. The session limit resets every five hours; paid plans also carry weekly limits, and Anthropic reserves the right to add other caps (Claude Help Center, About Claude's Pro plan usage; Claude, Plans & pricing). Claude Code draws on the same limit as chat (both pages). The only numbers Anthropic gives: about 45 messages per five hours on Pro, defined as about 200 English sentences of 15 to 20 words on a less compute-intensive model; at least 225 on Max 5x and at least 900 on Max 20x for "relatively short" conversations on such a model. The window stays at 200K tokens on every plan (Max usage; Claude Help Center, usage and length limits).

What draws it down. Anthropic's list, dated 22 September 2026: message length, attachment size, current conversation length, tool use (Research, web search), model choice, effort level, artifacts, and multi-step tasks such as running code or browsing. Project content is cached and counts less when reused (Claude Help Center, Usage limit best practices). Higher effort spends more tokens and reaches the limit sooner (Claude Help Center, Change the model, effort and extended thinking settings).

How much each model costs him inside the plan is undocumented. None of these articles gives a per-model rate. The Pro article says only that a less compute-intensive model allows more messages. Fable is the one model with rules of its own (article dated 2 September 2026):

  • On Pro, Fable 5.1 sits outside the plan's limits and runs on usage credits.
  • On Max, up to half of the weekly limit may go to Fable at no extra cost; this is a cap inside the same pool, and Fable uses the pool faster than other Claude models.
  • On Team and seat-based Enterprise, premium seats include Fable; standard seats pay with credits (Claude Help Center, Claude Fable models on your plan).

Usage credits are prepaid and billed at standard API rates once an included limit is used up (Claude Help Center, Manage usage credits for paid Claude plans). Anthropic also raised five-hour limits on Pro, Max, Team and seat-based Enterprise when Opus 5.5 launched, without saying by how much (Anthropic, Introducing Claude Opus 5.5).

The consequence for routing: he pays in two currencies. Inside his limits the currency is a share of a five-hour and a weekly allowance, at an exchange rate per model that Anthropic does not publish. Past his limits, and for every Fable run on Pro, the currency is credits at API prices. That second rule is why this unit uses the API price list as the common measure: it is the only published price that applies to him, and, with credits enabled, it is his literal marginal price whenever he runs over.

5 · Cost per task

The cost of a task is input tokens times the input price plus output tokens, thinking included, times the output price, summed over every turn, with the whole history re-sent on each turn (B1 §2; §3 above). The price list fixes one factor. The other, how many tokens a model spends to finish the task, varies by model and by effort, and it can swamp the first.

A small task, by arithmetic. Assume a Shōmen post takes 4,000 input tokens (instructions, brand notes, the brief) and 1,500 output tokens including thinking. Those token counts are my assumption; nobody measured them. At API prices:

6 · When the cheap model is the right one

Three bodies of evidence say the cheap model is often enough, and they agree on when.

Routing research. FrugalGPT (Chen, Zaharia and Zou, TMLR 2024; first posted on arXiv 2023) found API prices varying by up to two orders of magnitude and reported that a cascade, which learns which models to query for each request, matched GPT-4's performance with up to 98% lower cost (arXiv 2305.05176). RouteLLM (Ong et al., ICLR 2025) trained routers that send each query to a strong or a weak model and cut costs by more than half in some cases without loss of response quality on the benchmarks they tested (ICLR 2025 proceedings). The mechanism in both: for a large share of queries the weak model's answer is already as good as the strong model's, and the saving comes from identifying those queries. Both tested 2023–24 models.

Compute against size. Snell and colleagues compared spending more inference compute on a smaller model against using a larger one at equal total compute. On problems where the smaller model already had a non-trivial success rate, the smaller model with more compute beat a model 14 times its size (Snell et al., arXiv 2408.03314; published at ICLR 2025 under a slightly changed title). Effort can stand in for model size on tasks within the small model's reach.

Vendor guidance. Anthropic positions Haiku 5.5 for classification, extraction and routing (Models overview) and for summaries, compactions and classification (Haiku 5.5 launch). Its effort documentation says to run Haiku 5.5 at low for chat, short tool tasks and simple high-volume requests, and at high for knowledge work, longer agent tasks and strict instruction following (Anthropic, Effort). A Claude Code team post (Lydia Hallie, 7 Jul 2026, before the 5.5 family) gives the general rule: smaller models for more routine tasks; larger models for more complex or ambiguous ones (Claude blog, Choosing a Claude model and effort level in Claude Code).

Where the cheap model falls off. Anthropic's own table shows the gap widening with task length. On GDPval-AA v2.1, a knowledge-work test, Haiku 5.5 scores 1620 against Sonnet 5.5's 1840; on Terminal-Bench 4.0, long agentic terminal tasks, 39.2% against 70.6% (Haiku 5.5 launch; vendor).

Put together, Haiku is the right model when the task is fully specified by its input, its correct output can be recognised at a glance, volume or speed matters, and an error is cheap to catch: classification, extraction to a fixed schema, reformatting, short tool calls. It is the wrong model when the task is open-ended, long or multi-step, or when a mistake hides well (a plausible wrong figure inside a long text). The combination of conditions is my synthesis of the sources above; each condition traces to one of them.

Two cautions specific to his setup. On the API, Haiku's price quintuples once a prompt passes 100K tokens (§3), so a cheap task loaded with a heavy context loses most of its advantage. On claude.ai, the same applies to his limits in kind: conversation length and attachments draw usage whatever the model (§2), so a small task belongs in a light chat, outside a Project stuffed with course documents.

7 · What thinking and effort settings do

Thinking is adaptive. On each request the model judges whether reasoning would improve the answer and how much; a simple factual question may get no thinking at all, a multistep problem gets more (Anthropic, Steering thinking). On Opus 5.5 and Fable 5.1 thinking is always on and cannot be switched off; on Haiku 5.5 and Sonnet 5.5 it cannot be switched off in the apps either (Help Center, Change the model, effort and extended thinking settings).

Effort sets how many tokens the whole response may spend. Anthropic describes it as a behavioural signal, not a strict token budget, covering how often and how deeply Claude thinks, how many tool calls it makes, and how much it explains. The levels are low, medium, high, xhigh and max, with availability varying by model; xhigh is aimed at long-horizon work of more than 30 minutes. At lower effort Claude can skip thinking on easy problems and still thinks, less, on hard ones. On Opus 5.5, effort is the main lever on reasoning because thinking cannot be disabled (Anthropic, Effort).

Defaults. On the API, Opus 5.5 and Haiku 5.5 default to medium; Sonnet 5.5 and Fable 5.1 to high (Effort; Models overview). Opus 5 defaulted to high, so the same request on Opus 5.5 with no effort set runs one level lower than it used to (Effort). In the apps, Sonnet 5.5 defaults to medium (Sonnet 5.5 launch) and Fable 5.1 to medium on claude.ai and in Cowork, high in Claude Code (Fable 5.1 launch). The claude.ai help article marks each model's default in the menu without listing them (Help Center).

On claude.ai the effort setting sits inside the model menu next to the send button. Anthropic's labels: low and medium suit routine tasks and stretch his limits; high is the best overall balance; extra high is for long-running coding and agentic work (Opus 4.7 and newer); max is the most thorough. Higher effort means more thorough responses that take longer, use more tokens and reach limits sooner (Help Center).

What a step of effort costs, measured. On Artificial Analysis's index (third-party vendor data, §5), Opus 5.5 from low to max costs 10.9 times as much per task for 16 more points; medium to high costs 36% more for 3 points; high to xhigh costs 1.9 times more for 2 points; xhigh to max 1.7 times more for 2 points. Sonnet 5.5 from low to max costs 15.6 times more (leaderboard; arithmetic on its figures). The Claude Code post's diagram shows a high-effort path using roughly seven times the tokens of a low-effort one, but the post labels its curves illustrative (Claude blog), so that figure is not evidence.

Extra thinking on easy problems is waste. Chen and colleagues documented reasoning models spending large amounts of compute on trivially easy problems (the title's example is 2 + 3) and showed the excess could be cut while keeping accuracy (Chen et al., ICML 2025; arXiv 2412.21187). Anthropic's own guidance says the same in product terms: lower effort before trying to talk the model out of thinking (Steering thinking).

Model or effort: the diagnostic. The Claude Code post separates the two: "the model setting is roughly how capable; the effort setting is roughly how thorough." If Claude had full context, clearly tried, and still got it wrong, pick a more capable model; if it skipped files, skipped tests or did not double-check, raise effort (Claude blog). The same post recommends the default effort for most tasks and treating effort as a standing preference more than a per-task dial (same post).

Keep effort fixed within a chat. On the API, changing thinking or effort between requests invalidates the prompt cache, with two exceptions the page lists (Steering thinking). Whether the same happens on claude.ai is undocumented, as is the effect of caching on his limits (B1 §2). Setting effort once, before the first message, costs nothing and avoids the question.

8 · Where the quality differences show, and where they do not

Long-form synthesis

On Anthropic's knowledge-work tests the gap between Opus and Sonnet nearly vanishes. GDPval-AA v2.1: Opus 5.5 1846, Sonnet 5.5 1844; AA-Briefcase v1.1: 1822 against 1811 (Sonnet 5.5 launch; vendor). The Haiku launch reports Sonnet at 1840 and 1824 on the same two tests and Haiku 5.5 at 1620 and 1578 (Haiku 5.5 launch), so Anthropic's own figures for Sonnet move by a few points between pages; the Sonnet page notes those two runs used a pre-release deployment with a since-fixed bug and may understate Sonnet slightly (Sonnet 5.5 launch). Anthropic ran Opus 5.5 at max effort for its table unless noted (Opus 5.5 launch).

On reasoning with tools (Humanity's Last Exam), Anthropic reports Opus 5.5 at 67.7%, Sonnet 5.5 at 64.5% and Haiku 5.5 at 57.4% (Sonnet 5.5 launch; Haiku 5.5 launch; vendor).

At the efforts he actually gets by default, the third-party index separates Opus and Sonnet: Opus 5.5 at medium scores 51, Sonnet 5.5 at medium 41, and Sonnet needs max effort (56, at $5.46 a task) to come within two points of Opus at max (leaderboard).

A course unit is long, open-ended, searched and cited synthesis. The case for Opus there rests on that default-effort gap and on Anthropic's unquantified statement that Opus is stronger on open-ended work (§1). No source here measured anything like a course unit. His own record adds one data point about process: in the Greek review, reports written without a fresh checker scored 4.28 on his rubric, and syllabus unit 12, written with one, scored 4.8 (method.md §1, citing audits/greece-13.4-cohorts.md). That is a small internal sample, and it measures a checking step, which is the subject of B6.

Code

On agentic coding the top two are level and Haiku falls behind on long tasks. Terminal-Bench 4.0: Sonnet 5.5 70.6%, Opus 5.5 66.4% (at xhigh), Haiku 5.5 39.2%. FrontierCode 1.1: Opus 54.4%, Sonnet 52.1% (at xhigh), Haiku 46.4% (Sonnet 5.5 launch; Haiku 5.5 launch; vendor). Anthropic still recommends Sonnet or Opus over Haiku for complex agentic coding (Haiku launch). For his generator scripts and parsers, Sonnet is enough on this evidence; the Claude Code diagnostic (§7) says when to move up.

Audit

No benchmark measures auditing a course unit against its sources. Three pieces of evidence bear on it.

  • Knowledge reliability. On AA-Omniscience v1.1 (3,000 questions across six domains; it rewards correct answers, penalises wrong ones and leaves abstentions unpenalised), Fable 5.1 has the highest accuracy, 68%; Opus 5.5 has the highest overall index, 48, against 46 for Fable 5.1 and Gemini 4 Argon; Gemini 4 Argon has the lowest hallucination rate, 4% (Artificial Analysis, AA-Omniscience; third-party vendor data).
  • Model judges. Strong model judges agreed with human preferences more than 80% of the time, the same rate at which humans agree with each other, and showed position, verbosity and self-enhancement biases (Zheng et al., NeurIPS 2023, arXiv 2306.05685). The judges tested were 2023 models.
  • Self-preference. Model evaluators score their own outputs above others', and the bias grows with the model's ability to recognise its own text (Panickssery, Bowman and Feng, NeurIPS 2024; arXiv 2404.13076).

So the best-evidenced part of his method is that a different model audits from the one that wrote. Choosing Fable specifically rests on that independence and on a narrow lead in factual accuracy; general capability benchmarks favour Opus 5.5 (§1). Fable and Opus are both Anthropic models, and none of the studies above tested whether self-preference carries across sibling models from one maker. That question stays open.

His own record is consistent with this. Audit 1 failed 23 of 91 cited claims in the A2/A6/A9 research document and none of the 7 sampled in B1 (Audit 1, 28 Oct 2026); the research document was written by Fable 5.1 research agents and B1 by Opus 5.5 (courses-index.md; the audit notes that its own prompt named Opus 5.5 for all four and that the index record should stand). Two documents on different tasks prove nothing about the models. They do show that the dearer model's output was the one that failed, and that the audit, whatever wrote the text, is what caught it.

Reformatting and triage

No benchmark isolates reformatting or triage, so the evidence that model size stops mattering here is indirect: the routing results and the compute-against-size result in §6, and Anthropic's positioning of Haiku for exactly these jobs. The reasoning that ties them together is mine: reformatting and triage are tasks whose correct output is fully determined by the input, so a model has nothing to add beyond following the instruction, and capability differences show up where a task needs something the input does not contain (judgement, synthesis, search). The check is cheap, because the output can be compared with the input at a glance; sample it before trusting it (B6).

One exception. Where the task is arithmetic over numbers, such as totals or averages from an export, the model should write and run code, and the code should produce the numbers. The same input can give a model different outputs even at fixed settings (B1 §5); a script gives the same output every time.

1 · The context window is the one thing you control

Anthropic defines the context window as all the text a language model can reference when generating a response, including the response itself, and separates it from the corpus the model was trained on: the window is the model's "working memory" (Anthropic, Context windows). Everything in a request counts against it: the system prompt, every message (tool results, images and documents included), the tool definitions, and the output, extended thinking included (same page).

That is the whole interface. The model's trained parameters are fixed when he uses it; Anthropic's claude.ai system prompt is written by Anthropic (Anthropic, System prompts release notes). Everything else that reaches the model reaches it as text placed in that window, and almost all of that text is his choice: the preferences he saved, the project instructions, the files he put in the Project, which skills are installed, whether memory is on, what he pastes, what he attaches, and when he starts a new chat. A better answer comes from a better window. There is no other lever.

How big the window is (as of 28 Oct 2026). Two different numbers are in circulation, and the one that applies to him is the smaller.

  • On the API, Opus 5.5, Sonnet 5.5, Haiku 5.5 and Fable 5.1 all have a 1M-token window with up to 128K output tokens per request (Anthropic, Context windows; Anthropic, Models overview).
  • On claude.ai paid plans, Anthropic's help centre states that the context window is 200K tokens across all models and paid plans, with some models at 500K on Enterprise (Claude Help Center, usage and length limits).

His Project runs on claude.ai, so his working budget is 200K tokens, a fifth of the figure on the model page.

Worked example: what the chat that wrote this unit received (observed, 28 Oct 2026). Before the brief arrived, the window already held Anthropic's system prompt; his saved preferences; the Learning Project's instructions; a snapshot of his memory (a profile, a preferences line and a listing of memory file paths with one-line descriptions); and the names and one-line descriptions of his fifteen plugin skills. The brief came next. During the work, a list of the Project's 24 documents arrived by name, then five of them (method.md, sourcing-standard.md, courses-index.md, reader.md, units_data.py) and about twenty web pages were read in. Each of those reads added text to the same window. None of it was "known" to the model before it was placed there.

2 · Tokens: what he pays with and what the model attends with

What a token is

A token is the smallest unit the model works in: a word, part of a word, a character or a byte. For Claude, the number of characters per token depends on the model and the language. On models before Claude 4.7, a token is about 3.5 English characters; Claude 4.7 and later models use a newer tokenizer that produces about 30 percent more tokens for the same text (Anthropic, Glossary). Opus 5.5 is in the later group, so on Anthropic's two figures an English token is roughly 3.5 ÷ 1.3 ≈ 2.7 characters. That is arithmetic on the glossary's numbers; nobody measured it, and the glossary gives no figure for Portuguese; for an exact count Anthropic points to its token-counting endpoint (same page).

On that arithmetic, a 200K-token window holds roughly 540,000 characters of English. This unit, at about 36,000 characters, would be about 13,000 tokens: around 6 to 7 percent of his window, before anything else is loaded.

Tokens as cost

On the API, price is per million tokens, with output dearer than input. As of 28 October 2026: Opus 5.5 $4 input / $20 output; Sonnet 5.5 $2 / $10; Haiku 5.5 from $0.10 / $0.50; Fable 5.1 $10 / $50 (Anthropic, Models overview). Which model to route each task to is B2.

The mechanism that matters more than the price list is that the model keeps nothing between turns. Anthropic's API documentation says the Messages API is stateless and that the full conversation history is sent with every request (Anthropic, Working with messages). The context-window page says the same in turn-by-turn terms: each new turn's input contains all previous conversation history plus the new message, and each response becomes part of the input for the next turn (Anthropic, Context windows).

The consequence is quadratic. Illustrative arithmetic, unmeasured: if every exchange adds about 2,000 tokens, the 30th turn sends about 60,000 tokens of history, and the 30 turns together send about 2,000 × (1 + 2 + … + 30) = 930,000 tokens of input, plus every fixed layer (preferences, project instructions, memory, skill descriptions) once per turn. The thirtieth turn sends thirty times the history of the first, whatever the length of the question itself.

On claude.ai he does not see token prices; he sees a usage limit. Anthropic says usage depends on the length and complexity of conversations, the features used and the model, that claude.ai, Claude Code and Claude Desktop share one limit, and that tools and connectors are token-intensive (Claude Help Center, usage and length limits). On the API, a repeated prefix can be cached: cache reads on Opus 5.5 are priced at 0.05 times the base input price, with a default five-minute cache lifetime (Anthropic, Prompt caching). Whether, and how, caching reduces what a claude.ai conversation draws from his usage limit is not stated in the help articles cited here. Treat it as unknown. Usage rules are B8.

Tokens as attention

The transformer architecture that large language models are built on uses self-attention, a mechanism that relates every position in a sequence to every other position when computing each output (Vaswani et al., Attention Is All You Need, NeurIPS 2017). Anthropic's engineering team draws the practical consequence: with n tokens there are n² pairwise relationships, so the model has a finite "attention budget" that each new token draws down, and context is "a finite resource with diminishing marginal returns" (Anthropic, Effective context engineering for AI agents, 29 Sep 2025). Anthropic's own documentation names the effect: as the token count grows, accuracy and recall degrade, which it calls context rot, and it concludes that what is in context matters as much as how much fits (Anthropic, Context windows).

Three bodies of evidence stand behind this, and they do not measure the same thing.

  • Peer-reviewed. Liu and colleagues found that models performed best when the relevant information sat at the beginning or the end of a long input, and noticeably worse when it sat in the middle, including models built for long contexts (Liu et al., "Lost in the Middle", TACL 12, 2024, 157–173). The models tested date from 2023.
  • Vendor research. Chroma tested 18 models from four families, Claude Opus 4, Sonnet 4, Sonnet 3.7, Sonnet 3.5 and Haiku 3.5 among them, and found performance growing less reliable as input length grew, even on simple retrieval and copying tasks; distractors (passages similar to the answer) lowered accuracy further, and the degradation was not uniform across positions or tasks (Hong, Troynikov and Huber, Context Rot, Chroma, 14 Jul 2025). Chroma publishes this on the site of its own retrieval-database product, so it has an interest in the conclusion; label it vendor data.
  • Platform statement. Anthropic states the degradation as a property of its models generally, without a figure (Anthropic, Context windows).

Where they differ: Liu et al. report a U-shape by position on 2023 models; Chroma reports that position, similarity and distractors interact and that the curve is not a clean U. None of the three measured Opus 5.5. The direction is consistent across all three: more text in the window, less reliable use of any one piece of it. The size of the effect on his model is unmeasured.

For his Project this means the 24 documents, the memory snapshot, the preferences, the project instructions and fifteen skill descriptions all draw on the same budget as the brief they are meant to support. Anthropic's guidance for the equivalent file in Claude Code is explicit: target under 200 lines per instruction file, because longer files consume more context and reduce adherence (Claude Code docs, Memory). That guidance is written for CLAUDE.md; applying it to project instructions is my analogy, and the claude.ai help centre states no such limit. What belongs in which layer is B4.

3 · What persists: within a chat, across chats

Within a chat

Inside one chat, everything persists because everything is re-sent. Previous turns are preserved completely and accumulate in the window (Anthropic, Context windows). On Opus 5.5 that includes its own previous thinking: Opus 4.5 and later Opus models keep earlier thinking blocks by default, and those count against the window like any other input (same page).

Within a chat, a fact can be lost in two ways.

  1. It is still in the window but not used. That is the attention problem in §2. A rule stated in turn 3 of a 40-turn chat is present and competing with everything after it.
  2. It is summarised away. On claude.ai, when code execution is enabled, long chats trigger automatic context management: Claude summarises earlier messages to continue. The help centre says the full history is preserved so earlier content can still be referenced, and that such long chats consume more usage, so a new chat may help (Claude Help Center, usage and length limits). In Claude Code the equivalent is compaction: after /compact, the root CLAUDE.md is re-read from disk and re-injected, and an instruction that vanished after compaction had usually been given only in the conversation (Claude Code docs, Memory).

Across chats

Across chats, nothing persists in the model. A new chat is a new request with a new window, built from whatever the product places in it. Claude Code's documentation states it plainly: each session begins with a fresh context window, and two mechanisms carry knowledge across sessions, the CLAUDE.md files he writes and the auto-memory notes Claude writes, both loaded at the start of every session (Claude Code docs, Memory). claude.ai works on the same principle with more layers:

  • Profile preferences ("Instructions for Claude") are account-wide and applied to all conversations (Claude Help Center, personalisation features).
  • Project instructions apply only to chats within that project (same article).
  • Project files sit in project knowledge; when that knowledge approaches the window limit, claude.ai switches the project to retrieval mode and pulls only the most relevant parts into the chat (Claude Help Center, RAG for projects, 10 Sep 2026).
  • Memory is written as individual topics while he chats, and an update made in one chat is picked up in the next. Each project has its own memory; inside a project Claude uses that project's memory plus his account memory from non-project chats, never another project's (Claude Help Center, chat search and memory). By default it does not store sensitive topics; it never stores government ID numbers, criminal history, financial account numbers or immigration status; deleting a conversation does not delete the memories written from it; incognito chats are excluded (same article).
  • Chat search retrieves past chats by retrieval-augmented search, visible as a tool call; it searches all chats outside projects, and project chats only within their own project (same article).

Each of these is text that something re-inserts. Remove the re-insertion and the fact is gone.

One route does run from his chats into a model, and it is slow. Since Anthropic's consumer-terms change of 28 August 2025, Free, Pro and Max users choose whether new or resumed chats may be used to improve Claude; opted-in data is retained for five years, otherwise 30 days (Anthropic, Updates to our consumer terms, 28 Aug 2025). That feeds a future training run. None of the documentation cited here describes any path by which a chat changes the model he talks to the next morning.

"It forgot" usually means "it was never told"

When a new chat ignores something he said last week, the fault is almost always in one of the re-insertion layers: the fact was said in a chat inside another project; memory did not write it, or wrote a different version of it; chat search was not triggered; the project switched to retrieval and pulled other passages. The diagnostic question is: which layer was supposed to carry this into the window, and did it?

His own rules already encode this. His "Teresa" disambiguation lives in the preferences, the layer present in every conversation. The method's rule of one chat per unit exists because everything earlier in a chat conditions what comes later, which the method describes as leaked context making a course repeat itself (method.md §4). Both are correct applications of the model above.

4 · Three kinds of knowledge: trained, retrieved, in the conversation

Trained

Claude is built on a model pretrained on a large corpus to predict the next word, then fine-tuned and trained with human feedback to act as an assistant (Anthropic, Glossary). What it learned this way stops at a cutoff. As of 28 October 2026, Anthropic lists the reliable knowledge cutoff of Opus 5.5, Sonnet 5.5, Haiku 5.5 and Fable 5.1 as June 2026 (Anthropic, Models overview). Today is four months past that.

The reader profile in his own brief shows the gap. "Ended competitive karate after the WIKF Global Cup (Oct 2026)" describes an event after the cutoff. No amount of training covers it; the model knows it only because the brief and his memory put it in the window. The same holds for any model lineup, price or plan limit dated after June 2026, which is why B2 and B8 are flagged to search before writing.

Retrieved

Retrieval-augmented generation fetches material at runtime and passes it into the context window with the question, so that the answer is grounded in evidence; the model may fetch it itself through a tool (Anthropic, Glossary). In his setup that covers web search, chat search, a Project in retrieval mode, a file pulled in from GitHub, and Claude Code reading a file. Retrieval is selection: the model sees the chunks that were fetched and nothing else. A Project in retrieval mode can hold up to ten times more, because it no longer loads everything (Claude Help Center, RAG for projects); the price is that the answer depends on what the search returned.

In the conversation

Whatever he typed, pasted or attached, plus everything the model itself wrote earlier in the chat. The second half is easy to miss. Each response becomes input to the next turn (Anthropic, Context windows), so a wrong figure the model produced at turn 5 is in the window at turn 25, read on every turn like anything else there.

Why the distinction matters

The three kinds look identical in the output. A sentence about the 2026 WIKF results reads the same whether it came from a page fetched ten seconds ago, from memory written last week, or from nowhere. The only way to tell is to know what was in the window, which is what the sourcing standard forces: a claim that points to a fetched source can be checked; a claim that points to nothing came from training or was invented. How models fail when they answer from training (confident, specific, wrong) is B6.

5 · Why the same prompt gives different answers

Sampling is the design

At each step the model produces a probability distribution over possible next tokens, and one is drawn. Temperature controls how random that draw is: higher gives more varied output, lower gives more conservative, more probable phrasing (Anthropic, Glossary). Sampling is deliberate. Holtzman and colleagues showed that always choosing the most likely continuation produces bland, repetitive, degenerate text, and that sampling from a truncated distribution ("nucleus sampling") produced text closer to human writing (Holtzman et al., "The Curious Case of Neural Text Degeneration", ICLR 2020). Temperature is an API parameter; none of the claude.ai help articles cited in this unit offers him a temperature setting.

Even fixed settings vary

Anthropic states that even with temperature set to 0, results will not be fully deterministic, and identical inputs may produce different outputs across calls, on its own service and through cloud providers (Anthropic, Glossary). Anthropic does not give the cause there. A lab study gives one candidate: Horace He at Thinking Machines sampled 1,000 completions at temperature 0 from an open-weights model (Qwen3-235B) and got 80 distinct completions; the most common appeared 78 times, and they first diverged at token 103. He traced the variation to computations whose results depend on batch size, which changes with server load; with batch-invariant kernels all 1,000 were identical (He, Defeating Nondeterminism in LLM Inference, Thinking Machines Lab, 10 Sep 2025). That is a lab blog post, not peer-reviewed, on a non-Anthropic model and serving stack; whether the same cause dominates on Anthropic's service is not stated by either source.

The divergence point is the useful detail. Once one token differs, every later token is conditioned on a different history, so a small difference early becomes a different answer.

In his setup, the prompt is rarely the same

The more common reason two runs differ is that the window differed. Between two chats with the same typed prompt:

  • the system prompt carries the current date and is periodically updated (Anthropic, System prompts release notes);
  • memory may have been updated in between (Claude Help Center, chat search and memory);
  • chat search and project retrieval may return different passages (same; RAG for projects);
  • Opus 5.5 uses adaptive thinking, always on, so the amount of reasoning in the window varies with the request (Anthropic, Models overview).

What this means for checking. One good answer is one draw. It shows the window can produce a good answer; how often it does stays unknown until there are more draws. That is the mechanism behind sampling-based audits; the protocol is B6.

The system prompt sets the frame. Project instructions and preferences are his standing orders at two scopes. Anthropic's help centre says the features can be used "independently or in combination" and states no order of precedence between them (Claude Help Center, personalisation features). Repeating a rule in two layers spends tokens twice; two layers that disagree leave the model to resolve the conflict with no documented rule. That is a project-rot risk, and B4 audits it.

Skills sit across the layers. Only each skill's name and description load at startup, about 100 tokens a skill, placed in the system prompt; the SKILL.md body (under 5K tokens) enters the window only when a request matches the description, and bundled files only when read (Anthropic, Agent Skills overview). His fifteen plugin skills therefore cost about 1,500 tokens in every chat, on Anthropic's per-skill figure, and they fire on their descriptions alone. His decompression skill lists "Netflix" and "dinner" among its triggers (observed), so it becomes a candidate to load whenever those words appear, whatever the conversation is about.

The user message is the request itself, and once sent it is part of the history re-read on every later turn (§3).

None of these is enforced. Claude Code's documentation says Claude treats its instruction files as context and that they are not enforced configuration; to block an action regardless of what Claude decides, it points to a hook; and it says the more specific and concise the instructions, the more consistently Claude follows them (Claude Code docs, Memory). Every instruction layer raises the probability of a behaviour. None guarantees it. How to write instructions that hold is B3.

Reading a bad output

When an answer is wrong, the model above gives four places to look, in this order:

  1. Was it in the window? If the fact lives only in a past chat, another Project, the vault or his head, the model never had it (§3, §4).
  2. Was it buried? If it was there, how much else was there with it, and where? Long windows degrade recall (§2).
  3. Did it come from training? If no source was in the window, the answer came from trained knowledge, which stops at June 2026 (§4).
  4. Was it one draw? Run it again in a fresh chat. If the answers differ materially, neither is reliable on its own (§5).

Each question has a different fix: put the fact into a layer that re-inserts it; trim the window or start a new chat; supply the source; sample before trusting. B3, B4, B6 and B8 take these one at a time.

Deliverable · How Claude works, in five minutes

Claude doesn't remember anything. Every time I send a message, the app sends Claude a single long piece of text, and Claude writes its reply by reading that text from the top. That text is called the context window. It holds Anthropic's own instructions, my standing instructions, the files in whatever project I'm working in, some notes the app keeps about me, the whole conversation so far, and my new message. Claude reads all of it, writes a reply, and then keeps nothing. When I send the next message, the whole lot is sent again with the new message added at the end.

So the only way to change what Claude does is to change what's in that text. The model itself is fixed. It was trained on a huge amount of writing to predict the next word, then trained further to behave like an assistant, and what it knows from that training stops around June 2026. Anything that happened after that, it only knows if something puts it in the window: me telling it, a web search it runs, or the notes the app keeps.

Those notes are what the app calls memory. As I chat, it writes down facts about me and my projects, and at the start of a new chat it puts those notes back into the window. That's the only reason it seems to remember. If something I said never made it into the notes, or I said it inside a different project, a new chat has never heard of it. When it "forgets", it was usually never told.

The text is measured in tokens, which are chunks of a few letters each. Tokens are what it costs. Because the whole conversation is re-sent every time, a long chat gets more expensive with every message: by the thirtieth message, it is re-reading thirty messages' worth of history to answer one. Tokens are also attention. The more text there is, the less reliably Claude uses any one piece of it, especially things buried in the middle. A short, clean window gives better answers than a long, cluttered one. That's why I start a new chat for each piece of work.

The same question can get different answers. Claude draws each next word from the likely options, because always picking the single most likely one produces flat, repetitive writing. And even when the settings are fixed, the servers don't give exactly the same result every time. On top of that, two chats are rarely identical: the date has changed, the notes have been updated, a search found different pages. Once one word differs, everything after it can go a different way. So one good answer shows it can get it right; it says nothing about how often. For anything that matters, I check a sample of its claims against the sources before I trust the rest.

Different instructions sit at different levels. Anthropic writes instructions that go into every chat. I have preferences that go into every chat I have. Each project has its own instructions that go only into chats in that project. Then there's what I type. All of it is just text in the same window. None of it is a hard rule; it makes the behaviour I want more likely, and the shorter and more specific it is, the more reliably it's followed.

My setup is a set of ways to put the right text in front of it. The course project holds the method and the index, so every chat starts with them. Skills are saved procedures: Claude sees only a one-line description of each, and loads the full instructions when a request matches. The finished units live in a GitHub folder that Claude only sees when I bring files in. Scheduled tasks start a fresh chat each time with a saved prompt and nothing else. Claude Code does the same in a code folder, with an instructions file it reads at the start.

If it gets something wrong, I ask four things. Was the fact in the text it was given? Was it buried under everything else? Did it answer from its training instead of a source? Did I just get one unlucky draw? Each one has a different fix.

Stratified-sample audit (every four units)
You are auditing four units of a self-study course written by Opus 5.5 for one reader. Units: {list the four unit codes and their vault paths}.

Method, in this order:
1. For each unit, list its sections. From every section draw one claim that carries a number, a named source, a mechanism or a recommendation. That is the sample; record it before checking anything.
2. Check each sampled claim against the source the unit cites: open the source; confirm the figure, the date, the sample size and that the claim is what the source says, not a stretch of it. Mark each claim PASS, FAIL (wrong, unsupported, miscited, or invented) or STALE (true when written, superseded now, with the newer figure).
3. For each unit compute the failure rate. If more than one claim in ten is FAIL, audit that unit in full: every cited claim, same procedure.
4. Check the deliverable: it exists in full inside the unit, it follows from the unit's evidence, and it is usable without reading the unit.
5. Check against the course goal and sourcing standard (in the unit's preamble): does the unit move the reader toward the stated outcomes, and is anything in it advice without evidence?

Output: a table per unit (section, claim, source, verdict, note); the failure rate; a verdict per unit (PASS / FULL AUDIT RUN, result / REWRITE with the sections named); the three most consequential errors across the four units in plain language; and one line each for the bibliography (new sources to add, sources to mark failed) and the changelog. Do not fix the units; report.

You are re-checking one unit written with live search six months ago: {unit code, vault path, date written}. How to start the next course hub

What a project hub needs before the first unit is written. Copy this page's structure (the generator scripts are in the zip); change the content. Three requirement sets: the website, the syllabus, the AI rules.

Since v6

The website requirements below are now met by design/theme.css, the Personal Mythology system: the drawer slides out at every width (no persistent sidebar), and a new hub is started with the new-course skill from the generator in the Learning Project. The text below is kept as the v5.1 record.

Website UX

  • Two hubs, visibly separated by colour: Learn (courses, written units, reading pages) and Build (method, briefs, audits, bibliography, template, changelog). Home shows both as tiles and the status block.
  • Pages, not one scroll; hash-routed so every page has a link. Fixed top bar: menu (left), title, current page, theme toggle. Left sidebar persistent on desktop, drawer on phone; group labels carry the hub colour. 44 px targets.
  • A course page shows the course: goal, three outcomes mapped to units, progress, unit cards with status and a "Read" action. Prompts never appear on a course page; they live in Build · Briefs.
  • Written material has its own reading page under Learn, linked from the unit cards it covers, with a back link to the course.
  • Dark first, light designed not inverted; toggle remembers per browser, follows the system when unset. Fonts DM Sans + Space Mono. Dark: page #0a0a0c, cards #131316, border #1e1e24. Colours: Learn #ff6b6b, Build #f4a261, Course A #2a9d8f, Course B #9b5de5, deep dives #e9c46a, goals #457b9d. No grey as a dominant accent.
  • No horizontal overflow at 400 px: long text wraps anywhere; only tables and code may scroll, inside their own container.

Syllabus requirements

  • Course = 8–10 core units + 2 deep dives. Each unit: code, title, one-line scope, a shipped deliverable, an Opus brief. No length rule; the topic decides.
  • Course goal in one sentence plus three measurable outcomes, each mapped to the units that deliver it, shown on the course page and baked into every brief.
  • Every unit ends with something shipped (a document, a table, a skill, a model), built in full inside the unit, not described.
  • Flag units that depend on current facts; they are written with live search and re-checked for staleness six months on.
  • Two deep dives written last: the comparables (what others did) and the hard-number one (money, time, exit math).
  • An explicit writing order, with interleaving where two units build the same asset.
  • Frame first: the pick, the reasons, the override condition, and where the evidence pushes back. The reader decides; the hub records.

AI prompt and model rules

  • One shared preamble per course (reader, thesis, course goal, sourcing standard, tone, citation rules, "search before writing when facts are current"), baked into every brief so each chat is self-contained.
  • Sourcing standard: peer-reviewed, platform-official or large-sample vendor data; anecdote and vendor data labelled; no uncited figures; no invented names or numbers; advice without evidence fails the unit.
  • One chat per unit. Never reuse a chat across units. The deliverable is written in the same chat as its unit.
  • Writing: Opus 5.5. Audit: Fable 5.1 with the stratified-sample prompt every four units (more than one in ten fails → full audit) and the staleness prompt at six months. Sonnet/Haiku only for derivative work.
  • Output saved to the vault at courses/<course>/<code>-<slug>.md; the hub adds it to Learn, moves counters, logs the change, extends the bibliography.
