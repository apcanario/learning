# Supply Chain Planning for Product Teams · briefs

The shared preamble (`{GOAL}` is filled with the course goal) and one brief per unit. kit/pack.py reads every `## ` section; nothing above the first is read.

## Preamble

Write one unit of a self-study course in the long-form report format of the Learning Project's markdown courses: a long-form report in Markdown. Length is whatever the topic needs (2,000–6,000 words for the body; the deliverable is not counted in that range); do not pad and do not cut to a target. Quality over quantity: a shorter unit whose every claim was opened and checked is better than a longer one. Reader: someone who builds, sells or implements supply chain planning software, or is moving into that work: a product manager, a designer, an engineer, an implementer or a solutions consultant; experienced in their own craft, with no planning background; has never built a forecast, set a safety stock or sat in a sales and operations planning meeting; reads in order (each unit assumes the ones before it), reads on a phone, checks the citations, and wants genuine depth rather than a primer. Address the reader as 'you'. This course is written to be shared with anyone; like the other courses of the Supply Chain track it is an exception to the Learning Project's single-reader rule. Nothing about the Project's owner, his employer, his role or his work appears in it. The course stands on its own and assumes no other course: where the Supply Chain Execution course of this track treats a topic at length (the warehouse, transport, order promising, the buying committee, the stages of a sale, implementation), say so in one sentence naming that course and give here only what planning needs. Frame: assume no planning vocabulary: define each term on first use and add it to the unit's glossary lines. Teach no design, research, engineering or sales method; the reader has their craft. Teach the planning decisions, the people who make them, the data and the software they make them with, who buys that software and what it is said to be worth. The course is for learning the domain. It is not interview preparation and no unit is framed as one. Concepts, no calculation: no formula and no worked calculation appears in any unit. A method is described by what goes in, what comes out, what it assumes and when it fails; a quantity is described by what moves it and in which direction. A number appears only as a sourced finding, in its source's own terms, or as a case fact marked fictional. Where a textbook gives a formula, name it, say what it relates and cite the chapter; do not reproduce or apply it. Methods and AI: statistical forecasting, machine-learning forecasting and mathematical optimisation are methods, treated on their published evidence like any other. Generative AI, language-model assistants, agents and any vendor's AI branding are out of scope: where a source raises them, the unit says so in one sentence and moves on. Where a vendor calls a method 'AI', report that as the vendor's word and name the method if the vendor's own documentation names it. Vendors: no real vendor is assessed, ranked, compared or recommended in this course, and no real vendor's product line is mapped. A real vendor is named only in two ways: to attribute a public statement to the company that made it (dated, graded vendor-reported), or as an entry in a dated list taken from a named analyst report or an audited filing, every vendor on that list given alike. No vendor receives more space, a warmer word or a harder one than another; nothing from inside any vendor appears. Worked product examples use the fictional vendors of the case register (Lathmoor and its two fictional rivals), labelled '(fictional)'; the reader is given the template and the method to map a real product from its public pages themselves. A vendor's description of itself is evidence of what it claims, never of what its product does. Real companies as cases: a real company's planning practice is described only from a peer-reviewed paper, a published teaching or journal case with named authors, or the company's own audited filing, cited and dated; where the company's own staff or its software supplier co-wrote the account, say so and label it an interested party's account. Evidence grades: every claim that carries a number, a mechanism or a recommendation carries one grade from the key set in SCP1: independent (peer-reviewed research; a forecasting competition's published results; an industry association's survey with its sample stated; an audited company filing, for that company's own figures; the text of a standard, for what it says), analyst (a named analyst firm's report, with its date and how it was read), vendor-reported (a vendor's page, release, case study or sponsored survey; a peer-review site's content), practice (a standard textbook's or named practitioner's account of how the work is done, with no measurement behind it; a survey whose sample is not stated). A number a source repeats from another work is graded by its origin, and practice if the origin cannot be opened. Give a number in its source's own terms, with whose figure it is, the sample and the year. Study design: say what kind of evidence a finding is (a forecasting competition, a field experiment, a laboratory experiment, a simulation, a single-company case study, a survey), with its data, setting and year; a simulation result is not a field result, a correlation is called a correlation, and a finding from one industry or dataset applied to another is labelled as the unit's inference. Common claims: where a claim is common in the industry and its support is thin, name the claim and say what the evidence shows; the course's list of such claims is in the seed bibliography, each with the unit that tests it; say 'no independent source found' where none was. Decision pattern (SCP2 to SCP12): treat each planning decision as the decision itself and its horizon, who makes it and how often, the data it needs, what the software does, how it commonly goes wrong, and the measure that shows it improved. Kinds of company: say which kind of company a practice belongs to where it differs (a retailer, a distributor, a manufacturer); do not present one kind's practice as universal; where a term differs by region or by school (sales and operations planning, integrated business planning), give both and say who uses which. Running case: three fictional companies: Corvane, a fashion retailer; Ostrel Foods, a food distributor; Pellward, a maker of small kitchen appliances. Their facts come only from courses/supply-chain-planning/notes/case-facts.md; label each '(fictional)' on first use in the unit; a fact the unit invents about them is listed in the notes so that it can be added to the register; the case is never evidence for a claim about the world; no case company is ever said to use a real vendor's product; where a unit needs one to use a product, it is a fictional vendor's. Deliverables are built twice: in full on the case company the brief names, then as a template the reader fills for a real company or product, each blank saying what goes in it. Every unit ends its body, before the deliverable, with two short sections: 'In the planning meeting' (what to listen for and what to ask when you sit with a planner or in a planning meeting; for the units on products and buyers, what to ask a colleague in sales, services or product) and 'Self-check' (five to eight questions someone who runs that planning process or owns that product would expect the reader to answer, each with a short answer drawn from the unit's sourced text). Safety rules that hold in every unit: no price, discount, revenue or win rate of a private company is stated unless that company published it; no real customer of any vendor is described beyond what a dated public source states; no return, saving or payback is promised to the reader: a reported result is the reporter's, with its grade; no legal, tax or accounting point is stated as the reader's: it is put as a question with the body to consult. Tone: direct, no flattery, no filler, no motivational language, no 'not X but Y' constructions. Course goal: {GOAL} Sourcing standard: every claim that carries a number, a mechanism or a recommendation is sourced where it is made; acceptable sources are peer-reviewed research; published results of forecasting competitions; standard textbooks cited by edition and chapter; industry association reports and surveys with their sample and year; analyst reports, by firm, title and date, read in full, in a public reprint or in abstract, the unit saying which; audited company filings; standards bodies' own pages; and vendors' own pages, releases and documentation, labelled vendor-reported and dated; peer-review sites are evidence of what named kinds of user say, with where the count of reviews was read, never of how a product performs; a book or article by someone who sells the method it describes is an interested party's account and is labelled so; videos, podcasts, social-media posts, unsigned blogs, comparison and lead-generation sites, and press rewrites of a release are not sources, and may be named only as the claim under examination; every reference is opened before it is cited: found by a search, read at its publisher's page, its DOI address or the issuing body's own page, and recorded in the unit's source log with the address, what was read (abstract, reprint or full text), the date, the kind of venue it is (peer-reviewed journal, academic or professional publisher's book, forecasting competition, association, analyst firm, audited filing, standards body, vendor) and any commercial interest its authors have in what they describe; a claim that rests on an abstract alone says so; a reference that could not be opened carries no claim; the seed bibliography (courses/supply-chain-planning/notes/bibliography.md) is a starting list whose entries were checked for existence only, never an authority on what they say; cite the edition or release actually opened; no uncited figures; no invented names, companies, studies, quotations or numbers; if a claim cannot be sourced, say so and leave it out of the deliverable. A unit that reads as advice without evidence is a failed unit. Links are given as links (the DOI address for a paper), so the course bibliography can collect them. Figures: where a section's content has a shape, declare it as a fenced viz block of a kind kit/viz.py draws (one to four a unit is a guide; none where nothing has a shape); prefer the kinds that draw structure (flow, decision, matrix, timeline, states, swimlanes, tree); a figure adds no fact, draws numbers only where the unit's text gives them sourced or marked fictional, and its caption says what it shows and where its data comes from. Where the unit depends on current facts (analyst reports and their vendor lists, market figures, company filings, vendors' public pages), search before writing and date the facts. End with the unit's deliverable, built out in full, not described.

## SCP1

Unit SCP1, The planning map, the words and the evidence. Search before writing. Cover: supply chain planning as against supply chain execution, and why the line matters for product work; planning as a set of decisions sorted by horizon (years, months, weeks, days) and by flow (buying, making, moving, selling), from the advanced-planning textbook's own matrix, named and cited; the cascade from a demand forecast to a released order, at the level of a map only (each later unit takes a part of it); the same cascade for a retailer, a distributor and a manufacturer, and what each leaves out; what a plan is as an object (a quantity, an item, a place, a date, a version, an owner) and why plans come in versions; the systems a reader will hear named (ERP and its requirements planning, advanced planning systems, demand planning, inventory optimisation, sales and operations planning tools, retail planning) and where each sits on the matrix, as categories only; how the software market is described by its analysts, as categories only; the kinds of evidence in this field (forecasting competitions, field experiments, laboratory experiments, simulations, single-company case studies, surveys, textbooks, analyst reports, vendor material, peer reviews) and what each can and cannot show; how an analyst evaluation is made and paid for, from the analyst firm's own published method, saying which pages opened and which refused; how to read a vendor case study that reports an inventory or accuracy gain; the course's four evidence grades, defined so that two readers would give the same grade, including the rulings in the preamble (peer-review sites, unsampled surveys, repeated numbers, the text of a standard) and a public authority's statement of a rule it administers; the common industry claims on the course's list, each stated as it circulates, set against the evidence this unit opens, and marked with the unit that tests it. Deliverable: a one-page planning map (decision, horizon, who decides, system category), built for Pellward and for Corvane; a working glossary of the terms this unit introduces, which later units extend; and the grade key every later unit uses, with one worked example per grade and per kind of study; then the map as a template.

## SCP2

Unit SCP2, Demand and its data. Cover: what demand is as against sales, orders and shipments, and what a stockout hides from the record; the three dimensions every demand number has (item, place, time) and the hierarchies built on them; why the level at which demand is viewed changes what it looks like; the patterns planners name (level, trend, season, intermittent, lumpy, short life cycle), each with the source that defines it and the category scheme the intermittent-demand literature uses; what moves demand that history does not show (price, promotion, weather, events, a competitor, a new product replacing an old one); cleaning history: outliers, stockout periods, one-off orders, and who decides what counts; items with no history; the master data a demand plan rests on (item, unit of measure, pack, launch and end dates, customer and location) and how it commonly fails; where the data comes from (point of sale, orders, shipments, a customer's forecast) and what each is a record of; how a retailer's, a distributor's and a manufacturer's view of the same demand differs. Deliverable: a demand profile sheet (item group, pattern, drivers, history available, data source, known gaps, who owns the data), built in full for Corvane and for Ostrel Foods; then the template.

## SCP3

Unit SCP3, Forecasting methods. Cover: what a forecast is (a statement about the future with an uncertainty, made at a level and a horizon) and what it is for; the simple baselines and why every method is judged against one; the method families one by one (smoothing methods, models of trend and season, methods for intermittent demand, regression on causes such as price and promotion, tree-based machine learning, neural networks, combinations of methods), each by what goes in, what comes out, what it assumes, what data it needs and when it fails; forecasting across a hierarchy and making the levels agree; a forecast as a range or a distribution, and who uses that; new products and short life cycles; what the large forecasting competitions found, with their data, their year and what won, and what their organisers and their critics say the results do and do not show for a company's own data; what the retail forecasting review says separates research from practice; why no method is best everywhere, from the literature; how a planning product chooses a method per item, as the textbooks and vendors' documentation describe it, graded. Deliverable: a method selection table (demand pattern and data available, method family, what it needs, when it fails, the evidence and its grade), worked for the item groups of SCP2's profiles at Corvane and Ostrel Foods; then the template.

## SCP4

Unit SCP4, Forecast error and what it is used for. Cover: error, bias and accuracy as ideas, without calculation; the measures in common use one by one (percentage errors, weighted percentage errors, scaled errors, bias), each by what it rewards, what it hides and where the forecasting literature says it breaks; why the level, the horizon and the lag at which error is measured change the number, and why two companies' accuracy figures cannot be compared; forecastability: how much error is in the demand itself; measuring a forecast against a naive baseline; forecast value added as its author describes it, graded practice unless a measured test is opened; measuring the steps of a forecasting process separately; what error is used for downstream (safety stock, capacity, the plan's credibility) and why the measure should match the decision; what the research says about the link between forecast error and inventory, with design and setting, and where it says nothing; accuracy targets and benchmark surveys, each with its sample or the note that none is stated; how error measures are gamed, sourced or labelled practice. Deliverable: a measurement plan (what is measured, at what level, horizon and lag, against which baseline, for which decision, who sees it), built in full for Ostrel Foods and for Pellward; then the template.

## SCP5

Unit SCP5, Judgment, overrides and the demand planner. Cover: the demand planner's job: what they are measured on, their week and month, what they look at and what they change; judgmental adjustment of statistical forecasts: what the field studies found about how often planners adjust, in which direction, at what size and with what effect, with samples and settings; where judgment adds information (promotions, events, launches, things the history cannot know) and where it adds bias; the sources of bias the literature names (optimism, sales targets, anchoring, pressure from above); consensus forecasting between sales, marketing, finance and planning, and what each brings and wants; one forecast or several (a sales target, a financial plan and an operational forecast) and why they differ; what the experimental research says about people overriding algorithms, with design and setting, and the field experiment on merchants' override rights; what studies of forecasting support systems say helps a planner adjust well; how planning products present a forecast for review (exceptions, alerts, notes, audit trails), from textbooks and vendors' documentation, graded; short-horizon forecasting from recent signals as vendors describe it under the name demand sensing, graded vendor-reported unless an independent test is opened. Deliverable: an override policy (who may change what, at which level, with what recorded reason, how the change is judged afterwards), built in full for Corvane; a demand planner role card (measured on, rhythm, tools, top tasks, what goes wrong, what they do about it, what they would ask of the software) on Ostrel Foods' named planner from the case register; then the templates.

## SCP6

Unit SCP6, Why inventory exists and how much. Cover: the reasons stock is held, one by one (cycle stock from ordering in batches, safety stock against uncertainty, stock in transit, stock built ahead of a season or a promotion, stock that separates one process from the next), from the inventory textbook, each with what would have to change for it to shrink; service as a target: the measures in use (the chance of not running out in a cycle, the share of demand filled from stock, lines and orders filled complete), what each means to a customer and why the choice of measure changes the stock; the trade-off between service and stock as a curve, described without calculation: what moves it (demand uncertainty, lead time and its variability, review frequency, batch size) and in which direction; what holding stock costs, as the textbooks list the components, and working capital as finance sees it; what running out costs, from the out-of-stock research, with samples; grouping items to set policy (by volume, by variability, by margin, by criticality) and what the literature says for and against the common schemes; the textbook assumptions behind safety stock (about the shape of demand and of lead time) and what happens when they fail; excess and obsolete stock: how it arises and who answers for it; the inventory measures in common use (turns, days of cover, fill rate, excess), by definition. Deliverable: an inventory purpose map (item group, why stock is held, what drives the amount, who owns the decision, the measure), built in full for Ostrel Foods; a service policy table (item group, service measure, target set by whom, reason) for Corvane; then the templates.

## SCP7

Unit SCP7, Replenishment. Cover: the two questions every replenishment rule answers (when to order, how much); the standard policies one by one (order at a set level, order on a set day, order up to a level, minimum and maximum), each by what it assumes, what it needs and what kind of item it suits, from the inventory textbook; order quantity as a trade-off, described without calculation, and the constraints that override it (minimum order, pack and pallet sizes, a full truck, a supplier's calendar, shelf life, shelf space); lead time: its parts, who knows them, and how wrong the recorded figure commonly is, only as far as a source supports it; perishables and date-coded goods; replenishing a store against replenishing a warehouse; the buyer or replenishment analyst: what they are measured on and their day; system-proposed orders and what people do with them, from the empirical study of store ordering, with its sample; what the record does to the rule: inventory record inaccuracy and phantom stock, from the empirical research, with its sample and setting; supplier-managed replenishment and what the research says it changes; how replenishment commonly fails (a parameter set once and never revisited, a promotion the rule did not know about, a record that says stock is there); the measures, by definition. Deliverable: a replenishment policy decision table (item group, policy, the parameters and who maintains them, the constraint that binds, common failure), built in full for Ostrel Foods' warehouse and for Corvane's stores; a replenishment flow map (step, who, system, data needed, failure, measure); then the templates.

## SCP8

Unit SCP8, The network: stock in more than one place. Cover: echelons: what changes when a warehouse feeds stores or a factory feeds warehouses; where to hold stock and in what form: pooling uncertainty, holding stock upstream against downstream, postponing the last step, each from the textbooks and the original papers; planning each stocking point alone against planning them together, and what the multi-echelon literature says the difference is, with the design of each finding (model, simulation, documented company case); distribution requirements planning as the textbook describes it; deploying stock that has arrived and sharing out stock that is short, with the rules in use and who is favoured by each; the bullwhip effect: the original paper's four causes, the classroom experiment and what it showed about people, and the empirical study that looked for the effect in industry data and what it did and did not find; what sharing demand information and collaborative planning with suppliers and customers were found to do, with design and setting, and what collaborative forecasting schemes asked of the companies in them; transfers between sites and what triggers them; how network planning commonly fails. Deliverable: a stock positioning map (stocking point, what is held there and why, who decides, what it is protected against), built in full for Corvane's network from the case register; a bullwhip cause table (cause, where it would show in the case company, what would dampen it, the source and grade) for Ostrel Foods and one of its suppliers; then the templates.

## SCP9

## SCP10

## SCP11

## SCP16

Unit SCP16, Capstone: one planning product memo. Cover: what a product strategy memo is for and who reads it; its parts (the planning decision and the problem in it, the roles it touches by role card, who would pay and on what value case, how the decision is made today and with what data, how the field handles it, the bet, what would show the bet was wrong); how each part draws on a deliverable of an earlier unit, named by code; the difference between a claim the memo can source and one it must mark as an assumption; how a memo on planning differs from one on execution: the gain is a number someone has to believe, and the user is an expert who can ignore the product; how to write it for a product leader, a sales leader and a finance reader at once. Then write one memo in full, on an opportunity found in the case companies and argued only from public evidence and the case register, every figure marked fictional or sourced. The unit says plainly that the memo that matters is the one the reader then writes on a real opportunity, which needs knowledge this course does not hold. Deliverable: one complete worked memo; the memo template, each blank naming the earlier deliverable that fills it; and a one-page checklist for the reader's own memo.

## DD-SCP1

Deep dive SCP1, Planning as companies have published it. Written after the sixteen core units. Search before writing. Cover: five to seven planning practices that real companies have described in peer-reviewed or practitioner-reviewed journals or in published cases with named authors, chosen to span a retailer, a manufacturer and a distributor and to span forecasting, inventory, network and process; start from the seed bibliography's case entries and replace any that will not open; for each: the company and the year, the planning decision, what was done before, the method adopted, the result as reported and in whose words, who wrote the account and their interest in it, what the account leaves out, and what has been published since; read each in full where it can be opened and say where only an abstract was read; what the accounts have in common (who sponsored the work, how planners were brought to use it, how the gain was measured) and where they differ; what a reader can and cannot carry from a published success to another company; the survivorship in such accounts: failures are rarely published, and the unit says what it found when it looked for them. No vendor of planning software is assessed; where an account names its software supplier, the name is given once, as the account gives it, and nothing is drawn from it. Deliverable: a comparables table (company, year, decision, method, reported result, reported by, grade, what transfers and what does not), built in full for the cases read; a reading sheet for any published planning case (the questions to put to it); then the table as a template for cases the reader finds.

## DD-SCP2

Deep dive SCP2, The hard numbers. Written last. Search before writing. Cover: the numbers that circulate in planning, each traced to its origin and stated in its source's own terms with sample, design and year: how often shelves are empty and why, from the out-of-stock studies; how inaccurate inventory records were found to be; how large and how useful planners' forecast adjustments were found to be; what the forecasting competitions measured as the gap between methods; what the field experiment on override rights measured; what was measured about the bullwhip effect in industry data; what the published company accounts reported as stock or service gains; what surveys report on spreadsheet use and on process maturity, with their samples or the note that none is stated; then the numbers this field does not have: an independent measure of what a planning system returns, a general rate at which forecast error turns into stock, a benchmark of accuracy that holds across companies, each with what was searched and what was found; for the vendor-told numbers on the course's common-claims list, the claim as it circulates, who makes it, and what stands behind it; what a finance reader should ask of any number in a value case. The unit corrects, by the correction route, any earlier unit whose number it finds misstated. Deliverable: an evidence ledger (claim, the number in its source's terms, source, sample and design, year, grade, what it cannot show), built in full; a one-page list of the numbers the field lacks; then the ledger as a template for a number the reader meets.
