## 3 Disputes
1. Theodicy (section 1): real, but Lloyd-Jones is placed on the wrong side (row 6) and the counter-case (Poseidon's vengeance, the Phaeacians) is the report's own argument with no scholar behind it. One side wrongly named, the other unnamed. Erratum 1 supplies Allan (2006) with Lloyd-Jones for continuity and Clay, The Wrath of Athena (1983), for the narrative undercutting the frame.
2. Telemachy (section 2): real and classic, fairly stated (duplicated council vs simultaneous action, the ambush plot), but "Nineteenth-century analysts" and "Unitarians" carry no name. Both sides unnamed. Erratum 2 supplies Kirchhoff (1879) and Klingner (1944).
3. Unreliable narrator (section 4): real; the ancient evidence (Juvenal, Plato) is named and right, the modern sides are "Some critics" and "Others". Both sides unnamed. Erratum 3 supplies Most (1989) for the rhetorical reading and Richardson (1996) against.
4. Epicaste (section 5): Homer against Sophocles, both named, fairly stated. A variant rather than a dispute, but real.
5. Heracles' phantom and 11.565–627 (section 5): scholia and Page named; "Defenders note" unnamed. One side unnamed. Erratum 4 supplies Heubeck (Oxford commentary vol. 2, 1989). Also leaves out that Aristarchus athetised the passage in antiquity, so "modern analysts widen the suspicion" understates the ancient doubt (revise item).
6. Penelope's recognition (section 6): real; Harsh and Katz named on the two non-traditional sides; Winkler given with no title; the traditional view (she does not know until the bed test) has no representative. Erratum 6 gives Winkler's title and adds Heubeck for the traditional reading. Fair otherwise.
7. Wilson (section 7): "Reviewers object" is unnamed on one side and Wilson herself on the other. The three objections are real and the closing sentence is fair. I cannot supply a reviewer's reference I am sure of; revise item, not an erratum.
8. Ending (section 8): Page against Wender and Heubeck, both sides named with titles; the telos ambiguity flagged. The model box of the report.

Missing: whether the Odyssey poet knew the Iliad as a text (Pucci, Odysseus Polutropos, 1987, for intertextual allusion) or shares a tradition with it (Nagy 1979), which the section "Against the Iliad" needs before it can say the Odyssey "defines itself against" the Iliad; the ex halos ambiguity is stated in the body without a box (module 4.4 and 10.4 carry it, so acceptable); the Cyclops as colonial ethnography (the body's "colonist's eye") is a modern reading with its critics and is given as fact.

## 5 Depth
Strongest:
- The Cyclops read in full: moves from ethnography to xenia inverted to the Outis/mētis pun to the boast and ends on the reason ("the self-effacing cunning that saves Odysseus and the heroic need to be known that endangers him"), with the curse shown to be a plot summary.
- Demodocus and the reverse simile (section 3): the sacker of cities weeping as a captive widow, Alcinous' gloss turning suffering into song; the Odyssey's "sharpest comment on the Iliad" is earned, not asserted.
- The household against the battlefield (section 9): the oikos defined by its stores, the villains as consuming guests, the battle fought in a dining hall, and the cost falling "on those who belonged to the household without owning anything in it", which ties the hanging back to the theodicy.
Weakest:
- Laertes: three sentences; "gratuitous cruelty to some, a release of grief to others" names neither side and does not say why the poem tests a father who poses no danger.
- The catalogue of women: a list of names (one missing), "Hesiodic in form" and "aimed at its audience"; no reason given why the poet sets a Hesiodic catalogue inside Odysseus' speech or what Arete's reply does with it.
- Metis against bie: Nagy's reading is stated in one sentence and the section ends on "the bow is both force and skill", a qualification where the reader needs the argument; the quoted-line requirement is not met here.
