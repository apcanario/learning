## Findings
1. [should-fix] [register] Section "Self-test".
   Current: "Thomas reads it as pointing the emperor to the darker parts of his own poem and counts Ovid among the first anti-Augustan readers; the plainer reading is self-defence."
   Problem: The body says something narrower: "your" *Aeneid* points the emperor to the part of his epic least like his moral legislation, the unlawful love. "The darker parts" is not what the body (or the Tristia passage) says, so the answer and the body disagree.
   Fix: "Thomas reads it as pointing the emperor to the part of his own epic least like his moral legislation and counts Ovid among the first anti-Augustan readers; the plainer reading is self-defence."
   Basis: report body, section "What ancient readers thought" (Ovid paragraph); Ovid, *Tristia* 2.533–536; McGill, BMCR 2001.08.23 (Thomas reads *tuae Aeneidos auctor* as more than a claim of commission) (https://bmcr.brynmawr.edu/2001/2001.08.23).

## Confirmed
- W. R. Johnson, *Darkness Visible* (1976), coined the "Harvard school" label, naming Brooks, Parry, Clausen and Putnam, against a "European" school of Eliot, Pöschl, Büchner and Otis, and joined neither (Wikipedia, "Darkness Visible: A Study of Vergil's Aeneid" and "Harvard School").
- Brooks, "Discolor aura" (1953): the poem in limbo, "splendid despair" (Wikipedia, "Harvard School"); *AJP* 74 (1953) 260–280 from knowledge.
- Parry, "The Two Voices of Virgil's *Aeneid*" (*Arion*, 1963): "public voice of triumph", "private voice of regret"; Parry on the Umbro lament, 7.759–760 (Wikipedia, "Harvard School"; "Umbro (priest)"); that the article opens with Umbro, from knowledge.
- Clausen, "An Interpretation of the *Aeneid*" (*HSCP* 68, 1964): "long Pyrrhic victory of the human spirit", "no sense of triumph" (Wikipedia, "Wendell Clausen").
- Putnam, *The Poetry of the Aeneid* (1965), books 2, 5, 8 and 12; the ending as Juno's (wrath's) victory (Wikipedia, "Harvard School").
- Putnam, *The Humanness of Heroes* (2011): book 12 as the decline of a man famed for pietas into the killer of a wounded suppliant (West, CJ Online 2013.07.01).
- West: no suppliant in the *Iliad* or *Aeneid* is spared; *et debellare superbos* completes the precept (6.853); Tarrant takes the anger as "to some degree directed at himself" (West, CJ Online 2013.07.01).
- Vietnam: the school's founding papers predate 1965; Horsfall notes it began earlier (Wikipedia, "Harvard School").
- Galinsky, "The Anger of Aeneas", *AJP* 109 (1988) 321–348 (Perseus JSTOR index); Galinsky's critique that the school oversimplifies (Wikipedia, "Harvard School").
- Aristotle, *Nicomachean Ethics* 1125b31–32: praise for the man angry at the right things, with the right people, as, when and as long as he ought (from knowledge).
- Pöschl, *Die Dichtkunst Virgils* (1950), English *The Art of Vergil* (tr. Seligson, Michigan, 1962); "dark demon of passion", p. 15 (McDaniel, MA thesis, UGA 2006, openscholar.uga.edu).
- Otis, *Virgil: A Study in Civilized Poetry* (Oxford: Clarendon, 1963) (Wilkinson's review, *Classical Review* 15.2, 1965, Cambridge Core).
- Stahl, *Poetry Underpinning Power* (2016): "decisively pro-Augustan"; "Pious King Aeneas and his Ethically Flawed Opponents"; "King Turnus, the Impious Opponent"; readers guided to Aeneas' side (Classical Press of Wales page).
- Thomas, *Virgil and the Augustan Reception* (2001): Horace as first Augustan reader; Ovid and Lucan as readers of the oppositional material; Servius explaining difficulties away while recording *alii*; editors deleting the gates of ivory and the Cato on the shield; Dryden's 1697 translation; Wilamowitz, Haecker and Oppermann; veiled speech as a rhetorical category; the modest conclusion, "Virgil impels us to neither" (McGill, BMCR 2001.08.23).
- Kallendorf, *The Other Virgil* (2007): continuous early modern pessimistic readings across Europe and the Americas (Moul, BMCR 2008.04.02).
- Kennedy 1992 in Powell (ed.), *Roman Poetry and Propaganda*; Lyne, *Further Voices* (1987), epic voice and further voices (from knowledge).
- Servius' preface, *intentio Vergilii haec est, Homerum imitari et Augustum laudare a parentibus* (R. D. Williams, "The Purpose of the *Aeneid*", *Antichthon* 1, 1967, Cambridge Core).
- Servius on 6.893, *vult autem intellegi falsa esse omnia quae dixit* (Martínez Astorino, *RhM* 166, 2023, n. 10).
- Servius on 12.940 praises both the hesitation and the killing, a "dilemma of pietates" (Gaskin, *HSCP* 2020, academia.edu excerpt); the note's wording (*hostis parcendo pius ostenditur ... Euandri intuitu*) from knowledge.
- Lactantius, *Divine Institutes* 5.10: captives sacrificed, unresisting men and suppliants killed despite the plea by his father's shade and Iulus' hopes, called pious only because he loved his father (ANF tr., bkv.unifr.ch); the plea is *Aeneid* 10.524.
