## Findings
1. [error] [attribution] Section "The fortune and the charge of hypocrisy".
   Current: "James Romm (<em>Dying Every Day: Seneca at the Court of Nero</em>, 2014) tells the career as a long series of compromises that the writings could not redeem"
   Problem: The writer took this from memory (unsure list, item 4). The reviews describe a sympathetic, tragic reading: Seneca caught in Nero's net by good intentions and his hope of changing Nero, "more a tragic figure than a hypocrite". None of them says Romm judges that the writings could not redeem the career. The LRB review does say Romm goes further than Griffin's more cautious suggestions in showing Seneca more and more implicated.
   Fix: "James Romm (<em>Dying Every Day: Seneca at the Court of Nero</em>, 2014) tells the career as a tragedy of entrapment, in which the hope of guiding Nero drew Seneca step by step into complicity"
   Basis: Critical Inquiry review of Romm, https://criticalinquiry.uchicago.edu/shadi_bartsch_reviews_jamie_romm ("slowly entrapped by his own hopes of changing Nero for the better"; "more a tragic figure than a hypocrite"); LRB review, https://www.lrb.co.uk/v37/n12/shadi-bartsch/fratricide-matricide-and-the-philosopher ("a sympathetic Seneca, whose good intentions ... led to his entrapment"; Romm "pushes beyond the more cautious suggestions" of Griffin); Kirkus, https://www.kirkusreviews.com/book-reviews/james-romm/dying-every-day/ (leaves the verdict to the reader).

2. [error] [accuracy] Section "Quintilian's verdict and the style".
   Current: "though he was sometimes deceived by those he set to research for him; he wrote speeches, poems, letters and dialogues (128). In philosophy"
   Problem: Section 10.1.128 ends with the researchers who misled him. The list of speeches, poems, letters and dialogues opens 10.1.129, together with the verdict on his philosophy and on the sweet faults.
   Fix: "though he was sometimes deceived by those he set to research for him (128). He wrote speeches, poems, letters and dialogues; in philosophy"
   Basis: Quintilian 10.1.122–128 and 10.1.129 ff., Butler's Loeb translation on Perseids: https://cts.perseids.org/read/latinLit/phi1002/phi001/perseus-eng2/10.1.122-10.1.128 (ends "...entrusted with the task of investigating certain subjects on his behalf") and https://fgh.perseids.org/read/latinLit/phi1002/phi001/perseus-eng2/10.1.129-10.2.1 (begins "He dealt with almost every department of knowledge; for speeches, poems, letters and dialogues all circulate under his name").

3. [should-fix] [accuracy] Section "The death in 65: a Socratic performance".
   Current: "Dio's epitome gives a brief account of the death (62.25)."
   Problem: Dio's account differs from Tacitus' on points this section and its Paulina dispute box treat as settled. In Dio, Seneca wanted Paulina to die with him and opened her veins as well as his own, and the soldiers hastened his death. Dio also names Seneca outright, with the prefect Rufus, among the leaders of the plot (62.24.1), where the report says only that whether he knew more is uncertain. The epitome is cited, but its contents are left out.
   Fix: "Dio's epitome tells it otherwise: Seneca, whom it names with the prefect Rufus among the leaders of the plot (62.24.1), wished Paulina to die with him and opened her veins as well as his own, and the soldiers hastened his end (62.25.1–2)."
   Basis: Dio, Cary's Loeb on LacusCurtius, https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Roman/Texts/Cassius_Dio/62*.html, ch. 24.1 ("Seneca, however, and Rufus, the prefect, and some other prominent men formed a plot against Nero") and ch. 25.1–2 ("It was his wish to end the life of his wife Paulina at the same time with his own ... as he died hard, his end was hastened by the soldiers"). Writer's unsure list, item 2.

6. [minor] [style] Section "Tutor and minister: Agrippina, Burrus and On Clemency".

   Problem: The passage is right: chapter 12.1 has "He was incited likewise by Seneca (or so many trustworthy men have stated)". The number 61.12 is the traditional (margin) number, though. The Loeb's book divisions follow Boissevain, who starts his book 62 at traditional 61.11; LacusCurtius begins its Book 62 page at chapter 11.    Fix: "(61(62).12.1)", ordered by the house convention, if the maintainer confirms that Boissevain's book 62 starts at traditional 61.11. Otherwise leave it.
   Basis: LacusCurtius, https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Roman/Texts/Cassius_Dio/62*.html (its first chapters are 11 to 14, Agrippina's murder, before 62.1 on Boudica; footnote "See LXI (LXII).20"), and https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/e/roman/texts/cassius_dio/61*.html (ends at 61.10.6). The Loeb introduction (https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Roman/Texts/Cassius_Dio/introduction*.html) says the books follow Boissevain and that citations use the traditional numbers in the margin. Not fully checked: the book-by-book mapping was not found.
