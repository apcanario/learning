## 0.1 How we know anything: the sources
Sections: The defining problem: a past written down late · The first historians: Roman history in Greek · Cato and the Latin annalists · The raw material, and what the Romans said about it · The surviving narratives · The antiquarians: religion, custom and words · The imperial historians in outline · Material sources · Method: how far to trust the tradition · The main traps
- Keep the date of the event and the date of the source apart: everything before about 300 BC reached the page at least a century later, the kings five to eight centuries later, and the fullest accounts are the latest.
- Separate structure from narrative. Institutions, cults, treaties, monuments and names in the lists may preserve something old; speeches, motives, numbers and heroics belong to the annalists and their successors.
- Roman tradition is history-shaped and agenda-laden: ask whose family, faction or regime a story served when it was first told, and look for an outside control in an inscription, a coin, a Greek witness or a stratum.

## 0.2 Italy and the Mediterranean: the shape of Rome's world
Sections: The shape of Italy: a mental map · The site of Rome: ford, salt and hills · Latium and the Alban Hills · The regions of Italy and the Apennine spine · Why Italy, unlike Greece, could be unified by land · The islands and the sea lanes · The roads as the skeleton of conquest · Mare nostrum, the provinces and the frontiers · Myths pinned to places
- Rome is a river crossing and a market before it is anything else: the Tiber Island ford, the salt road and the Forum Boarium explain why a city grew on low, flood-prone hills between Etruria and Latium, and why its oldest myths cluster round that crossing.
- Italy's geography made unification by land possible (continuous western plains, a lengthwise Apennine spine, no inner sea), and roads and colonies made it actual; the same method, exported round the Mediterranean and halted at the Rhine, the Danube and the Euphrates, is the shape of the empire.
- Roman myths were pinned to visible places by writers centuries after the events; always ask who tied the story to the spot and when, because the topography (the Lupercal, the hut of Romulus, Juturna's spring) was curated to prove the story.

## 0.3 The timeline spine, 1000 BC to AD 476
Sections: The periods and why the boundaries fall where they do · The anchor dates: a list to copy out · Consuls and the Fasti · Years from the founding: seven ancient answers · Emperors' years and the indiction · The big chronological problems · Why 476 is a convention and 1453 is not
- Every Roman date before about 300 BC is a Varronian label, produced by counting a list back to a computed foundation; treat 753, 509 and 390 as positions in a system, and always ask who computed them and how many centuries after the event.
- The Romans dated by names, not numbers: consuls, then emperors' years and indictions. Counting "from the founding" was a historian's tool, so Roman chronology, like Greek, is built by reconciling lists.
- Period boundaries mark changes of regime or of evidence and are mostly modern; 476 is a retrospective convention and 1453 an event, and the Roman state outlived Rome's western empire by nearly a thousand years.

## 1.1 The peoples of Iron Age Italy
Sections: Before the Iron Age: Terramare and Proto-Villanovan · The Villanovan culture · Latial culture: Rome, the Alban Hills and Osteria dell'Osa · The Latins: shared cults and the League · Sabellic Italy and the other peoples · Languages and alphabets · Greeks and Phoenicians, seen from Italy · The Sabines: a first exercise in method
- Rome was one Latin community among many, sharing language, burial rites and federal cults (the Alban Mount, Aricia, Lavinium) older than its own leadership; every claim of early Roman primacy should be read against that crowded Latium.
- Iron Age Italy was multilingual and connected before it was colonised: Phoenicians, Euboeans, Sardinians and Etruscans traded through the Tyrrhenian, writing arrived as an elite import via Cumae and Etruria, and Rome's culture was hybrid from the start.
- Keep the ground, the language and the story apart, and date each witness: archaeology shows rank and period more readily than ethnicity, and ethnic labels in legend (Sabine, Trojan, Etruscan) usually explain later institutions and family claims.

## 1.2 The Etruscans
Sections: Origins: what the ancients said · Origins: Villanovans, Lemnos and ancient DNA · The twelve peoples and the Fanum Voltumnae · Metals, princes and the vase trade · The language: read aloud, barely understood · A revealed religion: the disciplina Etrusca · Tombs, banquets and women · What Rome took from Etruria · The long decline
- The Etruscans formed in Italy: the Lydian and Pelasgian stories are Greek genealogy, and archaeology and ancient DNA point to local formation. Apply the same scepticism to Rome's own imported ancestry, the Trojan claim of module 1.3.
- Almost everything Etruscan reaches us through outsiders: a language we can pronounce but barely translate, a lost literature, Greek moralists and Roman antiquarians. Judge every claim of Etruscan origin for a Roman institution on its own evidence, separating attested borrowings from ancient guesses.
- Etruscan religion was a revealed discipline of signs read by experts; Rome imported the experts rather than the theology, and haruspices served the Roman state until AD 408. Expect them wherever Livy reports prodigies.

## 1.3 Before Romulus: Evander, Hercules and Aeneas
Sections: Greek writers give Rome a past · Rival founders: Rhomos, the sons of Circe, Latinus · Saturn, Janus, Picus and Faunus · Evander's Arcadians, Hercules and Cacus · Aeneas in Greek tradition · Etruscan images and the tomb at Lavinium · The Roman version: Fabius Pictor to Varro · The Alban kings: four centuries filled · Why a Trojan and not a Greek · The claim at work: diplomacy and the Julii
- Rome's prehistory was a Greek product before it was a Roman one. For any "Roman" legend, ask which Greek first wrote it, how many centuries after the event, and what Roman writers from Fabius to Varro chose to keep.
- Rome took Greeks as precursors (Evander, Hercules) and Trojans as ancestors: kinship with the Homeric world without subordination to it, which is why the claim worked in diplomacy with Greek cities as well as against Greek kings.
- Chronology drives myth. Once the foundation was fixed in the eighth century, invented Alban kings filled the gap, and the Julii's Iulus shows genealogy staying open to political adjustment into the Augustan age.

## 1.4 Romulus and Remus
Sections: Sources: a founder written down five centuries late · Alba, the Vestal and the wolf · Recognition, augury and the death of Remus · The asylum, the Sabine women and Titus Tatius · The vanishing king and Quirinus · Variants: other founders, other parents, a wolf who was a woman · The earliest evidence · What the story is doing · What the ground shows
- The foundation story was first written down about 200 BC, five and a half centuries after its date, and never settled into one version: names, parents, hills and killers all vary. For every early Roman story, ask who first tells it, when, and what was at stake in Rome at that moment.
- Romulus is double-edged: founder, conqueror and god, but also fratricide, king and a ruler perhaps murdered by senators. Caesar and Augustus had to claim him while avoiding his fate, and from Horace to Augustine the twins stand for civil war.
- Archaeology confirms an eighth-century Palatine village with huts and a boundary wall, but it cannot reach a named person, and a rough match between pottery and a chronographers' date is no proof of a legend. Module 1.5 applies the same test to the kings.

## 1.5 The seven kings
Sections: The seven and who tells them · Romulus to Ancus: founder, priest, warrior, builder · The Tarquins and Servius in the tradition · Seven reigns for 244 years · Servius Tullius as the Etruscan Mastarna · The great Rome of the Tarquins · The Servian reforms · The nature of the kingship · How much is history: the modern positions
- Separate the narrative from the structure: the stories of the kings are late and shaped by aetiology, family pride and Greek models, but institutions, names and monuments can carry older information, and archaeology tests the structure, not the stories.
- Late sixth-century Rome was a large, Etruscanised city open to mobile warlords and their armed bands; the Tarquins, Mastarna and Porsenna belong to that world, and the Republic was born out of it rather than out of a tidy expulsion.
- Roman institutions are routinely projected back onto founders (Numa's priesthoods, Servius' centuries); before believing any "regal" institution, ask what form it took in the age of the historian who describes it.

## 1.6 The fall of the monarchy: Lucretia, Brutus and Porsenna
Sections: Tarquinius Superbus: the tyrant's portrait · Delphi, Lucretia and the oath on the dagger · The first year: Brutus' sons, Collatinus and Publicola · Porsenna of Clusium: heroes and a surrender · Aristodemus of Cumae, Aricia and Lake Regillus · The documentary evidence · Athens 510, Rome 509 · Lucretia as political myth
- The narrative of 509 is a literary construction, written about five centuries later and shaped by Greek models (Herodotus, the tyrant topos, the Dioscuri), yet a few documents (Polybius' treaty, Flavius' inscription, the Satricum stone, the Pyrgi tablets) anchor a real change around 509–504. Separate the frame from the anchors.
- The fall of the monarchy was a regional event: Porsenna, Aristodemus, the Latins and Carthage all had a hand in it, and the early Republic emerges in a Tyrrhenian world of tyrants and warlords with private followings, which the Fabii and Coriolanus of unit 3 continue.
- Lucretia and Brutus define Roman libertas as annual office, an inviolable citizen household and the state placed above family. The pattern of an outraged woman toppling a tyranny returns with Verginia in 449, and the myth was reused by Caesar's assassins, Augustine, Machiavelli and the Renaissance.

## 2.1 What Roman religion was
Sections: Religio, superstitio and the question of belief · The pax deorum, do ut des and the vow · Ritual exactness and instauratio · The state as worshipper · Prodigies and their expiation · Varro's three theologies and the gods without images · Origins: numen and the three functions · A people without myths? · One activity: religion and politics
- Roman religion is a relationship with the gods maintained by exact performance on behalf of the community: for any rite, ask who performed it, for whom, and whether it was done right, before asking what anyone believed.
- Religion and politics were one activity carried on by the same men under the same rules; every prodigy, vow and auspice in Livy or Cicero is also a political act, and manipulating the system presupposed that others took it seriously.
- Roman religion reaches us through systematisers and critics (Varro through Augustine, Livy's archaising lists) and through modern theories (numen, the three functions) that have risen and fallen; separate the attested practice from the frame laid over it.

## 2.2 The gods of Rome and their Greek doubles
Sections: Jupiter is not Zeus: names and layers · The Capitoline gods: Jupiter, Juno, Minerva · Mars, Quirinus and Janus · Hearth, seed and grain: Vesta, Saturn, Ceres · Venus, Apollo, Diana and Mercury · Neptune, Vulcan, Hercules and the Dioscuri · The system: triad, twelve and the lectisternium of 217 · Interpretatio graeca and interpretatio romana · Gods with no Greek double
- Every Roman god has two layers: an Italic cult (priest, calendar day, epithet, place) and a Greek identity (image, family, myth). Read cult evidence for function and literature for the overlay, and never assume one tells you the other.
- The equation was a dated process, not a fact of nature: some gods arrived already Greek (Apollo, Hercules, Castor), others were matched by stages through Etruria, Cumae, Sicily and the poets, and 217 is the year the state seated its twelve as Greek couples.
- Interpretatio joins and hides at once. The gods with no double (Janus, Terminus, Fides) or a bad one (Mars, Venus, Vulcan) mark what was specifically Roman, and the same habit turned outward built the double-named pantheons of the provinces.

## 2.3 Priests, augury and the calendar
Sections: Priests without a priestly caste · The king's heirs: rex sacrorum, pontifex maximus, flamines · The Vestal Virgins · The brotherhoods: Salii, Arvals, Luperci, fetials · Augury and the auspices · Haruspices in Roman service · The calendar from Romulus to Caesar · A year of festivals, and how to read Ovid
- Roman priests were the governing class in another capacity: senators holding priesthoods for life, increasingly by election, advising a Senate that decided. Religious authority and political competition were one system, which is why the emperors absorbed the priesthoods (module 6.2).
- Rome preserved ritual fossils whose meaning had gone: the rex sacrorum, the Flamen Dialis' taboos, the Salian and Arval hymns. Read the rite as old and the explanation as late, whether the explainer is Livy, Plutarch or Ovid.
- Time was administered by priests: the pontiffs controlled the calendar until Caesar replaced their discretion with a rule, and festivals kept their dates while their meanings changed, so a shepherds' Parilia could become Rome's birthday on the same day.

## 2.4 House, family and the dead
Sections: The household as a congregation · The Lares: house and crossroads · Penates, Vesta, the Genius and the Juno · Rites of passage · The aristocratic funeral and the ancestor masks · Burning, burial and the street of tombs · The Manes and the days of the dead · Ghosts · Private magic
- The household was a miniature state: the paterfamilias was its priest, the familia including slaves its congregation, and the Lares, Penates, Vesta and Genius its gods. Augustus' crossroads reform shows how easily the domestic model could be enlarged into a cult centred on the ruler.
- The Roman dead were a collective divinity, the Manes, to be fed in February and expelled in May; the aristocratic dead, through masks, eulogies and offices, were also a political resource, and Roman nobility was a claim about ancestors displayed in public.
- Magic in Rome was defined by harm and social disapproval, not by technique: the curse tablet, the Feralia spell and the amulet use the same means as religion, and the label fell on whatever was foreign, secret or aimed at others.

## 2.5 New gods: Cybele, Bacchus, Isis, Mithras and the Christians
Sections: Evocatio: calling a god out of an enemy city · Invited gods: the Sibylline route and Aesculapius · The Magna Mater, 204 BC · The Bacchanalia, 186 BC · Expulsions: astrologers, Jews and Egyptians · Isis and Serapis · Mithras: the bull, the cave and the grades · The "oriental religions" since Cumont · Jews at Rome · First encounters with the Christians
- Roman polytheism was absorptive by design: gods could be captured by evocatio, invited through the Sibylline Books or tolerated as a people's ancestral worship, and in each case the senate decided. Newness itself was never the offence.
- Repression targeted organisation, secrecy and allegiance, not belief: priests, funds and oaths outside the state's control in 186 BC, prophecy about the ruler, and finally refusal of the common cult, which Pliny turned into a sacrifice test. Watch the same logic harden into the persecutions of unit 8.
- "Oriental religions" is a modern category. Read each cult as a specific Roman construction (Roman games beside Phrygian eunuchs, an Egyptian style made in Italy, a Persian name on a Latin mystery) and keep each source's problem in view: Livy's melodrama, Apuleius' novel, Tacitus' hindsight.

## 3.1 The Struggle of the Orders
Sections: Patricians and plebeians: what the distinction was · The first secession and the plebeian state, 494–471 · Debt-bondage: nexum · The Decemvirate and the Twelve Tables, 451–449 · From the Valerio-Horatian laws to the consular tribunes, 449–367 · From the Licinio-Sextian laws to the lex Hortensia, 367–287 · The settlement: nobility, magistrates, Senate and assemblies · Livy books 2 to 10 and the Gracchan lens
- The Struggle of the Orders produced a broadened aristocracy, not a democracy: the patricio-plebeian nobility of 367–287 governed through Senate and magistracies, and the tribunate was absorbed into the state. Read Polybius' mixed constitution and the Millar debate (module 4.5 and deep dive module 11.1) against that outcome.
- The tribunate kept its revolutionary origin: sacrosanct by oath, armed with help, veto and the binding plebiscite, it lay dormant until the Gracchi used it in 133; and the annalists who wrote after 133 painted the early tribunes in Gracchan colours, so the traffic between 494 and 133 runs both ways.
- Separate skeleton from flesh: named laws, the Fasti's firsts and the Twelve Tables are early evidence; speeches, motives and the two-bloc drama were written three to five centuries after the events, through late-Republican concerns.

## 3.2 The legends of the early Republic
Sections: Coriolanus and his mother · Cincinnatus and the Fabii · Three men who aimed at kingship · Torquatus, the Decii and Curtius · The women in the stories · The exemplum as Roman myth · Families, funerals and the stage · Greek models · Valerius Maximus: the stock as a handbook
- The exemplum is the Roman form of myth: a deed dated by consuls, fixed to a place, a cognomen, a rite or a family, and judged as a model or a warning. Read every early-Republican legend as an argument about conduct, and expect one exemplum to justify opposite acts.
- These legends travelled through identifiable channels: family display (funerals, masks, coins, a Fabius as first historian), cult and topography (Fortuna Muliebris, the Lacus Curtius, razed houses), probably the stage, and Greek historiography, which supplied the shapes. Ask of each story which channel made it.
- Much of the early-Republican narrative is the late Republic arguing with itself: the would-be kings resemble the Gracchi, and Ahala, Torquatus and the Decii were cited in 63 and after. Valerius Maximus fixed the stock as a handbook of virtues, and that is the form in which later Europe received it.

## 3.3 Veii, the Gauls and Camillus
Sections: The Latin alliance and the hill peoples · Veii: the ten-year siege · What Veii did for Rome · Veii in the ground · The Gauls in Italy and the Allia · The sack, the Capitol and the gold · What the Greeks heard and what the ground shows · Rebuilding: the wall and the second founder · The Latin War and the settlement of 338
- For Rome before 300 BC the nearer witnesses (the Greek notices, Polybius, Diodorus) are the sparer ones; prodigies, rescues and saviours accumulate in the later annalists, so test each Livian set-piece against the barest version.
- Veii set the pattern of Roman expansion: conquered land became allotments, tribes and colonies for citizens, and each gain moved Rome further from partner to master of its allies.
- The settlement of 338 turned peoples into legal statuses: full citizenship, citizenship without the vote and Latin rights detached from Latium, each tied to Rome alone. That template governs Italy until the Social War.

## 3.4 The Samnite Wars and the Roman system in Italy
Sections: The sources and the Samnites · The First and Second Samnite Wars, 343–304 · Sentinum and the end of Samnite independence, 298–264 · Appius Claudius Caecus · Colonies and roads as instruments of control · The alliance system and the formula togatorum · The manipular legion · The manpower of 225 BC · Why Rome united Italy and no Greek city united Greece
- Rome conquered Italy by turning defeated enemies into allies who owed soldiers, not tribute; since the only obligation was war, the system needed war to work, and its manpower (three-fifths allied in 225) is the reserve that outlasted Pyrrhus and Hannibal.
- Colonies on confiscated land, roads that tied them to Rome, and a ladder of statuses (ally, Latin, citizen without the vote, full citizen) were instruments of control: each community had something to hope for and no common council in which to combine.
- The difference from Greece was institutional, an elastic citizenship and dependent colonies against the closed polis and the independent colony; but "unification" is partly a later story, and the allies' exclusion from citizenship is the fault line that opens in the Social War.

## 3.5 Pyrrhus and the Greek south
Sections: A Greek war in Roman memory · Tarentum and the soiled toga (282) · Heraclea and Asculum as Rome remembered them (280–279) · Cineas, the blind censor and the assembly of kings · Fabricius: gold, an elephant and a poisoner · Sicily, Beneventum and elephants at Rome (278–275) · Tarentum, Rhegium and the settlement of the south (272–264) · What Greek Italy gave Rome before 240 · Achilles against Troy, and the Greek world looks west
- The Pyrrhic war comes in two registers: battle accounts from Greek witnesses such as Hieronymus, and moral legends (Postumius, Appius, Fabricius, Curius) fixed by Roman annalists one to two centuries later. Read the first for events and the second for what second-century Romans wanted to have been.
- Rome's method in the south was to refuse terms while an invader stood in Italy, absorb defeats with manpower, and bind Greek cities by fides, ships and colonies, punishing breaches of faith even by its own soldiers; that is the posture it carried to Messana in 264.
- By 264 Rome had become a Greek subject and a Greek-equipped state: Greek coins, cults, clocks and ships at home, and Greek historians and poets abroad placing it in the Trojan scheme that Pyrrhus had tried to turn against it.

## 4.1 Carthage and Dido
Sections: Tyre and the Phoenician west · Qart-hadasht: the date of the foundation · Elissa before Virgil · Virgil's Dido and an impossible meeting · The Carthaginian state · An empire of coasts · Gods and the tophet · The treaties with Rome · Carthaginian voices · Punica fides: the Roman stereotype
- Carthage is known almost entirely through its enemies, Greek historians, Roman annalists and a comic poet; read every verdict on it (Punic faith, child sacrifice, its constitution) by asking who wrote it, when and for whom, and treat the treaties, Mago, Hanno and the inscriptions as the nearest thing to Carthaginian testimony.
- The founding queen has two layers: the Tyrian Elissa of Timaeus and Trogus, a chaste widow who dies rather than marry Iarbas, and Virgil's Dido, made Aeneas' contemporary against a chronology the ancients knew, so that the Punic wars begin in her curse.
- Rome and Carthage were treaty partners with complementary spheres for more than two centuries before 264; the wars that follow broke a long arrangement, and who broke faith was argued from the start, by Philinus against Polybius.

## 4.2 The First Punic War
Sections: Polybius' war and its partisans · Messana, 264: a hesitant Senate and a people that voted · Hiero changes sides; Agrigentum · A fleet from a wreck: Mylae and the corvus · Ecnomus and Regulus in Africa · Regulus returns to Carthage: the making of an exemplum · The sea as enemy: storms and Drepana · Hamilcar Barca and the Aegates Islands · The Mercenary War, Sardinia and Corsica, and the first province · The cost in Polybius' figures
- Rome's war aims grew with success: help for Messana in 264, all Sicily after Agrigentum (1.20.1–2), Africa in 256. A limited intervention that turns into a war for mastery is the pattern of Rome's later wars.
- Rome won by refusing to accept losses as final: it rebuilt fleets after storms that drowned tens of thousands of allied and poor rowers and paid for the last one by private loans. Polybius reads this as Roman force, ruinous at sea, and as proof that Rome's rise was not luck (1.63.9).
- The peace made the first province and the next war: Sicily taxed and later governed by praetors, Sardinia taken by force, and Hamilcar's resentment are the causes Polybius sets behind Hannibal (3.10), told by a Greek who judged between the partisans Philinus and Fabius.

## 4.3 Hannibal and the Second Punic War
Sections: Spain, the oath and the question of war guilt · Over the Alps · Ticinus, Trebia, Trasimene and the Delayer · Cannae, 216 · Rome after Cannae · Marcellus at Syracuse · Spain, New Carthage and the Metaurus · Africa, Zama and the peace of 201 · Hannibal after Zama · Hannibal in Greek and Roman hands
- Hannibal won the battles and lost the war of allegiance: Rome's power lay in its Italian alliance and its refusal to negotiate in defeat, and the colonies that held after Cannae mattered more than the legions that died there.
- The war reaches us through two frames, Polybius' analysis of causes and constitution and Livy's tragedy of virtue against a Punic antagonist; the Carthaginian account survives only as quotation, so every judgement on Hannibal is a Roman or Greek one.
- The war made the Republic's models and its means of empire: Fabian delay and Scipionic daring, commands given to private men, permanent armies overseas, and a treaty with Carthage whose terms set up the conquests of 201–133.

## 4.4 East and West: Macedon, Spain and the year 146
Sections: How the Senate went to war · Defensive imperialism or a habit of war · Provinces and governors · Publicani and the extortion court of 149 · Cato's fig and the road to the Third Punic War · 146: Carthage and Corinth · Spain: Viriathus and Numantia · Triumphs, loot, slaves and the Italian estate · Polybius' fifty-three years
- Rome's wars were decided in Rome by Senate and assembly through a procedure (demands, vote, fetial certification) that made every war formally just; whether that was defence or habitual aggression is the central modern argument, Mommsen and Holleaux against Harris, and it returns with every later war.
- Rome governed provinces with almost no administration: a governor for a year, self-governing communities and contracting companies. The extortion court of 149 admitted that governors needed policing, and courts and publicani become political battlegrounds from the Gracchi on.
- Between 167 and 133 Rome moved from arbitration to erasing cities (Carthage, Corinth, Numantia) and poured booty and slaves into Italian land; Polybius framed the process as fifty-three years extended to 146, while Roman moralists dated their decline from the same victories.

## 4.5 Polybius, Cato and the Greek invasion of Rome
Sections: Polybius: hostage, tutor, witness · Book 6: three powers in one state · Camp, funeral and the fear of the gods · Judging the analysis · Latin literature begins as translation · Comedy in Greek dress: Plautus and Terence · Greek history and Latin prose: Fabius Pictor and Cato · Philosophers in the Senate, 155 BC · The "Scipionic circle" and captive Greece
- The first systematic explanation of Rome's success was Greek: Polybius' mixed constitution and his chapters on custom. Cicero took over the frame, and the modern argument over how democratic Rome was still begins from Book 6.
- Latin literature began as translation sponsored by the Roman elite at state festivals: Greek forms carrying Roman content. Every later Roman poet works inside this double inheritance, where allusion to a Greek model is the norm.
- Roman identity was defined against Greece by Romans who were themselves Hellenised. Cato's invective, the expulsions of philosophers and the dates of moral decline are ways of managing Greek culture, not refusing it; "captive Greece" is a metaphor, not a description.

## 5.1 The Gracchi
Sections: The land question as Appian and Plutarch tell it · The modern revision: a vanishing peasantry? · Tiberius' law and the deposition of Octavius, 133 · Pergamon, a second tribunate and the killing on the Capitol · The land commission and its boundary stones · Gaius Gracchus' programme, 123–122 · The final decree and the death of Gaius, 121 · Cornelia · Heroes or demagogues · Why 133 looked like the beginning of the end
- The agrarian crisis as Appian and Plutarch tell it is Gracchan rhetoric relayed by imperial Greek writers; modern work (Rosenstein, de Ligt) makes it a crisis of rural poverty and population rather than of a vanishing peasantry. Either way the landless citizen soldier became the problem that later generals solved with land for their veterans.
- The Gracchi showed that the tribunate and the plebeian council could legislate over the Senate's head on land, money, courts and empire; the Senate answered with force, private in 133 and authorised by the final decree in 121. Every later crisis of the Republic repeats both moves.
- Whether the Gracchi were martyrs or demagogues depended on who was speaking and to whom: Cicero praised them to the people and damned them in his philosophical works. Read every source on them, and every source that starts the fall in 133, for its side.

## 5.2 Marius and Sulla
Sections: Jugurtha and Sallust's case against the nobility (118–105 BC) · Marius, the new man, and the "Marian reforms" · The Cimbri and Teutones · Saturninus and 100 BC · Livius Drusus and the Social War (91–88 BC) · Mithridates and the march on Rome (88 BC) · Marius, Cinna and the peace of Dardanus (87–84 BC) · Civil war, the Colline Gate and the proscriptions (83–81 BC) · The dictator's settlement, retirement and death · Sulla's memoirs and Plutarch
- Between 107 and 82 the decisive political fact became who held a command and whether his soldiers would follow him: the plebiscite of 107, the transfer of 88 and Sulla's march turned armies into political actors, and every later dynast started from that precedent.
- The Social War made Italy Roman in law at the cost of a war against Rome, and the fight over how the new citizens would vote drove the violence of 88–87; Sulla's colonies and proscriptions then remade the landowning class of Italy.
- Sulla's settlement, the most thorough attempt to restore senatorial government, was imposed by the first march on Rome and secured by proscription; the method outlived the system, and the record of it comes largely from the memoirs of the men who won.

## 5.3 Pompey, Crassus, Cicero and Catiline
Sections: Sulla's system under attack: Lepidus and Sertorius · Spartacus, 73–71 BC · The year 70: Pompey, Crassus and the end of Sulla's constitution · The great commands: the Gabinian and Manilian laws · Pompey's settlement of the East · Cicero's consulship and Catiline · Caesar before 60, and Clodius at the Bona Dea · Pompey's return and the alliance of 60
- The extraordinary command became the normal answer to crisis: Pompey's career from 77 to 62 was a run of commands, the greatest of them voted by the people over senatorial objection, with resources and imperium no annual magistrate held, and the dynasts of the 50s and 40s built on that model.
- Sulla's settlement failed because it could not exclude the tribunate and the people. Once the tribunes were restored in 70 the assemblies were again the road to power, and the Senate's counter, the final decree, was itself put in question by the execution of the Catilinarians.
- This period has contemporary evidence for the first time, but it is advocacy: one interested witness shaped the whole tradition of 63, and every figure, from Spartacus' army to Pompey's conquests, belongs to the writer who gives it.

## 5.4 Caesar
Sections: The consulship of 59: Bibulus watches the sky · Gaul in outline, 58–50 · Luca, Julia and Carrhae · The breakdown with Pompey and the Rubicon · Civil war: Italy, Spain, Dyrrachium, Pharsalus · Civil war: Egypt, Africa, Munda · The dictator's reforms · Honours that looked like kingship · The Ides of March in outline · Caesar as author and as legend
- Caesar's career after 59 was driven by legal exposure: laws passed against the auspices and the threat of prosecution meant he could never safely become a private citizen, and the civil war began over whether a proconsul could be forced to be one.
- Almost every number and motive in this story comes from Caesar himself or from writers using partisan witnesses (Pollio, Oppius, Cicero) generations later; always ask whose figure or verdict you are reading before you use it.
- The dictatorship joined lasting reform (calendar, colonies, an Italian Senate) to honours indistinguishable from kingship and to a clemency that made the pardoned into dependants; the conspirators removed the man, not the problem of one man's supremacy, which modules 5.5 and 6.1 take up.

## 5.5 Antony, Octavian and Cleopatra
Sections: After the Ides: the amnesty, the will and the funeral · Caesar's heir and Cicero's war: Octavian and Mutina · The triumvirate and the proscriptions · Philippi, 42 · Perusia, Brundisium and Sextus Pompey · Antony in the East: Cleopatra, Parthia and the Donations · The war of words, the will and the declaration · Actium and Alexandria, 31–30 · The poets' Actium
- Between 44 and 30 Caesar's name was the decisive political asset: Octavian's adoption, the comet, the veterans' loyalty and the rivalry with Caesarion show legitimacy passing by inheritance and army rather than by office.
- The triumvirate was a dictatorship by commission, and its instruments (private armies, proscriptions, confiscations, the oath of Italy) are what the Principate was built from; the Res Gestae rewrites each of them as service to the Republic.
- Actium as Octavian framed it, a foreign war fought by Italy and its gods against an Eastern queen, became the founding myth of the new order; read every Augustan Cleopatra and Antony against the declaration of 32 that created it.

## 5.6 Writing in a falling Republic
Sections: Writers who were also the state · Lucretius: physics against fear · Catullus and the new poets · Cicero: orator, philosopher, correspondent · Varro and Nepos: putting the past in order · Caesar: the general as stylist · Sallust: decline as a thesis · What was going wrong: the authors disagree · What this generation did to Latin
- The writers of the late Republic were participants: consuls, generals, partisans and their friends. Even a turn away from politics, Lucretius' withdrawal or Sallust's and Cicero's otium, is a position in the crisis, and should be read as one.
- They disagreed about the disease: false belief, the end of fear after 146, the want of good men, forgetting, faction. The Augustan settlement borrowed from all of them, from Caesar's language of faction in the Res Gestae to Varro's restored gods and Sallust's decline in Livy's preface.
- This generation made Latin able to carry Greek philosophy, Alexandrian poetry and serious history: Cicero's vocabulary and periodic prose, Catullus' elegiacs and Lucretius' hexameter are the instruments that Virgil, Horace, Livy and Ovid took up (module 6.3).

## 6.1 From Octavian to Augustus
Sections: From Actium to the sixth consulship, 31–28 BC · The settlement of January 27 BC · The crisis of 23 BC and the second settlement · Refusals and the father of his country · The Res Gestae: Augustus' own account · The inner circle and the problem of succession · AD 14 and Tacitus, Annals 1.2–10 · Syme and the favourable readings · Why the arrangement worked
- The Principate was a bundle of separately voted powers, provincial imperium, tribunician power and greater imperium, dressed in republican names with the army underneath; every later accession repeats the pattern, with the Senate voting the powers and the soldiers deciding who receives them.
- Augustus governed by refusing titles and keeping functions, and the Res Gestae lies by arrangement and silence rather than by statement; read every imperial claim as true word by word and test it as a whole.
- The succession was never solved: because power attached to a person, every transfer turned on adoption, the women of the family and the army's loyalty, which is the question the Julio-Claudians after AD 14 pose.

## 6.2 The city of marble and the restored religion
Sections: Brick and marble · The house on the Palatine and the temple of Apollo · The Forum of Augustus and Mars Ultor · The Campus Martius: Mausoleum, obelisk and Ara Pacis · The Prima Porta statue and the image of the princeps · The restored religion: temples, priesthoods, pontifex maximus · The Secular Games of 17 BC · Marriage, adultery and the failure of law · Genius, Lares and divus: the cult of the princeps
- Augustus argued through places: a house shared with Apollo and Vesta, a forum where Roman history ended in his chariot, and a Campus binding tomb, sun and peace. Read later imperial building as the same kind of claim.
- The religious restoration was a relocation: priesthoods, calendar, games and crossroads were renewed with the princeps at their centre, and from 12 BC the head of state religion and the head of state were one man.
- Ruler cult was graded by place and time: god in the Greek East, Roma and Augustus in the provinces, Genius and son of the deified at Rome, divus after death. Law could not achieve what images and ritual did, as the marriage laws showed in his own family.

## 6.3 Virgil, Horace, Livy and Ovid: the Augustan myth
Sections: Maecenas and the friendship of poets · Virgil before the Aeneid: the Eclogues and Georgics · The Aeneid in outline and its Augustan programme · Horace: lyric for the res publica · Livy: a history for these times · Propertius and Tibullus: love instead of war · Ovid: the calendar, the changes and Tomis · Propaganda, collaboration or something freer?
- The Augustan myth was made in words before it was quoted in stone: Aeneas' piety, the returning golden age, Actium as a foreign war and the refounder after Romulus and Camillus come from Virgil, Horace and Livy, and the Forum of Augustus and the Ara Pacis set them in stone (module 6.2).
- The characteristic Augustan device is prophecy: history told as fate from the mythic past, every line ending at Augustus. Later writers who attack the regime attack that teleology, and the cost the Augustan texts themselves record is where the attack begins.
- Patronage was friendship, not employment, and the recusatio let poets praise while declining; the space narrowed only in the reign's last decade. "Augustan" and "anti-Augustan" are labels the texts resist, so ask what each voice does within a work.

## 6.4 Empire without end
Sections: Empire without end, empire within limits · The standing army · Spain, the Alps and the Danube · Egypt, the emperor's province · The standards from Parthia · Germany: Drusus, Tiberius and Varus · Client kings and the provinces of the Senate and of Caesar · The Res Gestae's map of the world
- Augustan ideology promised empire without end while the paid army and its treasury set a finite scale; the advice of AD 14 records the moment the two met, and every later emperor who annexed or withdrew was choosing between them.
- Augustus made the army permanent, salaried and bound to the princeps, with its pensions paid from taxes on citizens; from now on whoever paid and commanded the legions and the guard held the state, which is the question the Julio-Claudians and their successors keep answering.
- Roman power was graded, not bounded: provinces of the people and of Caesar, client kingdoms waiting to be annexed, and peoples who sent embassies; the Rhine after Varus was where expansion stopped, not a line anyone had drawn in advance.

## 7.1 The Julio-Claudians
Sections: The sources reign by reign · Tiberius: accession, mutiny and Germanicus · Tiberius: Sejanus, Capri and the treason trials · Gaius Caligula: the first years and the turn · Claudius: the guard's emperor · Nero: the good years and the murder of Agrippina · Nero: Boudica, the fire and the Golden House · Nero: conspiracy, the Greek tour and the end · When the princeps is bad
- The Senate voted the powers, but the guard, the armies and the family chose the man: Claudius was made in the praetorian camp and Nero unmade by governors and the guard, and every succession after 68 turns on the same fact.
- Tacitus, Suetonius and Dio wrote from the Senate's injuries, generations later; documents (the Piso decree, the Lyon tablet, Claudius' letter), contemporaries (Velleius, Philo, Seneca) and the modern revisions (Barrett, Champlin, Winterling) show competence, popularity or calculation behind the monsters, so read every portrait for whom the emperor harmed.
- A bad princeps could be removed only by murder or rebellion, yet the empire kept being governed; the age's answers, Seneca's education of the prince, Tacitus' endurance as of bad weather and Galba's choice by adoption, frame the politics of the next century.

## 7.2 The year of four emperors and the Flavians
Sections: Galba and the secret of empire · Otho, Vitellius and the burning of the Capitol · The law on Vespasian's powers · The Jewish War and the fall of Jerusalem · Vespasian and Titus · Domitian and the senatorial memory of terror · Agricola in Britain · Rehabilitating Domitian · The Flavian poets
- The year 69 exposed the Principate's base: provincial armies chose emperors, and the Senate and people then voted the powers, as the law on Vespasian's powers did, retroactively. Every accession by force afterwards, 193 above all, replays this sequence.
- A new family could manufacture legitimacy: the Flavians built theirs from a foreign victory turned into triumph, arches, temples and an amphitheatre, from miracles and a restored Capitol, and from succession by blood sons, which failed when Domitian left no heir.
- The "bad emperor" is largely a senatorial verdict written after the fact by men who had served him: read Tacitus, Pliny and Suetonius on Domitian as evidence for the Senate's relationship with the princeps, and test it against inscriptions, coins, buildings and the provinces.

## 7.3 The adoptive emperors
Sections: Sources, and Nerva, 96–98 · Adoption as ideology and as accident · Trajan, 98–117 · Hadrian, 117–138 · Antoninus Pius and the quiet reign, 138–161 · Marcus Aurelius and Lucius Verus, 161–180 · Commodus, 180–192 · Gibbon's verdict and what it leaves out
- The "adoptive" succession was the expedient of childless emperors dressed as a principle of choosing the best; power still passed through family, marriage and the army's acceptance, and the doctrine ended the moment an emperor had a son. Later dynasts will still borrow the Antonine name to claim legitimacy.
- The second-century peace was made at the edges: conquest in Dacia, withdrawal from Mesopotamia, devastation in Judaea, and from 165 plague and invasion. The empire's greatest extent on the map (116) and its high point in reputation (Gibbon's 96–180) are different measures.
- Our picture of the century is senatorial and damaged: Dio in epitome, a Historia Augusta of uneven worth, a panegyric and Herodian. Emperors are judged by how they treated the Senate, and a silence such as Antoninus' may be a loss of evidence rather than an absence of events.

## 7.4 The Severans and the citizenship of 212
Sections: 193: the murder of Pertinax and the auction of the empire · Septimius Severus: Lepcis, the civil wars and the army · The arch, Plautianus and the death at York · Julia Domna and the Syrian women · Caracalla: fratricide, erasure and the baths · The edict of 212 · Macrinus and Elagabalus · Severus Alexander, Mamaea and the Sasanians · Papinian, Ulpian and Paul · Cassius Dio and Herodian as witnesses
- The Severans made explicit what Augustus had disguised: the emperor ruled because he paid the army. Each pay rise drove new taxes, the fiscal side of 212 and the debasement of the coinage, and the cycle of soldier emperors after 235 follows from it.
- The edict of 212 made nearly every free inhabitant a Roman citizen, ending the graded citizenship that had built Roman Italy; from now on "Roman" names a subject of the empire, while real status runs through rank, wealth and office, and local law lives on beneath.
- The dynasty's legitimacy ran through invented descent and through women, from Severus' self-adoption into the Antonines to Maesa's claim that Elagabalus was Caracalla's son; its history is told by senators who measured emperors by senatorial and Roman norms, so read the scandals of Elagabalus and the rule of women as judgements, not reports.

## 7.5 How the empire worked
Sections: The emperor at work: petition and response · Senators, knights and government without bureaucracy · The governor on the spot: Pliny in Bithynia · The cities as the government · Taxes, census and coin · The frontiers and the army in peacetime · "Romanisation" and the quarrel over the word · The Greek East under Rome · The limits of the system
- The Roman state governed through others: an emperor who answered petitions, amateur governors on short terms, and self-governing cities that collected taxes and judged small cases. When the cities' elites failed or the levers bit too hard, there was little machinery behind them.
- The army was the empire's civil service as well as its defence: soldiers guarded, escorted, policed, built and kept records, and the frontier armies were the largest market for taxes and goods. Changes in the army are therefore changes in government.
- "Romanisation" is a modern word for many local bargains; ask of each piece of evidence who adopted what, for whose benefit, and under what pressure, and remember that the Greek East became Roman while staying Greek.

## 8.1 The third-century crisis
Sections: Maximinus and the soldier emperors · Shapur I, Valerian and the rock of Naqsh-e Rustam · Decius: the order to sacrifice and the death at Abritus · The plague of Cyprian · Gallienus and the Gallic empire · Odaenathus and Zenobia of Palmyra · Aurelian: reunification, the wall and the Sun · The coin and the prices · Lost and garbled sources · Was it a crisis?
- From 235 the emperor was whoever an army could make and keep; the men who survived were Danubian officers ruling from the camp with equestrian commanders, and the late Roman state of Diocletian and Constantine is their improvisation made permanent.
- The century is told by late, thin and partisan witnesses (the Historia Augusta, Zosimus, the breviaries, Christian writers judging persecutors), so the contemporary documents, Shapur's inscription, the libelli, papyri, altars and coins, are the controls on every story.
- Decius' universal sacrifice and Aurelian's Sol show emperors seeking one religion for the whole empire, a single test of loyalty or a single supreme god; the Great Persecution and Constantine's turn answer the same demand with opposite gods.

## 8.2 Diocletian and the Tetrarchy
Sections: Sources and the rise of Diocles · Two Augusti and two Caesars · A rebuilt state: provinces, dioceses, army and court · Taxes, coinage and the Edict on Maximum Prices · Lord and god? Adoration and the "Dominate" · The ancestral gods defended: incest law and the Manichaeans · The Great Persecution · Abdication, Split and the collapse of the system
- The late Roman state was Diocletian's answer to the third-century crisis: more emperors, more provinces, more soldiers and officials, paid for by a census-based tax in kind. Its machinery of prefects, vicars, governors and indictions frames the West to 476 and the East long after.
- The Tetrarchy solved the problem of presence, an emperor on every front, but not of succession: armies were loyal to sons, and Constantine and Maxentius broke the system within eighteen months of the abdication. Watch heredity return as the principle of imperial legitimacy.
- The Great Persecution was the last and most systematic attempt to secure the gods' favour by compulsion; it was uneven because enforcement followed the map of the Tetrarchy, Galerius conceded its failure in 311, and its victims, Lactantius and Eusebius, wrote the story the Christian empire remembered.

## 8.3 Constantine
Sections: The evidence: panegyrists, a bishop and a hostile historian · From York to Rome, 306–312 · The sign: three versions of one vision · The "Edict of Milan" and what it actually was · The Arch of Constantine as evidence · Sole emperor: Licinius, Crispus and Fausta · The emperor and the bishops: Donatists, Arius, Nicaea · A new Rome on the Bosporus · The legislation · Baptism, death and the debate over the conversion
- Read every Constantinian source by its date and audience: the panegyrics of 310 and 313, Lactantius about 315 and Eusebius after 337 tell one story progressively rewritten, and the later the account, the more Christian and the more shaped it is.
- The Christian empire began as patronage and arbitration, not prohibition: money, exemptions, courts and councils for bishops, with the emperor as judge of their quarrels. The pattern of imperial councils and coercion set by the Donatists and Nicaea governs the rest of the late empire.
- Ambiguity was a method: an unnamed divinity on the arch and in the Milan letter, Sol beside the Chi-Rho, a Sunday named for the Sun, let pagans and Christians each read in their own god. Constantine's successors were forced to choose (module 8.4).

## 8.4 Julian, Theodosius and the end of the old gods
Sections: The sons of Constantine, 337–361 · Julian's restoration of the gods · Persia, 363: the death of Julian · Valens, the Goths and Adrianople · Theodosius: the Goths, Thessalonica and Ambrose · The altar of Victory, 384 · Law, the Serapeum and the Frigidus · The last Vestals and the last Games · The slow conversion of the senatorial aristocracy
- Law ended public sacrifice; society ended paganism as an elite identity. The edicts of 391–392 forbade sacrifice and temple visits, but the old religion lost the senatorial families through marriage, career and fashion over two or three generations, not by decree.
- Almost every narrative of these years is Christian and reads events as judgement: Valens burned for heresy, the wind at the Frigidus, the penance at Thessalonica. Ammianus, a pagan officer who saw much of what he describes and still criticised his hero, is the control to test them against for the years he covers, to 378.
- After Adrianople and the treaty of 382 an undefeated Gothic people lived and fought inside the empire, and after Thessalonica a bishop could discipline an emperor. Both precedents shape the West of module 8.5 and the relations of emperors and popes long after.

## 8.5 The fall of the West, 395 to 476
Sections: Two courts and a general, 395–408 · Alaric and the sack of Rome, 408–410 · Augustine's reply: the City of God · The Rhine crossing and the lost provinces · Aetius and Attila, 425–454 · Gaiseric: Africa and the sack of 455 · Ricimer and the last emperors, 455–476 · Gibbon and Pirenne: why and when · Transformation, and the return of fall
- The West fell through its revenues: each lost province, above all Africa in 439, shrank the taxes that paid the army, until the government depended on barbarian armies and on generals who made and unmade emperors. Follow the money and the army, not the battles.
- The "invasions" were mostly settlements negotiated with armies already inside the empire, often drawn in by Roman civil wars; Alaric, Athaulf and Odoacer wanted a place in the Roman system, not its destruction, and the successor kingdoms inherited Roman land, law and taxpayers.
- "Fall" and "transformation" answer different questions: the western state and its economy did fall, unevenly and over generations, while religion, law and Roman identity carried on, and the eastern empire did not fall at all. Ask of any claim which region, which class and which evidence.

## 8.6 The Rome that did not fall
Sections: Why the East survived · Justinian: reconquest, plague, law and the Great Church · Persians and Arabs: the seventh-century amputation · Rhomaioi: Romans who spoke Greek · Dynasties and the Macedonian height · Charlemagne and a second Roman empire · Manzikert, the Crusades and 1204 · The Palaiologan revival and the last fragments · The afterlives of the Roman title
- The Roman state did not end in 476: it continued at Constantinople with Roman law, the Roman name and an unbroken imperial succession until 1453. The East's survival is the control case for every explanation of the western fall.
- Medieval Rome survived by money, walls and diplomacy more than by conquest: a tax base, an impregnable capital and the habit of buying, dividing and redirecting enemies. Each time the tax base was lost (the seventh century, after Manzikert, after 1204), the empire shrank to what its capital could defend.
- From 800 the Roman title had rival claimants, each by a different right: papal coronation in the West, conquest of the capital for the Ottomans, Orthodox faith and dynastic marriage for Moscow. Ask of any later "Rome" which of these rights it claims.

## 9.1 Roman law
Sections: Ius, fas and mos · The Twelve Tables and the pontiffs · The praetor's edict and the formula · The jurists · Persons, things and actions · Paternal power, slavery and manumission · Citizenship and its grades · Criminal law and its penalties · From the jurists to the codes · Bologna and the civil-law world
- Roman law grew less by statute than by interpretation and remedy: pontiffs, praetors and jurists made new law out of old forms and annual edicts, so the first question about any rule is who had the power to interpret it.
- Law was the grammar of status: freedom, citizenship and family defined the person, and rank fixed the penalty. Devices such as adoption, the triple sale, the Junian Latins and the grades of Latin right were instruments of social policy.
- Roman law survived as text, not as institution: what reached later Europe was what Theodosius' and Justinian's commissioners chose and altered, read at Bologna and recast in the modern civil codes, so every "Roman" rule has passed through a compiler's hands.

## 9.2 The army
Sections: The citizen militia: census, levy and oath · The manipular legion and its discipline · Marius, the cohort and the end of the militia · The standing army of the emperors · The soldier's life: tablets and tombstones · Equipment and engineering · The triumph · The late army: field and frontier · The army as political actor
- Rome's army changed by degrees from an annual citizen militia graded by the census into a long-service professional force paid by the state; whoever paid, settled and commanded the soldiers held their loyalty, which is why the army became a political actor from Sulla to the soldier emperors.
- The army and citizenship were bound together: service was a citizen's duty under the Republic and auxiliary service the road to citizenship under the emperors; after 212 the late army drew on soldiers' sons, conscripted peasants and barbarians, so changes in who served are changes in who counted as Roman.
- Normative texts (Polybius' manual, Vegetius' lost ancient legion, Valerius Maximus' rules of the triumph, Trajan's Column) describe an ideal; documents and finds (Vindolanda, papyri, diplomas, tombstones, Inchtuthil, Corbridge) show practice, and each should be read against the other.

## 9.3 Family, women and slaves
Sections: Familia and paterfamilias · Marriage, manus, dowry and divorce · Age at marriage and the shape of the family · Women: law, practice and the Laudatio Turiae · Childhood, nurses and schools · Slavery: numbers, sources and work · Manumission and the freedmen · How the study of the Roman family changed
- Roman family law describes powers, not lives: patria potestas, manus and guardianship were extreme on paper and limited by custom, by emperors and above all by death, so every legal rule must be read against who was alive to exercise it.
- Marriage without manus, easy divorce and the returnable dowry gave women of property a practical independence the law never declared, while office stayed closed to them; women's power ran through fathers, husbands and sons, and under the empire through nearness to the emperor.
- Slavery was a structure of the Roman household and economy, and manumission its outlet: it made freed slaves citizens and their sons freeborn, which kept the system stable after the war captives of the late Republic and made freedmen and their descendants a large, visible part of the urban population.

## 9.4 Bread, baths and games
Sections: How many people · Domus and insula · Water: the aqueducts and Frontinus · Bread: the annona and the dole · The Roman day: salutation, baths, dinner · The arena: gladiators, beasts and the condemned · Circus and stage · "Bread and circuses" · Pompeii and Herculaneum: the town-level check
- The city of Rome lived on the empire: its numbers, water and grain depended on provincial surplus, migration and a growing administration (curator of the waters, prefect of the grain supply), so a supply crisis in the capital was always a political crisis.
- The capital's privileges, free grain and imperial shows, were citizen privileges and public displays of rank and imperial generosity, not poor relief or simple mob control; read "bread and circuses" as elite satire, not as description.
- Most literary evidence for daily life is satirical or moralising and about the capital (Juvenal, Martial, Seneca); test it against inscriptions, graffiti and the excavated towns, which both confirm the picture and correct it.

## 9.5 Reading a site: Rome on foot
Sections: The Capitoline · The Forum, west end · The Forum, east end and the Sacred Way · The Palatine · The Imperial Fora, Trajan's Markets and the Column · The Colosseum and the Arch of Constantine · The Circus Maximus, the Forum Boarium and Sant'Omobono · The Campus Martius · The Baths of Caracalla and the Palazzo Massimo · Walking orders
- Rome is a stack, not a set: on almost every site what stands is the last rebuilding (Diocletian's Curia, a late antique Saturn, Tiberius' Castor, Hadrian's Pantheon, Valadier's arch), so the first question at any ruin is which layer you are looking at.
- Monuments are claims before they are evidence: the Fasti's Romulus, the column's hill, Titus' Jerusalem, Agrippa's name and Constantine's divinity say what someone wanted said, and erasures such as Geta's line or the Vestal of 364 are part of the message.
- Names on the ground are hypotheses with histories: Tabularium, House of Augustus, House of Livia, Trajan's Markets, Lupercal and tomb of Romulus rest on an inscription, a pipe or an excavator's reading, and the city you walk was arranged by Michelangelo, Ricci and Mussolini as much as by emperors.

## 10.1 Ovid and the Roman remaking of Greek myth
Sections: A Roman poem that taught Europe Greek myth · From Chaos to Caesar's star · The Roman frame: a heavenly Senate and a new star · Invented and transformed: five stories · The lost Hellenistic shelf · The Roman names, and why they stuck · The Fasti: the Roman year as a poem · The Heroides: myth in the heroine's voice · Afterlives: from the Ovide moralisé to Bernini
- Read the Metamorphoses as a Roman universal history: Greek in matter, its frame runs to Rome, and in the Roman books metamorphosis becomes apotheosis, ending with Caesar's star and the poet's own claim to outlast Jupiter's anger.
- Ovid changed frames, voices and textures more than plots, and his Latin names carried the result into Europe's schools; where Antoninus Liberalis allows a test, the plot is Hellenistic and the frame, voice and texture are Ovid's.
- The Fasti and the Metamorphoses are a pair, Roman myth in elegiac couplets by the calendar and Greek myth in hexameter by time; the Fasti is half a year, retouched in exile for the dynasty that banished its author, and its explanations are poetry before they are evidence.

## 10.2 Imperial literature, Seneca to Apuleius
Sections: Why drop "Silver Latin" · Seneca's tragedies · Lucan: the anti-epic of civil war · Petronius' Satyricon and Trimalchio's dinner · Satire and epigram: Persius, Juvenal, Martial · The Flavian epic: Valerius, Statius, Silius · The Plinys and Quintilian · Tacitus and Suetonius as writers · Apuleius: the one complete Latin novel · Coda: late Latin and the bridge to the Middle Ages
- Imperial literature is a conversation with the Augustan classics, not a decline from them: Lucan, Valerius, Statius and Silius answer the Aeneid, the satirists rework Lucilius and Horace, and Petronius parodies epic and the critics of his own day.
- Under a princeps writing works by indirection: praise that may be irony, attacks kept for the dead, civil war displaced into myth, and history that judges by arrangement. Ask of each text what its author could safely say, and how he said the rest.
- The forms this period made, the comic novel, the epigram with a sting, biography by categories, the encyclopaedia and Quintilian's canon, together with the Christian Latin of Jerome and Augustine, are what the Middle Ages and the Renaissance inherited alongside Virgil.

## 10.3 Reading Roman art and architecture
Sections: The inheritance: an Etruscan plan and a Greek grammar · The republican portrait: verism and the ancestors · Greek statues in Roman settings: what "Roman copy" means · Reading historical relief · Painted walls and mosaic floors · Concrete, arch and dome · The imperial portrait and its erasure · Sarcophagi: myth for the dead · Late antique style: change, not decline
- Roman art speaks in borrowed parts recombined for Roman ends: an Etruscan plan with Greek orders, a Greek body with a Roman head, a Greek style chosen for what it says, verism for the republican noble and classicism for Augustus. Ask of every work what its manner claims.
- Images of rulers are ritual and type, not reportage: the relief shows the emperor in a fixed role, the portrait in a fixed type, and both were recut or erased when power changed, so read them as statements by whoever paid for them.
- Concrete turned architecture into the shaping of interior space, from the Golden House to the Pantheon and the vaulted basilica, and late antique style turned the image from illusion into sign; both are changes of aim, not failures of skill, and both lead to the church, the apse mosaic and Hagia Sophia.

## 10.4 Reception: how Rome reached you
Sections: Rome without an emperor: popes, Franks and a forged gift · The city of marvels and Dante's providential empire · Petrarch, Cola di Rienzo and the measured ruins · Machiavelli and the republican tradition · Shakespeare's Roman plays · 1776: Gibbon's fall and the founders' republic · Brutus in Paris, eagles at the Tuileries · Spades, scholars and the Duce · Rome on screen and on the page · Who owns Rome?
- After 476 Rome was an idea open to any claimant, and each claimant chose a different Rome: the papacy a Christian Constantine, emperors a renewed empire, communes and republics the Senate and Brutus, nation-states the legions and the arches. Ask of any use of Rome which Rome it chooses, and against whom.
- Reception works through intermediaries and inventions: Plutarch reached Shakespeare through North, Livy reached the Atlantic world through Machiavelli, the papacy's Constantine through a legend and a forgery, and Gibbon's fall through Montesquieu. Forgery and the criticism that exposes it, from the Donation to Valla, belong to the same tradition.
- Republic and empire have alternated as models: the Republic for revolutions (Florence, Philadelphia, Paris in 1789), the empire for consolidations (Charlemagne, Napoleon, Mussolini). The present quarrel over who owns Rome repeats the old pattern of claiming it to legitimise.
