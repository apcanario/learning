01The household floor, and what a survivable floor leaves out

The floor. The household floor is what has to be paid every month whatever happens: housing, food, utilities, transport, insurance, debt payments, and the bills that arrive once or twice a year spread across twelve months. It is read from the last twelve months of bank statements: a budget records intentions, and statements record what left the account. If the household spends everything it takes home, the floor is take-home pay. If it saves, the floor is take-home less saving.

Whose floor. If you share costs with someone, the model needs two numbers: what the household as a whole must pay, and what your salary pays of it today. The second is the one an exit removes.

The survivable floor (this course's rule; no source sets it).

F_s = outgoings − C − V_min

  • C is the spending the household has agreed, in writing, to stop if your salary stops. Written, because a cut that has only been imagined is the first thing to fail in a bad month.
  • V_min is the lowest single month, over the last twelve, of any other income that pays your share of the household's costs: a partner's variable pay, rent from a room, a pension. The lowest month is used because an average hides the month that breaks the budget; a runway sized on the average runs out in that month. If no other income pays your share, V_min is 0.

What a survivable floor leaves out. F_s is what your salary covers today, net of tax. Six things sit outside it, and each has to be answered before a date means anything:

  1. Tax and contributions on the income that would replace the salary. Salary arrives after tax; income from an account or from clients arrives before it. §4 turns this into one input.
  2. What your employer pays that never reaches your take-home. Pension contributions, sick pay, insurance, and in the US often health cover. The US Department of Labor's guide to COBRA lists termination of employment "for any reason other than gross misconduct" and a reduction in hours among the events that can let a worker keep the employer's health plan, for 18 months as a rule, at a premium that "cannot exceed 102 percent of the plan's total cost" (US Department of Labor, A Worker's Guide to Health Benefits Under COBRA, undated, read 14 Nov 2026; regulator guidance). The page does not say whether a resignation qualifies. The question for every reader: what does my employer pay for that I would have to buy, and at what price?
  3. Unemployment benefit after a resignation. Leaving to build an income is a resignation, and in the three places this unit checked it is treated as leaving by choice. US: state unemployment insurance requires you to be "unemployed through no fault of your own (determined under State law)", and a separation for a reason other than "lack of work" is decided by the state (US Department of Labor, State Unemployment Insurance Benefits, last updated 11 Sep 2026; regulator guidance). UK: Universal Credit applies a high-level sanction if you "leave a job or lose pay by choice while claiming Universal Credit or just before you claim, without a good reason", 91 days for a first one in a year (GOV.UK, Universal Credit sanctions, updated 6 Apr 2026; regulator guidance). EU: benefits are national, so the source is your national social security institute; Mara's is Portugal's, whose guide requires that the claimant has become unemployed "por razões alheias à sua vontade (desemprego involuntário)" and treats a worker's own resignation as voluntary (Instituto da Segurança Social, Guia prático: subsídio de desemprego, 6001 v4.72, 20 Feb 2025, pp. 6 and 14, read 14 Nov 2026; official guidance). So the model counts no benefit in any month after an exit.
  4. The account's own costs. Platform fees, professional fees, a postal address. This unit nets them out of account income (§3) so that the floor holds household costs only.
  5. Saving for later life, and replacing what wears out. A floor that pays this month's bills and nothing towards later years is survivable for a while and costly afterwards. The model does not price it; the template asks you to say whether your floor includes it.
  6. The other earner's own risk. V_min is read from the last twelve months. If the other income could stop, the floor that matters is the one with V_min at 0.

Mara's floor (Mara, fictional). She lives with her partner in a rented flat; they split shared costs equally, and each pays their own share from their own pay. Her take-home pay from the job averages €2,150 a month across the year. Her outgoings, read from twelve months of statements, are €1,700 a month, including €120 a month set aside for annual bills. She and her partner agreed in writing on €200 a month of cuts if her salary stops (meals out, holiday travel, a cheaper phone plan). Her partner's pay covers the partner's own share and none of hers, so her model counts none of it: V_min = 0 (her rule). Her survivable floor is €1,700 − €200 − €0 = €1,500 a month, and she saves €2,150 − €1,700 = €450 a month today (derived).

02Runway: the guidance that exists, and its grade

What runway is for. Runway is the number of months of survivable floor saved and held apart before an exit. It has to cover the months between leaving and the point at which income from the new work pays the floor, plus the delay before you can see that it does. If income never arrives, it is the time you have to return to a job.

What the guidance says. No dataset was found that measures how long a person who leaves a job to build creator income takes to replace a salary, on either platform; PB1 §10 lists "who continues among part-time creators with full-time jobs" among what no dataset measures, and DD-PB1 found no figure for channel income at the date any of its six creators left a job (DD-PB1 §3 to §8, §11). What exists is consumer guidance on emergency savings, written for an unplanned loss of income:

Source What it says Grade
UK: Money and Pensions Service, MoneyHelper "have three to six months' essential outgoings available in an instant access savings account" (MoneyHelper, Emergency savings: how much is enough?, undated, read 14 Nov 2026) Consumer guidance, government-backed; no dataset; no mention of self-employment or variable income
US: Washington State Department of Financial Institutions "Many experts suggest saving 3 to 6 months of basic living costs" (WA DFI, Building an Emergency Savings Fund, undated, read 14 Nov 2026) Consumer guidance, a state regulator reporting unnamed experts; no dataset
Portugal (Mara's country): Caixa Geral de Depósitos, a state-owned bank an emergency fund of "pelo menos, seis meses de despesas" (at least six months of expenses), and under current uncertainty "pelo menos, um ano de despesas" (at least a year) (CGD, Saldo Positivo, Fundo de emergência, 9 Apr 2026, read 14 Nov 2026) Consumer guidance, a bank; no dataset; no mention of independent work or variable income

This unit opened no EU-level guidance. The grade of all three is the same: advice for emergencies, from bodies that publish no evidence for the number, and none written for a planned resignation. Three facts from the sources above push a planned exit towards the longer end. None of the three places checked pays unemployment benefit after a resignation as a matter of course (§1): the US leaves the decision to the state, the UK applies a sanction, 91 days for a first one, where there was no good reason, and Portugal treats a worker's own resignation as voluntary. The income lines with a sourced timeline at your size are few (§3). And the evidence on whether a line is working arrives only at checkpoints: PB10's review reads income once a quarter, and PB9 §8 shows why a single month of sales says almost nothing at small numbers. A runway shorter than two quarterly reads is spent before the second read arrives.

The runway rule is yours. Pick a length R in months, write down why, and test the shorter and longer values in the sensitivity (§6). The arithmetic is:

  • runway needed = R × F_s
  • months to save it = (R × F_s − S0) ÷ s

where S0 is what you have saved and can hold apart for this, and s is what you save each month now. C does not enter s: the cuts are agreed for after an exit, unless you start them now.

Mara's runway (Mara, fictional). Her rule: 12 months of F_s, with 6 months as the sensitivity (her rule; basis: the guidance above, at its longest; no dataset). On 5 July 2027 she holds €9,000 for this. Twelve months is 12 × €1,500 = €18,000; the €9,000 she lacks takes €9,000 ÷ €450 = 20 months of saving from July 2027, complete at the end of February 2029, first read at the checkpoint of Monday 2 April 2029 (derived; her checkpoints, D1 i14). Six months is €9,000, already saved.

04Tax: an input the model does not compute

Your floor is net of tax because your salary is. Any income that replaces it arrives gross, and some share of it goes to income tax and social contributions. That share depends on where you are taxed, how the income is classed, what else you earn in the same year, whether you are still employed, and what you can set against it; this course cannot know any of those for you, and states no rate. The model therefore carries tax as one input, t: the share of each unit of new income that goes to tax and contributions in your case. The threshold is

T = F_s ÷ (1 − t)

and until t is filled, the model runs at t = 0 and labels every date it prints as earlier than the true one.

What to ask, and of whom. Ask a tax professional where you are taxed, in writing, with the date:

  1. What share t of account income (by line: sales, platform payouts, brand payments) and of contract income goes to income tax and contributions, at two levels: your current account income, and the threshold T.
  2. Whether t differs while you are employed and after you leave, and from which date the after-leaving figure applies.
  3. When those amounts are paid (in arrears, in advance, quarterly), so the runway holds them.
  4. What registration the income needs before the first payment (PB8 register T1), and whether a platform that acts as merchant of record changes anything for you (PB9 §5).

The sources to open before that conversation are the ones PB8 §6.5 gives for the EU (Your Europe, Income taxes abroad FAQ, which sends you to your national authority), the UK (HMRC, Tax Help for Hustles, Tax rules for content creators) and the US (IRS, Topic 554, Self-employment tax). Unemployment and health cover after leaving (§1) go to the same list, because they change what the floor must buy.

Mara (Mara, fictional). She is taxed in Portugal. Her T1 question (registering the guide's income) was answered on Thursday 18 March 2027 by a tax professional she knows, without charge (PB10 D3). Her t is a separate question: she asks the same professional for t at her current income and at T, under Portuguese rules, by the twelve-month review on Monday 3 January 2028; the fee, if any, is an unfilled input, as is a lawyer's fee for a first brand contract (PB9 D2 V18); the postal address is priced from her receipt (PB10 D3, R8). Until then her model runs at t = 0.

05Reduced hours, a sabbatical, contract work: questions

Each of these keeps part of what a job gives, so each changes the arithmetic. None has a sourced answer for a creator; each is a set of questions, and the deliverable models one of them as a path only to show how its arithmetic differs.

The one study found on leaving in stages. Raffiee and Feng followed the US National Longitudinal Survey of Youth 1979 cohort from 1994 to 2008 (2,198 spells of full-time self-employment by 1,093 people) and report that entering self-employment after a period of running it beside a job went with "a 33.3% decrease in the hazard of exit" from self-employment, against entering directly from paid employment (Raffiee and Feng, "Should I quit my day job? A hybrid path to entrepreneurship", Academy of Management Journal 57(4), 2014, 936–963, author's copy, read 14 Nov 2026). Peer-reviewed; observational. The authors attribute the difference to learning, which they did not measure directly, and acknowledge the rival explanation that people who choose the staged route "systematically select better ideas ex ante", which they test with a shared frailty model and judge "unlikely". The sample is US self-employment of every kind from one national cohort; it contains no measure of creator income, and what it measures is whether the self-employment continued, with nothing on the household's finances. It describes who lasted; it says nothing about your date.

Reduced hours.

  • Can you ask, and must the answer be considered? EU: under the framework agreement on part-time work, employers should "fully consider" requests "to transfer from full-time work to any part-time work that becomes available", and a worker's refusal to transfer from full-time to part-time work or back "is not sufficient reason" for dismissal (EUR-Lex summary of Directive 97/81/EC, last updated 4 Dec 2016; primary law, summarised); your national law implements it. UK: since 6 April 2024 an employee can make a statutory flexible-working request from the first day of employment (The Flexible Working (Amendment) Regulations 2023, S.I. 2023/1328; primary law). US: your employer's policy and your state's law; this unit did not research US rules on requesting fewer hours.
  • What does the payroll show as take-home at the reduced hours? Use that figure; a pro-rata figure is only a placeholder.
  • What happens to benefits that follow hours: pension contributions, insurance, and in the US health cover (COBRA lists a reduction in hours as a qualifying event, §1)?
  • What will the freed hours do? No dataset measures what extra hours buy a one-person account (PB1 §10). If they go to the account, PB3 D2's 10-hour plan is the template, and PB1's posting-rate rows and stop rule are re-read on the day of the change (PB3 D2).

A sabbatical (unpaid leave with the job held).

  • Does your contract, a policy it points to, or your national law offer unpaid leave, for how long, and is the job held in writing? This unit opened no EU, UK or US source on unpaid leave; the sources to open are your contract and your country's employment law.
  • What stops during it: pension contributions, insurance, health cover?
  • The arithmetic is runway only: months of leave × F_s, saved before it starts.
  • What can it show? A leave of a quarter or two gives one or two quarterly reads (PB10), which at small numbers is a direction at most (PB10 §3; PB9 §8). Decide before it starts which count, read at which checkpoint, would make you extend, return or resign.

Contract work in your field.

  • Does your employment contract allow paid outside work, and does any clause apply after you leave? PB8 §6.4's checklist, items 1, 2 and 6, read before any paid work.
  • What rate, how many hours, and how soon after leaving? No source meeting this course's standard gives a time to a first contract or a rate for a person leaving a job; the model enters contract income only from signed contracts.
  • Is it taxed differently from account income? A separate t (§4).
  • Where do its hours come from? PB9 §7's rule applies: from a named cut, never the account's cadence.

The two that move the date most are the two Mara has only her own counts for: income per audience member (how much a subscriber is worth to her in a month) and audience pace (how many subscribers she adds a month). They enter the date in the same way: the subscribers she needs are T divided by her income per subscriber, and the months to get there are that gap divided by her pace. A factor of two in either moves the date by seven years or more; her own two readings of income per subscriber (launch month against trailing year) differ fourteenfold, and her two readings of pace (Q1 against Q2) by 1.65 times. Tax comes third, and is the one input she has not filled. The floor and the exchange rate move the date by two years or less (no agreed cuts, 2.0 years; the August 2026 high, 0.8 years).

On the paths where the runway binds (D5), the date moves with two different inputs: the runway length R and the monthly saving s. For Mara, 6 months in place of 12 removes the runway's bound altogether, since she has already saved six months; €100 a month of extra saving brings her 12-month runway forward by 3.6 months (derived: €9,000 ÷ €550 = 16.4 months against 20).

What the sensitivity means for any reader: the date your model prints is mostly a statement about two numbers you will measure, and its first decimal is noise. Read the two inputs first; the date follows from them.

D2 · The thresholds, line by line

  1. Outgoings: €1,700 a month.
  2. Survivable floor: F_s = €1,700 − €200 − €0 = €1,500 a month.
  3. Income threshold before tax: T = F_s ÷ (1 − t) = €1,500 ÷ (1 − 0) = €1,500 a month, €18,000 a year; in dollars €1,500 × 1.1052 = USD 1,657.80 a month, USD 19,893.60 a year. Any value of t multiplies T by 1 ÷ (1 − t): at t = 10%, USD 1,842.00 a month (illustrative value of the input).
  4. Runway: 12 × €1,500 = €18,000 (6 months: €9,000).
  5. Months to runway: (€18,000 − €9,000) ÷ €450 = 20 months, from July 2027 to the end of February 2029; first read Monday 2 April 2029. At 6 months: already saved.
  6. The rule. An exit can be considered at the first checkpoint at which (a) the path's income test has passed at that checkpoint and the one before, (b) the runway is saved, and (c) the preconditions (i18) are done. Until all three hold, the state is the present one: employed, building, read next quarter. The model decides nothing beyond that.

What has to be true.

  1. Income grows in step with subscribers, at her launch month's rate per subscriber, for fifteen years: an assumption no source supports (PB9 §5 finds no conversion rate for a creator's own product).
  2. She adds about 12.7 subscribers a month, every month: above the band median of +0.5 a month for channels of her size, 1 to 99 subscribers, and the +1.4 of the next band, 100 to 499 (PB1 D1 Y6; vendor), and constant where the evidence shows percentage growth falling with size (PB1 §6).
  3. The fortnightly cadence holds for fifteen years inside 6 hours a week, a span no dataset follows (PB1 §2, §10).
  4. Her trailing income reaches T at two checkpoints running, and t, J1, J2 and T1 are settled by the second.

D4 · Path B · Full exit: contract accounting work carries the floor with the account

Arithmetic.

  1. Income test: K (net of its own t) + account income ≥ T = USD 1,657.80 (€1,500) a month over the trailing twelve months at two checkpoints running. At the account's Q2 run rate of USD 45.66 a month (€41.31), K must supply about €1,459 a month (derived, t = 0).
  2. K is unfilled: no source gives a rate or a time to a first contract (§5). It enters from signed contracts only.
  3. Runway: 20 months, complete at the end of February 2029, first read Monday 2 April 2029 (D2 line 5). At a 6-month runway: already saved.

Earliest honest date: none from the evidence. The only dated bound is the runway's, Monday 2 April 2029, which falls after the 24-month review in the week of 1 January 2029. If her employment contract allows paid outside work (PB8 §6.4, items 1 and 2) and she could find hours for it, two quarters of contract receipts before leaving would let the income test be read; if it does not, K can be counted only after an exit, and the pass rule cannot be met in advance. Whether any hours exist for it is itself a question: her 6 hours a week are the account's (PB1 D2), and PB9 §7 takes no hours from the cadence.

What has to be true. Her contract permits outside paid work, or she gives the notice it requires and keeps the reply (PB8 J1); signed recurring contracts cover about €1,459 a month for two quarters; her professional has given t for contract income; the 12-month runway is saved.

D5 · Path C · Reduced hours: the job stays, at four days

Arithmetic.

  1. Take-home at 80%: €2,150 × 0.80 = €1,720 (her planning value; payroll replaces it).
  2. Floor test against her full outgoings: €1,720 ≥ €1,700, a margin of €20 a month, with no account income and no cuts. Against the survivable floor, the test holds at any take-home down to €1,500, which is 69.8% of her present pay (derived: 1,500 ÷ 2,150).
  3. Saving falls from €450 to €20 a month. Path B's 12-month runway then takes (€18,000 − €9,000) ÷ €20 = 450 months: Path C, taken first, removes Path B's date unless account or other income fills the gap.
  4. No new runway is needed while the job pays the floor; her €9,000 stays held.

Earliest honest date: Monday 3 January 2028, the model's first full reading, if her employer's written answer and the payroll figure are in by then; otherwise the first checkpoint after both arrive. The arithmetic does not bind on Path C: the questions in §5 do. Whether her employer agrees, what happens to her pension contributions and any insurance at four days, and what the freed day buys the account are all unfilled. If the day goes to the account, PB3 D2's 10-hour plan applies and PB1's posting-rate rows and stop rule are re-read that day.

What has to be true. Her employer agrees in writing; the payroll figure at four days is at least €1,500 (or €1,700 if she keeps her present spending); the loss of €430 a month of saving is accepted, with Path B's runway then dated by other income.

D6 · The three dates together, and the prior from DD-PB1

Read together.

  • On a full exit, no path has an income date the evidence supports. Path A's date is fifteen years out on her most favourable counts, and its two inputs (income per subscriber, pace) are her own, a quarter old. Path B's only date is the runway's.
  • Path C is the only path whose arithmetic passes now, and it is the only one that keeps the job. Its open items are her employer's answer and her payroll's figure.
  • DD-PB1 gave her an untested prior: "Keep the job through year two" (DD-PB1, Deliverable A). This model's dates agree with it on both full exits: at her 12-month runway neither can pass before Monday 2 April 2029, after year two, and Path B at a 6-month runway has no income date at all. Path C differs: it changes the job inside year two, from January 2028, while keeping it. The prior was drawn from six survivors (DD-PB1 §11) and the model's dates from her own counts; where they differ, the model's arithmetic is the one she reads, and the prior is a reason to look twice.

  • Every quarterly review: one line in PB10's money block, net account income over the trailing twelve months against 12 × T, written beside R1 (about a minute; her estimate).
  • Every annual review: the whole model, with every input replaced by her counts: the twelve-month review on Monday 3 January 2028, then each January. 0.5 hours (her estimate), one Reel fewer in the cut window of the fortnight after the review (Sunday 30 January 2028 for year two) (her rule).
  • First full reading: Monday 3 January 2028, with t from her tax professional, her Q3 and Q4 counts in place of i10 to i12, and her employer's answer on four days if she has asked for it.

The writer's opening note

Search was off for this unit. It was written from PB1 to PB10 and DD-PB1, which it cites by unit and section, and it searched only to verify six points, each opened on 14 November 2026: the euro reference rate for the US dollar, three pieces of consumer guidance on emergency savings, the unemployment rules after a resignation in the US, the UK and Portugal, the US rule on continuing health cover, the UK and EU rules on asking for fewer hours, and one peer-reviewed study on leaving a job in stages. The evidence labels are the ones PB1 §1 defines (platform-official, vendor, correlational, survivor sample, peer-reviewed, anecdote or documented creator case, derived) and the two PB8 added (primary law, regulator guidance); this unit adds one, consumer guidance, for advice published by a public body or a bank without a dataset behind it. Amounts stay in their source's currency, with their date. This unit is information, not legal, tax or financial advice.

The unit computes; it does not recommend. Its question is the earliest date on which the evidence would let an exit be considered, path by path, and what has to be true for that date to hold. Whether to leave stays with you. Every path in the deliverable starts from the job you have and returns to it whenever a test fails.

The running example is Mara Venn (Mara, fictional): 38, a full-time accountant who restores mid-century furniture at weekends and teaches it as One Box Restoration, one 10-minute video a fortnight since Tuesday 5 January 2027 and six Instagram posts a fortnight, in 6 hours a week, living in Portugal (PB1, PB4, PB8). Her numbers are illustrations; every benchmark she is set against is sourced; nothing she does is evidence about anyone else. Her latest counts are those of her Q2 review on Monday 5 July 2027 (PB10, D4), and the model below is built on them.
