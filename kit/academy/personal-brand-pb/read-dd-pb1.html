01How the six were chosen

The criteria. A creator entered only if all of these held, on pages opened for this unit:

  1. Evidence. A start date; at least one dated audience number from the first three years, or a dated number close enough to read against a 12-, 24- or 36-month point; and at least one statement in the creator's own words.
  2. Spread, as the brief sets it. At least two Instagram-first; at least two who built beside a full-time job; at least two outside the United States; no one who was a minor when they started.
  3. Fit. One person on camera, teaching or showing a skill or practice, with an audience built on YouTube, Instagram or both.

02How each history is read

PB1 §9's six-point checklist is applied to every creator, in a short block at the end of each section: (1) the denominator, (2) cadence and hours against yours, (3) the start (an audience elsewhere, a job that gives material or credibility, a thin niche), (4) numbers kept apart from the creator's advice, (5) the date, since platform rules and formats change, (6) the sample's exclusions.

Months are counted from a stated start, named in each section. Where no number sits at 12, 24 or 36 months, the nearest dated number is given with its month count (my arithmetic, from the dates shown), and the point itself is marked unpublished. No number is interpolated.

PB6's and PB7's rules are read only where outside evidence can reach them. Of PB6's rules, R3 (titles carry the words a viewer would type), R4 (one topic and format per season), R7 (hold the date; no catch-up video), R8 (no Shorts in year one), R10 (do not chase an hour of the day) and R11 (keep every video public) can in principle be checked from public pages; R1, R2, R5, R6 and R9 need the creator's own YouTube Studio and are not treated as checkable. Of PB7's rules, IR1 (Reels for strangers, carousels for followers), IR2 (original content only), IR5 (caption first line and at most five hashtags), IR6 (three feed posts a week), IR7 (send Instagram viewers to YouTube) and IR9 (collabs) can be checked from outside an account; IR3, IR4, IR8, IR10, IR11 and IR12 need private Insights. "Can be checked" means a page could show it; where no page opened here does, the reading says "not documented". §9 sets the readings side by side.

03Ali Abdaal

Start. His first upload was a guitar cover on 4 January 2017, and his medical content began on 20 June 2017, filmed on his phone during a placement in Cambodia (Noah Kagan, 25 Jan 2022; the author's account of an interview). His own site says "I started my YouTube channel in 2017 while I was at university" (aliabdaal.com/about). Months below count from June 2017.

Platform mix. YouTube first; an email list Kagan puts at "over 130,000" in January 2022 and lists, among the tactics that worked, as a backup in case the platform changes (Kagan); a cohort course whose first cohort ran in November 2020 (aliabdaal.com, ?p=222); a book, Feel-Good Productivity, in 2023 (about page).

Cadence. Kagan reports one to three videos a week since 2017 and "He's never skipped a week" (author's account). In September 2020 Abdaal wrote: "In my last three months of working full-time as a doctor, I was cranking out three YouTube videos a week", and since leaving, "down to only one or two videos a week" (Every, Superorganizers, 24 Sep 2020; own words). vidIQ counts 1,513 videos, and 5 long-form videos and 26 Shorts in the 28 days to its data date (vidIQ, data 11 Nov 2026; tracker).

Hours a week. No figure was re-verified. A podcast's show notes that give one could not be opened (the page redirected, and is not checked); the figure stays out. By September 2020 he assigned videos to "either me or someone on my team" (Every), so any later hours figure is his share of a team's output.

12, 24 and 36 months.

  • 12 months (June 2018): unpublished. Nearest: a video of 24 August 2018 celebrates 50,000 subscribers, about 14 months in, and in it he says he started the channel "a year ago" and calls himself "a junior doctor working in Cambridge" (video transcript on Rosetta, 24 Aug 2018; own words). His 100,000-subscriber Q&A is dated 8 December 2018, about 18 months in (Rosetta, 8 Dec 2018); his site says "I hit 100,000 subscribers on YouTube in 2018" (?p=222).
  • 24 months (June 2019): unpublished.
  • 36 months (June 2020): unpublished. One million came in September 2020, about 39 months in (?p=222: "1 million subscribers in September 2020").
  • After: 2 million in July 2021, 3 million in June 2022, 4 million in April 2023 (?p=222); 6.71 million on 11 November 2026, after gains of 10,000 to 30,000 a month from November 2025 to September 2026 and none in October 2026 (vidIQ).

Breakout piece. Not re-verified. The source that dates it is the podcast page that redirected; nothing here names it.

What he stopped. Medicine. Both versions of his site give two years as a junior doctor in the NHS (?p=222; about page). The current page says "In August 2020, I took a break from Medicine, intending to go to Australia to do a year's placement in Emergency Medicine", that the pandemic closed Australia's borders, and that in 2021 he "made the decision to officially leave Medicine" (about page); Kagan writes that he quit his day job at 1 million subscribers, which his site dates to September 2020 (?p=222). The work stopped in August 2020, about 38 months in (my arithmetic); the decision to leave is dated 2021.

How he earns. In September 2020 he called sponsorships "a vitally important part of our revenue" (Every). Courses from November 2020 and a book from 2023 (his site). His site says the business has "grown to over 20 employees" (about page). No income figure was re-verified at its source.

What he says he got wrong. "I thought I'd be way more productive than I've been so far", of his sabbatical after leaving full-time medicine (Every, 24 Sep 2020).

Read against PB1 §9. (1) No denominator: no source counts the medical students who started channels in 2017. (2) One to three videos a week while working full time puts him in the 52-plus band of PB1 §2's 360-day table, twice the rate of this course's running example or more (Mara, fictional: one video every two weeks, 26 a year, PB1's planning assumption); hours unpublished. (3) The start: a narrow audience of medical applicants at first (Kagan's first tactic: start very niche, with videos on an admissions exam, then broaden), and a job that supplied both material and credibility. (4) Kagan's list of tactics is advice; the dated milestones are the evidence. (5) Shorts did not exist in his first three years: YouTube first launched them in India in September 2020 and in a US beta on 18 March 2021 (Tubefilter, 18 Mar 2021). (6) Milestones are the ones he chose to announce.

04Christian Pascual (Very Normal)

This is one of PB1 §9's two first-year cases, carried here to 24 and 36 months.

Start. "I uploaded my first video on April 9th, 2023" (Very Normal, Issue 41, after 4 Jun 2025; own account; the page carries no post date, and its latest date is 4 June 2025). Months count from April 2023.

Platform mix. YouTube, with a newsletter on Substack, "a newsletter for the Very Normal YouTube Channel" (verynormal.substack.com); a Ko-fi page for support (Issue 41).

Cadence. In his first year, 36 videos totalling 311 minutes (Issue 14, 9 Apr 2024; own account). A weekly schedule became "an awful experience"; he moved to uploading when a video was ready, which takes "about two weeks" (Issue 14). His about page: "I post roughly every two weeks" (about).

Hours a week. Unpublished. "Balancing it with my research is an ongoing struggle" (Issue 14). He was "not going to give up my Ph.D just to get explosive growth on the channel" (Issue 25, 11 Sep 2024).

12, 24 and 36 months.

  • 12 months (9 April 2024): 29,385 subscribers (Issue 14). He reached the Partner Programme 212 days in, on 7 November 2023 (Issue 14).
  • 24 months (April 2025): unpublished. Nearest: 100,000 subscribers on 4 June 2025, "it took 787 days" (Issue 41), about 26 months in; he had set that goal at the start of 2025, "to reach 100K before I graduate" (Issue 41).
  • 36 months (April 2026): unpublished. No tracker page for the channel was returned by the searches run, and his newsletter posts opened give no later count.

Breakout piece. Not named as one. In May 2024 his most-viewed video was "The most important skill in statistics" and his best earner "The better way to do statistics" (Issue 18, 17 May 2024); no view counts given.

What he stopped. The weekly schedule (Issue 14). By mid-2025 he wrote of adjusting "to a new life in industry" after graduating (Issue 41): the channel did not replace the job.

How he earns. Advertising after 7 November 2023; some affiliate links, of which he writes "I may get a small amount of money" (Issue 14); Ko-fi (Issue 41). His explainer videos, after their first two weeks, "hover at around 1-2 cents a day" in ad revenue, with a maximum of "$1.20 in a single day" (Issue 18; own account).

What he says he got wrong. "Maybe this has hurt my potential channel growth", of giving up strict consistency in uploads (Issue 14); later, "I don't think the weekly pace helped, but rather a more mindful approach to the thumbnail and title" (Issue 41). His content "has ended up confusing a lot of people" (Issue 14).

Read against PB1 §9. (1) No denominator. (2) 36 videos in a year sits in PB1 §2's 12–51 band, the same band as Mara's 26, where 8.65% of channels crossed 1,000 subscribers within 360 days (vidIQ, 11 Sep 2026, as PB1 cites it; vendor data, correlational); his rate was higher than hers and his hours are unpublished. (3) A specialist topic (statistics) explained by a trained statistician; the PhD gave material and credibility. (4) His view that thumbnails and titles mattered more than pace is his reading; nothing tested it. (5) He started in April 2023, before the 1 February 2027 change in Partner Programme thresholds that PB1 §4 sets out. (6) He published because the year went well; PB1 §9 says the same.

05Mike Boyd

Start. vidIQ dates the channel to 23 October 2008 (vidIQ, data 13 Nov 2026; tracker); an undated, unsigned profile says it lay dormant until 2015 (yespress.io, undated). The format began with a kickflip: a MetaFilter post of 11 November 2015 says "It took him five hours and forty-seven minutes" (MetaFilter, 11 Nov 2015). Months count from November 2015.

Platform mix. YouTube, with a second channel, Mike Boyd Climbs, launched "three years ago" (so about 2023, my arithmetic); on TikTok: "I struggle to put stuff on TikTok. I don't think it's a good thing to be on" (Net Influencer, 4 Mar 2026; his words).

Cadence. No schedule found. 197 videos by 13 November 2026 (vidIQ) is about 18 a year since November 2015 (my arithmetic; it overstates the rate a little if any video predates 2015).

Hours a week. Unpublished. Kim, his wife, describes the start: "When we realized we could pay both of us, we both quit our jobs and went all in"; now "We have an editor and occasionally hire other cameramen" (Net Influencer; her words). No date is given for either.

12, 24 and 36 months. All three unpublished. In his words: "There wasn't a grind to 10,000 subs. The first one just hit the nail on the head" (Net Influencer). Latest: 2.93 million subscribers, 477,016,889 views and 197 videos on 13 November 2026, with net losses of 10,000 in December 2025, April 2026 and September 2026 and no change in the other months of the year (vidIQ).

Breakout piece. The kickflip: "people resonated with the first video I put up" (Net Influencer). His most-viewed long-form videos on vidIQ date from December 2021 to November 2022, led by "Can I make money counting cards?" (23 Feb 2022, 3.89 million views).

What he stopped. Growth past what two people could run: "Deciding not to become a huge production company is what has made it sustainable" (Kim, Net Influencer). Projects that drag: "I always say mood over work" (Kim).

How he earns. Brand partnerships through an agent: "Get a good agent who brings you third parties that are worth trusting", and "Stay away from gambling, alcohol" (Net Influencer; his words). No income figure found.

What he says he got wrong. No statement about the channel found. Of the business side: "I'm really bad at that stuff" (Net Influencer).

Read against PB1 §9. (1) No denominator. (2) About 18 videos a year sits in PB1 §2's 12–51 band, below Mara's 26; hours unpublished, and two people worked full time from an undated point. (3) Two engineering degrees and a master's thesis in progress at the start (yespress.io, undated); no audience elsewhere is documented. (4) "The first one just hit the nail on the head" is his account; no page shows the first video's numbers. (5) The start predates Shorts (Tubefilter, 18 Mar 2021). (6) Only his announced statements exist; his early counts were never published.

06Cam Anderson (Blacktail Studio)

The nearest subject to this course's running example: one person making furniture, building on Instagram first, while holding a full-time job.

Start. He began on Instagram, where "my wife showed me what a hashtag was", and on YouTube in 2016 (Tubefilter, 3 Jun 2022; his words). vidIQ dates the channel to 19 September 2016 (vidIQ, data 13 Nov 2026; tracker). One podcast page says he has posted "since 2018" (The Woodworking Talk Show, 6 Aug 2021), which disagrees with both. Instagram's start date was not found. Months count from September 2016.

The job. A LifeFlight pilot on seven days on, seven days off, which left him, in Tubefilter's words, "so much downtime"; he quit in March 2020 (Tubefilter), about 42 months after the channel's creation date (my arithmetic). In February 2019 he still worked as an emergency services flight pilot while running Blacktail Studio, which the show notes call "a one-man woodworking company based out of Portland, Oregon" (The Woodpreneur Podcast, ep. 83, 27 Feb 2019).

Platform mix. Instagram, then YouTube; Etsy for early sales; by 2022 courses and plans for physical and digital products (Tubefilter). His early YouTube videos drew "the thousands of views" because of his Instagram following (Tubefilter; his words).

Cadence. About one video a month in 2022, with a wish to move to every two to three weeks, and Shorts under test (Tubefilter). 147 videos by 13 November 2026 (vidIQ), about 14 a year since September 2016 (my arithmetic).

Hours a week. Unpublished.

12, 24 and 36 months.

  • 12 months (September 2017): unpublished. The show notes say his first year was slow and that starting an Instagram account, at his wife's suggestion, is what took the business off (Woodpreneur, 2019).
  • 24 months (September 2018): unpublished. He dates his first sponsorship offers, of which he said "They weren't very good offers", to about 30,000 to 40,000 subscribers in 2018 to 2019 (Tubefilter; his recollection).
  • About 29 months: 93,400 Instagram followers on 27 February 2019 (Woodpreneur show notes: "the account now at 93.4k followers").
  • 36 months (September 2019): unpublished.
  • After: about one million YouTube subscribers by June 2022 (Tubefilter); 3.46 million on 13 November 2026, with no net change in the 28 days to that date and none in four of the last twelve months (vidIQ).

Breakout piece. His fifth or sixth video, an epoxy table tutorial: "that one got a couple million views pretty quick", taking the channel to about 20,000 subscribers (Tubefilter; his words; undated).

What he stopped. The pilot job (March 2020). Barnwood, "a big part of the whole 'ugly end products' thing" (Tubefilter). Commissions that do not fit the business, since he is "regularly turning down $20K commissioned projects that don't fit his business model", and most sponsorship offers (The Tilt, 3 Jul 2023). Growth into a firm: "I never wanted to be a big furniture company with a bunch of employees"; he hired a videographer instead (Tubefilter).

How he earns. Furniture sold for "thousands, or even tens of thousands" of dollars; sponsors including Home Depot; courses; "I never spent any money I didn't make from furniture" (Tubefilter, 2022). A blog post in his name gives the advertising revenue of five videos, from $5,486 to $70,533 each (5 Terrible Builds That Paid Me $131,708, 9 Nov 2023; own account).

What he says he got wrong. His first table: "I was an idiot so I sold it on Etsy for like $350 and didn't charge shipping"; shipping cost $150 (Tubefilter). Sponsorships: he "was apparently pricing myself way too low" (Tubefilter).

Read against PB1 §9. (1) No denominator. (2) About 14 videos a year (12–51 band), below Mara's rate; his hours are unpublished, and a seven-on, seven-off rota is a different week from a five-day office job. (3) He sold furniture before the channel grew; the channel showed a product with a buyer. (4) His view that giving away his methods brought more sales and followers (Tubefilter) is his reading. (5) He started on Instagram years before Reels, which Instagram announced on 5 August 2020 (Instagram, 5 Aug 2020). (6) His numbers before 2019 exist only as his recollection.

09Against PB6's and PB7's rules

Each cell reads only what the sources in §3 to §8 document. "Consistent" means the documented practice matches the rule; "departs" means it does not; "n/a" means the rule's object did not exist for that creator at that time, or the rule is for a platform no source here shows the creator building on (Instagram for Abdaal, Pascual and Boyd; YouTube for Itsines); "not documented" means no source opened here shows it. The rules are those PB6 and PB7 set for Mara's account; a creator who departs from one was not trying to follow it.

PB6 (YouTube).

  • R4, one topic and format per season. Consistent for Boyd (one procedure since 2015, climbing moved to a second channel, §5), Anderson (furniture built start to finish, §6) and Pascual (two video types inside one subject, Issue 18). Abdaal departs: he started narrow and broadened, by Kagan's account (§3). Anecdote; no case shows what the format did to returning viewers.
  • R7, hold the date, no catch-up video. Abdaal's "never skipped a week" (Kagan) is consistent with the rate R7 protects. Pascual departs in his own account: he dropped a fixed weekly date and now uploads when a video is ready (Issue 14), and later judged that the weekly pace had not helped (Issue 41). Upload dates are not documented for the others.
  • R8, no Shorts in year one. Not applicable to Abdaal, Boyd, Anderson or Wicks, whose first years predate Shorts (Tubefilter, 18 Mar 2021); Pascual's year-one use is not documented. Abdaal's 26 Shorts in the 28 days to 11 November 2026 (vidIQ) and Anderson's Shorts tests in 2022 (Tubefilter) are later practice and do not test a year-one rule.
  • R11, keep every video public. Boyd's and Anderson's most-viewed videos date from 2021 to 2024 and still carry their views on 13 November 2026 (vidIQ), consistent with a back catalogue left up; deletions are not documented for anyone.
  • R3 and R10. Not documented. Top titles such as "$4000 Block of Wood" (vidIQ) are curiosity titles, but whether search or recommendations brought their viewers needs the private traffic-source report; publish times appear in no source.

PB7 (Instagram).

  • IR1, Reels and carousels. Not applicable: Itsines (2012), Wicks (2014) and Anderson (by 2019) built their Instagram audiences before Reels were announced (Instagram, 5 Aug 2020).
  • IR2, original content. Wicks filmed his own recipes and Anderson his own pieces. Itsines posted her followers' progress photos (Yahoo, 2015): pictures of other people, which is a question for PB8's consent clauses before it is one for IR2.
  • IR5, caption and hashtags. Wicks built on one hashtag, #leanin15 (Campaign); Anderson learned hashtags from his wife (Tubefilter). Counts per post are not documented.
  • IR6, three feed posts a week. Both trainers departed far upward: Itsines a post every three or four hours (USA Today, 2015), Wicks three a day (Campaign, 2017). PB7's evidence for IR6 is a within-account vendor association (Buffer, 13 Aug 2025, as PB7 cites it); two cases neither confirm nor refute it.
  • IR7, send Instagram viewers to YouTube. Anderson is the one documented case: his early videos reached "the thousands of views" because of his Instagram following (Tubefilter; his account, no numbers for the transfer).
  • IR9, collabs. Not documented for any of the six.

10What a sample of survivors cannot show

  • The rate. Six successes say that each path was possible. None says how often it works. PB1 §2's base rates stay the only rates in this course: 8.65% of channels adding 12 to 51 videos crossed 1,000 subscribers within 360 days (vidIQ, 11 Sep 2026, as PB1 cites it; vendor data, correlational).
  • The cause. Every creator above offers an account of why it worked. Pascual says titles and thumbnails mattered more than pace; Kagan credits Abdaal's niche and email list; Anderson credits Instagram. Each is the creator's or writer's reading of one history with no comparison case.
  • The early numbers. Of 18 points (six creators at 12, 24 and 36 months), one is a dated count at its point: Pascual's 29,385 at 12 months. The rest are unpublished; where a dated number sits near a point, it falls 2 to 5 months away (§3 to §8). Creators publish milestones when they reach them, so the gaps sit exactly where the slow months were.
  • The hours. No source opened gives weekly hours for any of the six. Nothing here shows an account grown on 6 hours a week by one person, and nothing shows that it cannot be.
  • The platform as it is now. Five of the six started before Reels (5 Aug 2020) and Shorts (US beta 18 Mar 2021); only Pascual (April 2023) started after both. Their growth happened under ranking systems that PB6 and PB7 do not describe, and their numbers cannot be set against PB1's 2025–2026 datasets as if the conditions matched.

The trackers do show the late stage: on 13 November 2026 Boyd and Anderson had no net subscriber change in the latest 28 days, Boyd had lost 10,000 in each of three months of the last twelve, and Abdaal, after gains of 10,000 to 30,000 in each month from November 2025 to September 2026, added none in October 2026 (vidIQ, data 11 Nov 2026 for Abdaal and 13 Nov 2026 for Boyd and Anderson).
