01Cost per task

A price list gives the cost of one token. What you pay for is a finished job. The cost of a task is the input tokens times the input price, plus the output tokens times the output price, summed over every turn the task takes, with the history re-sent on each turn (BW1 §2). The price list fixes one factor. The other, how many tokens the model spends to finish, is set by the model and by the effort setting (§3), and it varies more than the price does.

Two parts of the output are easy to miss.

  • Thinking is output. On Claude, the tokens the model spends thinking are billed as output tokens, and in full even when the app shows only a summary of the thinking; thinking blocks kept from earlier turns are billed again as input (Anthropic, Steering thinking, read 15 Nov 2026). OpenAI documents the same for its reasoning tokens, which are hidden, count toward the context window and are billed as output (OpenAI, Reasoning models, read 15 Nov 2026), and Google bills a Gemini response as its output plus its thinking tokens, for the full thoughts even when only a summary is returned (Google, Gemini thinking, last updated 14 Oct 2026, read 15 Nov 2026).
  • Output costs five times input on every current Claude model: $4 and $20 per million tokens for Opus 5.5, for example (Anthropic, Models overview, read 15 Nov 2026). A model that thinks at length pays the dear rate on every token of it.

What the measured comparison shows. Artificial Analysis, an independent benchmarking company, runs one set of tasks across models and reports a cost per task: the weighted-average cost in US dollars to complete one task of its Intelligence Index, computed from the tokens each model consumed and its price, which is the first-party API rate or the median across providers (Artificial Analysis, Artificial Analysis Benchmarking Methodology, read 15 Nov 2026). On its leaderboard on 15 November 2026, under Intelligence Index v5.0.1 (Claude Opus 5.5: Intelligence, Performance & Price Analysis, read 15 Nov 2026, for the version), each model at its highest listed effort (Artificial Analysis, LLM Leaderboard, read 15 Nov 2026):

Three readings, all arithmetic on the table (grade: measured, by a third party, on its tasks):

  1. Same token price, different task price. Sonnet 5.5 and GPT-6.1 Sol both cost $2 and $10 per million tokens and both score 55 at max. Sonnet's task costs $10.02, Sol's $1.27: 7.9 times as much for the same score. The difference is tokens spent per task.
  2. A cheaper token can make a dearer task. GPT-6 Astra's tokens cost more than Fable 5.2's ($10 / $50 against $8 / $40), and its task costs less ($6.24 against $26.54). Fable 5.2 scores 8 points higher.
  3. Price ratios understate the gap at the top. Fable 5.2's tokens cost twice Opus 5.5's; its task at max costs 2.6 times as much.

A Claude Code engineer says the same in practice terms: larger models cost more per token, yet on tasks that stretch a smaller model the total cost per task can come out lower, and some tasks a smaller model cannot finish at any effort (Lydia Hallie, Choosing a Claude model and effort level in Claude Code, Claude blog, 7 Jul 2026; grade: documented practice). Anthropic's own launch figures point the same way and are vendor data: at default (medium) effort, Opus 5.5 beat Opus 5 at max effort on Terminal-Bench 4.0 for about a fifth of the cost, and beat GPT-6 Astra at max effort on GDPval-AA for about a fifth of the cost per task (Anthropic, Introducing Claude Opus 5.5, 22 Sep 2026, read 15 Nov 2026).

None of these tasks is your task. What transfers is the mechanism: the bill is set by tokens spent per finished job, and the model and the effort setting both move that number. One cost no table holds is your own time. A cheap output that needs a second run, or an error you have to find, is paid in hours, and nothing measured that for the reader of this unit. The routing table at the end handles it with override triggers.

The case. Academy (Academy case), a library of self-study courses built by one person with Claude in October and November 2026, is this course's running example; it is one project, an anecdote, never evidence about AI tools in general. Its record holds no measure of hours or money spent per unit (Academy case file §9). So the case cannot say what any of its tasks cost; this unit's routing of it rests on the evidence above, and the deliverable says where the case file records a model and where it does not.

What each vendor says the rungs are for (vendor statements):

  • Anthropic describes Haiku 5.5 as for high-volume, latency-sensitive tasks such as classification, extraction and routing; Sonnet 5.5 as the best combination of speed and intelligence; Opus 5.5 as for long-running agentic coding and knowledge work; and Fable 5.2 as for demanding reasoning and long-horizon agentic work.   • OpenAI's reasoning guide says, for cost, to consider a smaller model: start with its largest and try a mid model for lower cost or its smallest for the lowest cost and latency (OpenAI, Reasoning models, read 15 Nov 2026). The guide names those models gpt-5.6-terra and gpt-5.6-luna, while the pricing page lists gpt-6.1-terra and gpt-6-luna: on the day, OpenAI's own pages did not agree on the names.
  • Google sets Gemini 4 Argon as its flagship reasoning model for complex software engineering, deep maths and science and autonomous multi-step agents (Gemini 4 Argon, read 15 Nov 2026).

Three things make the ladder a better unit of decision than any model name.

  1. Names change; rungs stay. On 15 November 2026 Anthropic's lineup lists Fable 5.2 at the top at $8 / $40, with Fable 5.1, at $10 / $50, moved to the list of all models (Models overview); Anthropic's page on the new model says its input and output cost less than Fable 5.1's (Anthropic, What's new in Claude Fable 5.2, read 15 Nov 2026). A routing rule written as "large tier, high effort" survives that change; one written as "Fable 5.1" does not. In Academy, a session's model named "fable" resolved to a newer version than the method names, and the checks it ran were recorded as owed again on the named version (Academy case file §7): the name moved under the rule.
  2. The rungs are priced alike across vendors. Haiku 5.5 and GPT-6 Luna cost the same per token, as do Sonnet 5.5 and GPT-6.1 Sol (the tables above). The cost per task does not line up the same way (§1), which is why each rung needs its own evidence.
  3. Anthropic now sells two large rungs. Opus 5.5 and Fable 5.2 are both large, and the vendor's own advice is to start on the cheaper one. The brief of this course names three tiers; on Anthropic's lineup the top tier has a step inside it.

Where the tier pattern holds less well. The routing evidence (§4) shows a small model is often enough, and the measured data (§1) shows a mid model at max effort costing as much per task as a large one. A tier is a starting point for a task. The override triggers in the deliverable move a task up or down.

03What effort and thinking settings do

Every vendor in this unit now sells two dials: which model, and how hard it works. This section is from the vendors' documentation (grade: documented practice) and one benchmark.

Claude. Anthropic's documentation says effort controls how many tokens Claude uses when responding, and states: "Effort is a behavioral signal, not a strict token budget." At lower levels Claude still thinks on hard problems, but less, and it can skip thinking entirely on simple ones. Effort applies to every output token: the text, the tool calls and the thinking. Lower effort makes fewer tool calls and terser confirmations; higher effort may make more calls and explain its plan. The levels are low, medium, high, xhigh and max; xhigh is described as for long-running agentic and coding tasks of over 30 minutes, max as capability with no constraint on token spending (Anthropic, Effort, read 15 Nov 2026).

Defaults differ by model, and the differences matter when a model is swapped (same page, read 15 Nov 2026):

  • Opus 5.5 and Haiku 5.5 default to medium; Fable 5.2 and Sonnet 5.5 to high on the API.
  • Opus 5 and earlier Opus models defaulted to high, so the same request with no effort set runs one level lower on Opus 5.5.
  • On Opus 5.5, thinking is always on and cannot be turned off; effort is the lever.
  • Per model: for Fable 5.2, start at high, where most of the capability gain already is, and keep max for measuring the ceiling; for Sonnet 5.5, start at high unless the work is agentic or latency-sensitive, at medium for well-specified agentic coding, and at medium or low for chat; for Haiku 5.5, use low for chat, short tool tasks and simple high-volume requests, high for knowledge work, longer agent tasks and strict instruction following. At low, the page warns, Haiku in a long agent prompt is more likely to skip a search, stop early or skip a check.

On claude.ai the effort setting is inside the model menu next to the send button. The help centre describes low and medium as suiting routine tasks and stretching usage further, high as the best overall balance of quality and speed, extra high as for long-running coding and agentic tasks (Opus 4.7 and newer), and max as the most thorough; higher effort uses more tokens and reaches usage limits sooner. On Fable 5.2, Haiku 5.5, Sonnet 5.5, Opus 5.5, Fable 5.1 and Opus 5, thinking cannot be turned off in the app; to reduce it, lower the effort. The menu marks each model's default without the article listing them (Claude Help Center, Change the model, effort, and thinking settings, read 15 Nov 2026).

OpenAI and Google. OpenAI's effort values depend on the model and can run from none through max; GPT-6.1 Sol defaults to medium and supports neither none nor minimal. Lower effort favours speed and fewer tokens, higher effort reasons more completely, and the guide advises starting at medium, evaluating medium against high before using high or xhigh, and treating effort as a tuning knob, since clear prompts and explicit output contracts matter more (OpenAI, Reasoning models, read 15 Nov 2026). Google's Gemini 4 Argon takes low, medium and high thinking levels, defaults to medium and cannot have thinking turned off; Google's page assigns little or no thinking to fact retrieval and classification and a high level to complex maths and coding, and says to lower the level to cut cost or latency (Gemini thinking, read 15 Nov 2026).

What a step of effort buys, measured. Artificial Analysis reports Opus 5.5 and Sonnet 5.5 at each effort level, with the index score and the time per task; on 15 November it gave a cost per task only at max (index v5.0.1; third-party benchmark):

Effort Opus 5.5: index · minutes per task Sonnet 5.5: index · minutes per task
low 35 · 3.1 26 · 2.6
medium 48 · 6.9 31 · 4.1
high 52 · 9.4 42 · 6.9
xhigh 57 · 15.8 50 · 14.5
max 59 · 25.4 55 · 33.3

Sources: Claude Opus 5.5: Intelligence, Performance & Price Analysis and Claude Sonnet 5.5: Intelligence, Performance & Price Analysis, both read 15 Nov 2026.

Readings, arithmetic on the table:

  • On Opus 5.5, low to medium buys 13 points for 2.2 times the time; medium to high 4 points for 36% more; high to xhigh 5 points for 1.7 times; xhigh to max 2 points for 1.6 times.
  • Sonnet 5.5 at high and Opus 5.5 at medium take the same 6.9 minutes a task; Opus scores 6 points higher.
  • Fable 5.2 at its lowest level scores 54, above Opus 5.5 at high (52), on the same site's Fable page (Claude Fable 5.2: Intelligence, Performance & Price Analysis, read 15 Nov 2026), which gave no cost per task below max.

Time per task is not cost. Opus 5.5's output speed on that page is 104 to 114 tokens a second at every level, so the longer times suggest more tokens spent; that reading is this unit's inference, and the page does not state it.

Extra thinking on easy problems is waste, measured. Xingyu Chen and colleagues found long reasoning models spending compute on simple problems for little gain, and cut that overhead while keeping performance on GSM8K, MATH500, GPQA and AIME (Chen et al., Do NOT Think That Much for 2+3=? On the Overthinking of Long Reasoning Models, ICML 2025, PMLR 267; the abstract does not name the models). Anthropic's thinking page says the same in product terms: to get less thinking, lower the effort before trying to prompt the model out of it (Steering thinking).

Model or effort: the diagnostic. Lydia Hallie puts the two dials apart: "The model setting is roughly how capable; the effort setting is roughly how thorough." If Claude had the right context and clearly tried and still failed, choose a larger model; if it skipped a file, did not run tests or did not double-check, raise effort. She frames this as a starting heuristic, and recommends the model's default effort for most tasks, treating effort as a standing preference more than a per-task choice (Hallie, Claude blog, 7 Jul 2026; grade: documented practice, written before the 5.5 models).

Keep effort fixed inside one piece of work. On the API, changing the top-level effort between requests can invalidate all or part of the prompt cache; models that support per-message effort keep it (Effort, read 15 Nov 2026). The claude.ai help article says changes apply from the next response and says nothing about caching (Change the model, effort, and thinking settings). Setting both dials before the first message costs nothing and avoids the question.

04Where quality differences show, and where they do not

Read a benchmark by who ran it, which version and when

Every benchmark number in this unit comes with three facts attached, and each of them changes what the number means.

  • Who ran it. A vendor's launch table is the vendor running its model against rivals it chose, under settings it chose. Anthropic's Opus 5.5 table runs Opus 5.5 at max effort except on Terminal-Bench 4.0, where it was run at xhigh; tasks its safeguards stopped were finished by older models, which Anthropic says likely lowers the scores; one test (AutomationBench) was run by Zapier; and the same page gives Opus 5.5 57.8% on CursorBench 4.0 in the table and 52.5% at default effort in the text (Introducing Claude Opus 5.5, read 15 Nov 2026). Each of those is stated on the page; none of them is in the headline numbers.
  • Which version. Artificial Analysis moved to Intelligence Index v5 on 27 October 2026: it dropped GDPval-AA, SciCode and AA-LCR, added a private coding test and a set of terminal-based science tasks, and now rests about 60% of the index on private test sets or held-out answers (Artificial Analysis, Announcing Artificial Analysis Intelligence Index v5, 27 Oct 2026, read 15 Nov 2026). On 15 November its pages show v5.0.1. A score under v4 and a score under v5 measure different tasks.
  • When. The index above changed version on 27 October 2026, so a leaderboard read before that day and one read after it rank models on different tasks. In Academy's second audit (anecdote), ten claims in one unit were stale, all from one cause: an independent benchmark published a new index version the day before the unit read its leaderboard (Academy case file §4).

A benchmark that the vendor ran, on a version that has since changed, on a date you do not know, is a claim about someone else's tasks under someone else's settings. Use it to rank options, never to predict your own result.

Where the differences show

Small against mid, on long or open work. Anthropic's Haiku 5.5 launch sets Haiku 5.5 beside Sonnet 5.5 as a reference: GDPval-AA v2.1, a knowledge-work test, 1620 against 1840; Terminal-Bench 4.0, long agentic terminal tasks, 39.2% against 70.6%; Humanity's Last Exam with tools 57.4% against 64.5% (Introducing Claude Haiku 5.5, read 15 Nov 2026; vendor). The gap is widest on the long agentic task. On the third-party index at max effort, Haiku 5.5 scores 36 against Sonnet 5.5's 55 (§1).

Effort, at the bottom of the range. On Opus 5.5, low to medium is the largest step on the third-party index, 35 to 48 (§3). On Sonnet 5.5, medium to high is 31 to 42.

The top tier, on hard reasoning. On the third-party index, Fable 5.2 at max scores 65 against Opus 5.5's 59, at 2.6 times the cost per task (§1).

Where they shrink

Large against larger, on the vendor's own table. Anthropic's Opus 5.5 table scores Opus 5.5 above Fable 5.1 on all nine tests listed, among them GDPval-AA v2.1 (1846 against 1735), Humanity's Last Exam with tools (67.7% against 65.6%), OSWorld 2.1 (81.8% against 80.7%, both marked partial) and Chartography with tools (89.0% against 88.4%). The same page says Opus 5.5 performs at the level of Fable 5.1 on most work, and that the gap is narrower in practice than the scores suggest (Introducing Claude Opus 5.5, read 15 Nov 2026; vendor). That table compares Opus 5.5 with Fable 5.1; Fable 5.2, now on sale, is not in it.

Mid against large, on knowledge work. GDPval-AA v2.1 gives Opus 5.5 1846 on Anthropic's Opus page and Sonnet 5.5 1840 on its Haiku page (both read 15 Nov 2026; vendor). The two figures come from two pages and their settings are not stated side by side, so read them as close and leave the order open.

Mid against large, on agentic coding. Terminal-Bench 4.0 gives Sonnet 5.5 70.6% in the Haiku page's reference column and Opus 5.5 66.4%, run at xhigh, on the Opus page (both read 15 Nov 2026; vendor). Again two pages, two sets of settings; on this evidence the mid model is not behind on that test.

Routing studies: a large share of requests does not need the large model. Lingjiao Chen, Matei Zaharia and James Zou found that API fees could differ by two orders of magnitude, and that a cascade which learns which model to call for each request matched the best single model (GPT-4) with up to 98% lower cost (Chen, Zaharia and Zou, FrugalGPT, arXiv 2305.05176, 2023; published in Transactions on Machine Learning Research, 2024; measured, on 2023 models). Isaac Ong and colleagues trained routers that send each query to a strong model (GPT-4) or a weak one (Mixtral 8x7B), and on MMLU, MT Bench and GSM8K cut costs by more than half without loss of response quality (Ong et al., RouteLLM: Learning to Route LLMs from Preference Data, ICLR 2025; models from the arXiv version; measured). The saving in both comes from identifying the requests the cheap model already answers well.

Compute against size. Charlie Snell and colleagues found that on maths reasoning problems where a smaller model already had a somewhat non-trivial success rate, in a FLOPs-matched comparison, spending more inference compute on it outperformed a model 14 times larger (Snell, Lee, Xu and Kumar, ICLR 2025; preprint at arXiv 2408.03314; measured; the abstract does not name the model). The study varied the compute a model spends at answer time, which is not the same control as a vendor's effort setting; reading it as evidence that a higher effort setting can stand in for a larger model, on tasks within the small model's reach and only there, is this unit's inference.

What follows (stated opinion, built on the evidence above)

A small model is right when the task is fully specified by its input, a correct output can be recognised at a glance, volume or speed matters, and an error is cheap to catch: triage, reformatting, extraction to a fixed schema, short tool calls. Each condition traces to a source above: Anthropic's positioning of Haiku, the routing studies, and Haiku's gap on long agentic work. It is the wrong model when the task is long, multi-step or open, or when a mistake hides well, such as a plausible wrong figure in a long text. The large tier earns its price on long, open, searched work and on judgement, and the top rung is the vendor's own second step, after Opus at higher effort has fallen short. One exception cuts across tiers: where a task is arithmetic over numbers, have the model write code and let the code produce the numbers, because the same input can give a model different outputs (BW1 §5) and a script gives the same output every time.

05Self-preference, and why the check runs elsewhere

On the evidence below, a model asked to grade its own work grades it too kindly. Three findings bear on this.

  • Self-enhancement. Lianmin Zheng and colleagues found strong model judges, GPT-4 among them, agreeing with human preferences over 80% of the time, about the rate at which humans agree with each other, and named three biases in model judges: position, verbosity and self-enhancement (Zheng et al., Judging LLM-as-a-Judge with MT-Bench and Chatbot Arena, NeurIPS 2023 Datasets and Benchmarks Track; measured, on 2023 models).
  • Self-recognition drives it. Arjun Panickssery, Samuel Bowman and Shi Feng found that models such as GPT-4 and Llama 2 can tell their own outputs from other models' and humans' with non-trivial accuracy, and, by fine-tuning, a linear correlation between how well a model recognises its own text and how strongly it prefers it, with controlled experiments pointing to a causal link (Panickssery, Bowman and Feng, LLM Evaluators Recognize and Favor Their Own Generations, NeurIPS 2024; measured).
  • Checking itself without outside input does not reliably help. Jie Huang and colleagues found that on reasoning tasks (GSM8K, CommonSenseQA, HotpotQA) GPT-3.5-Turbo and GPT-4 struggled to correct their own answers without external feedback, and that performance sometimes fell after self-correction (Huang et al., Large Language Models Cannot Self-Correct Reasoning Yet, ICLR 2024; models from the arXiv HTML version; measured).

What each kind of separation removes, by this unit's reasoning on those findings:

Check run by Removes the writer's conversation from the window Removes self-recognition Evidence
The same chat, asked to review No No Huang et al.: no reliable gain without outside feedback
The same model in a fresh context Yes No Inference from BW1 §1 (the window) and Panickssery et al.
A different model in a fresh context Yes Yes, as far as the studies tested other makers' models Panickssery et al.; Zheng et al.

Two limits. None of these studies tested a sibling model from the same vendor, such as one Claude model checking another; whether self-preference carries across siblings is untested in the sources cited. And all of them tested models of 2023 and 2024; carrying them to current models is this unit's inference.

The case (anecdote). Academy's method has one model write (Opus 5.5) and a different model audit (Fable 5.1) (Academy case file §2). Its fresh checks before filing were run by three models: Fable 5.1 checked 19 drafts, Opus 5.5 checked 12, Fable 5.2 checked 5; the checkers found at least one error in 15, 8 and 5 of them respectively, and 58 errors in all (counts from the table in Academy case file §5). So a checker of the model the method names as its writer (case file §2; case file §5 records Opus 5.5 as the writer of all 36 drafts), in a fresh context, still found errors in two thirds of the drafts it read. The units, courses and dates differ between the three groups and there is no control (case file §9), so the counts compare nothing; they show only that a fresh context of the writer's own model was not a blind spot in this project.

The rule this course uses (stated opinion, on the findings above): the writer never checks its own text in the same conversation; a check runs in a fresh context at the least, by a different model where the stakes justify the cost, and the report records which model checked. How to check by sample, and what a clean sample proves, is BW6.

06A subscription against the API

What a subscription buys (Anthropic, read 15 Nov 2026). Anthropic's pricing page lists Pro at $20 a month billed monthly or $17 a month billed annually ($200 up front); Max at $100 a month for 5 times Pro's usage every five hours and every week; and Ultra at $200 a month for 10 times Pro's weekly usage, with no five-hour limit for verified accounts. On Fable models, Pro pays with usage credits; Max and Ultra include them up to 50% of the weekly limit (Claude, Pricing, read 15 Nov 2026). A second copy of the same page, read the same day, listed Max at 5x or 20x Pro's usage and no Ultra (Claude, Pricing, read 15 Nov 2026), and the help article on Max prices still lists Max 20x at $200 (Claude Help Center, How much does the Max plan cost?, read 15 Nov 2026). Anthropic's help article on Ultra, read the same day, says the Max 20x plan is now called the Ultra plan (Claude Help Center, What is the Ultra plan?, read 15 Nov 2026), so the second copy and the Max price article are out of date; check the plan page on the day you buy.

How usage is metered. Every plan except Ultra on a verified account has a session limit that resets five hours after your first message (What is the Ultra plan?), and paid plans have weekly limits across all models, with a separate weekly limit for Fable on some plans. What you use depends on conversation length and complexity, features, the model and the effort level; claude.ai, Claude Code and Claude Desktop count toward the same limit (Claude Help Center, How do usage and length limits work?, read 15 Nov 2026). Anthropic's list of what draws usage down names message length, attachment size, conversation length, tools such as Research and web search, model choice, effort level, artifacts and multi-step tasks, and says project content is cached and counts less when reused (Claude Help Center, Usage limit best practices, read 15 Nov 2026). On plans where Fable counts toward weekly limits, it draws from them faster than other Claude models (Claude Help Center, Claude Fable models on your plan, read 15 Nov 2026).

What Anthropic does not publish. None of these pages gives a rate per model: how much of a five-hour or weekly allowance one Opus turn uses against one Sonnet or Haiku turn. Inside a plan you pay in a share of an allowance, at an exchange rate that is not stated.

Where the two meet. Past your limits, usage credits keep you working at standard API rates, billed separately and bought in advance, on Pro, Max and Ultra (Claude Help Center, Manage usage credits for paid Claude plans, read 15 Nov 2026). On Pro, every Fable run is on credits. So the API price list is the one published price that applies to a subscriber, at the margin.

What the API adds. You pay per token at the prices in the appendix, with no five-hour window. The Batch API halves input and output prices for work that can wait, which none of the plan pages read offers; cached input is billed at a fraction of the base rate, as project content counts less on a plan; web search costs $10 per 1,000 searches plus the tokens it adds (Anthropic, Pricing, read 15 Nov 2026).

A sense of scale (arithmetic, illustrative). Pro's $20 a month buys, at API prices, one million Opus 5.5 output tokens, or about two of Artificial Analysis's index tasks on Opus 5.5 at max effort ($10.04 each, §1). Those tasks are long agentic jobs, larger than most chat turns; the point is only that a monthly fee and a per-task cost are of the same order for heavy work, and far apart for light work.

When which (stated opinion, on the documented mechanics above).

  • Subscription for interactive work: drafting, critique, research you read as it runs, work whose volume varies by week. The fee caps what you spend, and the limits cap what you can do.
  • API for work that repeats at volume or runs without you (a batch of extractions, a scheduled check), for work you need to cost per task, and for anything that can wait for the batch discount. You need to write or run a small script, or use a tool that calls the API for you.
  • Both, with credits switched on, when a subscription carries the daily work and a few heavy runs would otherwise stop at a limit.

Which plan the Academy project runs on is not recorded (Academy case file §7), so the case cannot illustrate this choice. How to keep a project inside its plan's limits, and what to keep out of the tool, is BW10.

07A rule for re-deciding when the lineup changes

A routing table is a set of dated claims about prices, defaults and capability. Four kinds of change make it wrong without anyone touching it, and each has a dated instance in this unit's sources.

Change Instance Source
A new model in a tier, at a new price Fable 5.2 at $8 / $40 beside Fable 5.1 at $10 / $50 Models overview, read 15 Nov 2026
A price with an announced date Gemini 3.8 Flash from $0.75 / $3.75 to $1.50 / $7.50 on 1 Jan 2027 Google, Gemini API pricing page, read 15 Nov 2026
A default effort that moved Opus 5.5 defaults to medium; Opus 5 defaulted to high Effort, read 15 Nov 2026
A name that resolves to a newer model, or a benchmark that changes version Academy's "fable" resolving to a newer version (case file §7); Intelligence Index v5 Academy case file §4, §7; Announcing … v5

The rule (stated opinion; each step rests on the sources named in it):

  1. Write each row by tier and effort, with the model and the date it was read. "Large, high; Opus 5.5, read 15 Nov 2026."
  2. Re-decide on an event, and on a date. The events are the four in the table. The date is six months after the table was written: the interval Academy's method sets for its staleness check of any unit written with live search (Academy case file §2).
  3. On an event, re-read before re-running. Read the vendor's models page, price page and effort page on the day; if a tier gained a model, read its default effort, since the same request can run at another level on a new model (Opus 5.5, above).
  4. Test the change on your own work. Keep three past inputs per row whose good output you know. Run them on the new option at the row's effort, check each output the way the row's check says (BW6 for claims against sources), and compare with the old option on what you can see: errors found, reruns needed, and the cost or usage shown. A vendor benchmark ranks the options; your three runs decide. Anthropic's model page frames its own advice the same way, in terms of your evals (Models overview), and OpenAI's guide advises evaluating effort levels against your own evals (Reasoning models); grade: documented practice. If the new option is as good on all three inputs when checked, and costs less or uses less of the plan, change the row and log it (step 6); otherwise keep the row and re-date it with the day you re-read the pages (stated opinion).
  5. Pin what can be pinned. Where a tool lets you name an exact model version, name it in the brief; where it does not, record which version ran (BW1 §5).
  6. Change one row at a time, and log it: the row, from, to, the trigger, the date.

Rules for using the table

  1. Set both dials before the first message and keep them for that piece of work; on the API an effort change mid-session can invalidate the cache (§3).
  2. Escalate by cause. Missing context: fix the window (BW1 §9). Skipped steps: raise effort one level. Full context, real attempt, still wrong: one tier up (§3). Grade: documented practice (Hallie) with this unit's first step added.
  3. Never trust one run of anything that will be published or filed. Its claims are sampled against their sources by a check run outside the writer's context (§5; BW6).
  4. Log every override (row, from, to, trigger, date). Two firings on one row in a month re-open the row under the rule in §7. The threshold is this unit's stated opinion.
  5. Re-decide on an event and on a date: a new model in a tier, a price change, a moved default, a name or benchmark version change, or six months, here 15 May 2027 (§7).

Every product fact in this unit (a model name, a price, a default, a plan limit) was read on the vendor's own page on 15 November 2026 and carries that date.
