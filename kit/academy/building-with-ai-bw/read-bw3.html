01The four parts of a brief that survives

A brief survives when a reader with no context can act on it without coming back with a question. Anthropic's general prompting guide states the test in those terms: think of Claude as "a brilliant but new employee who lacks context on your norms and workflows", and before sending, show the prompt to a colleague who knows little about the task; "If they'd be confused, Claude will be too." (Anthropic, Prompting best practices, read 15 Nov 2026; documented practice, vendor). A model given a gap in a brief usually fills it with the most probable reading of the window (BW1 §7). A brief is the text that removes the gaps that matter.

Four parts do most of that work. The vendors' guides read for this unit cover all four, each in its own words; none of them presents them as a set, so the grouping is this unit's.

Who the reader is

Two different things travel under the word "role". One is a persona for the model ("You are an expert editor"). Anthropic says a role set in the system prompt focuses Claude's behaviour and tone, and that "Even a single sentence makes a difference" (Prompting best practices, read 15 Nov 2026). Measured on factual accuracy, personas did not help. Mingqian Zheng and colleagues compiled 162 roles, tested four families of models on 2,410 factual questions, and found that "adding personas in system prompts does not improve model performance"; picking the best persona for each question helped, but choosing it automatically did about as well as choosing at random (Zheng, Pei, Logeswaran, Lee and Jurgens, Findings of EMNLP 2024; measured). The two sources measure different things, tone and correctness, so they do not contradict each other. Together they place a persona's documented effect in register.

The other meaning is the reader of the output: who will read it, what they already know, and what they will do with it. Anthropic's article on prompting lists "Explaining the purpose or audience for the output" among the cases where context improves results (Claude, Best practices for prompt engineering for 2026, 10 Nov 2025, read 15 Nov 2026; vendor). OpenAI's help centre asks for prompts that are specific about "the desired context, outcome, length, format, style" (OpenAI Help Center, Best practices for prompt engineering with the OpenAI API, read 15 Nov 2026; vendor). Anthropic gives the mechanism: an instruction that carries its reason lets the model apply the reason to cases the instruction did not name. Its example replaces "NEVER use ellipses" with the reason (the response will be read aloud by a text-to-speech engine that cannot pronounce them) and adds that "Claude can generalize from the explanation" (Prompting best practices). A reader line is that kind of reason, given once for the whole output: "a lawyer who will read this on a phone between meetings" settles length, register and layout for every sentence without a rule for each.

The case. Academy (Academy case), a library of self-study courses built by one person with Claude in October and November 2026, is this course's running example; it is one project, an anecdote, never evidence about AI tools in general. Its method joins a shared preamble (the reader, the goal, the sourcing standard, the tone) to each unit's own brief, so every context is self-contained (Academy case file §2). The reader travels with every brief because no writer sees any other.

What the output is

The second part names the artefact, its form and where it will live. Anthropic: "Be specific about the output format and constraints you want." (Prompting best practices). OpenAI: "Articulate the desired output format through examples" (OpenAI Help Center). §3 turns this part into a contract.

Constraints

Constraints are the rules the output must satisfy: what it must contain, what it must leave out, its tone and its limits. Three pieces of guidance shape how they are written.

State the positive. Anthropic: "Tell Claude what to do instead of what not to do", replacing "Do not use markdown in your response" with "Your response should be composed of smoothly flowing prose paragraphs." (Prompting best practices). OpenAI's help centre gives the same rule: "Instead of just saying what not to do, say what to do instead" (OpenAI Help Center). Both vendor guidance.

When a prohibition is needed, name the pattern. Anthropic's guide to Opus 5.5 says a general instruction such as "avoid a generic AI look" "mostly swaps one default for another", and that the model "responds well to instructions that name specific patterns to avoid" (Anthropic, Prompting Claude Opus 5.5, read 15 Nov 2026; vendor, written about frontend design). Carrying it to prose ("no opening sentence that restates the question" in place of "avoid filler") is this unit's extension.

Give an absolute its scope. Anthropic's guide to Fable 5.2 says the model treats an instruction that contains "never" as absolute, and advises writing "ask for approval before deleting files" in place of "never delete files" where an exception is meant; it also says Fable 5.2 follows style rules closely, so a hard limit on sentence length can make its prose choppy (Anthropic, Prompting Claude Fable 5.2, read 15 Nov 2026; vendor). A constraint is followed as written; write it as you mean it.

Older research gives a reason to be wary of bare negatives. Joel Jang, Seonghyeon Ye and Minjoon Seo tested nine tasks phrased with negation and found that, across pretrained, instruction-tuned, few-shot and negation-fine-tuned setups, "all LM types perform worse on negated prompts as they scale" (Jang, Ye and Seo, Proceedings of the 1st Transfer Learning for NLP Workshop, PMLR 203, 2023, 52–62; measured). The models were OPT and GPT-3 from 125M to 175B parameters, and InstructGPT. None is current, and the finding says nothing measured about today's models. It supports one habit only: pair each "do not" with the "do" that replaces it.

Examples, including negative ones

The fourth part shows the output instead of describing it. What the evidence and the vendors say about examples, and how far they disagree, is §4. The short version: an example teaches its shape (length, structure, register) more than its content, and a bad example belongs in a brief only beside a good one, labelled as wrong, with the reason it fails.

02Specificity against length

Length is a cost, and specificity is what the cost buys. Anthropic's prompting article lists among common mistakes that "Longer, more complex prompts are NOT always better." (Best practices for prompt engineering for 2026). Anthropic's Applied AI team frames the goal as the "smallest possible set of high-signal tokens" that makes the outcome you want likely, adds that "minimal does not necessarily mean short", and describes two ways to miss: "hardcoding complex, brittle logic" at one end and "vague, high-level guidance" at the other (Rajasekaran, Dixon, Ryan and Hadfield, Effective context engineering for AI agents, Anthropic, 29 Sep 2025; vendor). All documented practice.

Why extra text costs. BW1 §2 sets out the attention budget and the evidence that a model uses any one piece of a long window less reliably. Three studies bear on prompts in particular; §7 gives their models and dates.

  • Mosh Levy, Alon Jacoby and Yoav Goldberg padded the same reasoning questions with extra text of different lengths, types and positions, and found "a notable degradation" in reasoning "at much shorter input lengths than their technical maximum", in every version of their dataset (Levy, Jacoby and Goldberg, ACL 2024; measured).
  • Shi and colleagues added irrelevant sentences to school arithmetic problems (GSM-IC) and found performance "dramatically decreased when irrelevant information is included"; one mitigation that helped was an instruction telling the model it could ignore irrelevant information (Shi et al., ICML 2023; measured).
  • Daniel Jaroslawicz and colleagues gave 20 models from seven providers up to 500 keyword-inclusion instructions in one report-writing task; the best frontier models reached 68% accuracy at 500, and models showed a bias towards earlier instructions (Jaroslawicz, Whiting, Shah and Maamari, arXiv 2507.11538, 2025; preprint, presented as a NeurIPS 2025 workshop poster; measured).

The direction is the same in all three: material that does not serve the task competes with material that does.

What specificity means. Claude Code's documentation gives a working definition: write instructions "concrete enough to verify", such as "Use 2-space indentation" in place of "Format code properly", and it states that "The more specific and concise your instructions, the more consistently Claude follows them" (Anthropic, How Claude remembers your project, read 15 Nov 2026; vendor, written for instruction files). OpenAI's help centre gives the same rule from the other side: "Reduce “fluffy” and imprecise descriptions" (OpenAI Help Center). A specific instruction is one the output can be checked against, and it can be short. "Keep it concise" cannot be checked; "at most three reasons, twelve words each" can.

Where sources disagree. OpenAI's API guide says "reasoning models will provide better results on tasks with only high-level guidance", and adds that this differs from its GPT models, "which benefit from precise instructions" (OpenAI, Prompt engineering, read 15 Nov 2026). Anthropic's general guide points the other way: "The more precisely you explain what you want, the better the result." (Prompting best practices). On one point Anthropic's Opus 5.5 guide agrees with OpenAI: in chat applications, instructions telling the model to think carefully before answering can be removed, because the model decides how much to think and "the effort level is the main control" (Prompting Claude Opus 5.5). The reconciliation this unit uses (stated opinion): be exact about the output (what it is, who reads it, when it is done), which every source supports, and leave the route to the model, which is where the reasoning-model guidance points. The templates at the end follow that split: they specify artefacts and checks, and none of them scripts the model's reasoning.

03Output contracts

An output contract is the part of a brief that says what will be handed back. It has three clauses.

Format. Anthropic's guide gives three levers: say what to do; use XML tags to mark the parts; and match the prompt's own style to the output wanted, since "removing markdown from your prompt can reduce the volume of markdown in the output" (Prompting best practices; vendor). A format clause names the container as well as the layout: a Markdown file, a table in the chat, an email ready to paste, a file at a given path. A chat assistant that can make documents will choose a container if you do not, and the one it chooses may not be the one your next step can read.

Length rule. Length targets hold for short outputs and fail for long ones. Wei Zhang and colleagues built LIFEBench, 10,800 instances in English and Chinese with length targets from 16 to 8,192 words, tested 26 models, and found that most follow short-length instructions reasonably but "deteriorate sharply beyond a certain threshold"; almost all fell short of the maximum output lengths their vendors claim (Zhang et al., LIFEBench, NeurIPS 2025 Datasets and Benchmarks Track; measured). Anthropic's guidance adds two product facts (read 15 Nov 2026): its latest models are less verbose and "Might skip detailed summaries for efficiency unless prompted otherwise" (Prompting best practices), and on Fable 5.2 "Effort has little effect on the length of assistant turn responses"; length is set by prompting (Prompting Claude Fable 5.2). So a word limit is a usable contract for a caption, a message or an abstract. For a long piece the contract that holds is structural: one section per item of the brief, each answered, none padded, and a range stated as a guide. That form is this unit's synthesis of the LIFEBench result and the vendor notes (stated opinion).

The case. Academy sets a unit's length at 2,000 to 6,000 words "by guide"; its longest UX unit is about 16,900 words (Academy case file §1). The case file does not say whether that length was wanted. It shows one unit that ran past a stated range. That is an anecdote, not a test of the evidence above, and not the failure LIFEBench measured, where long outputs often came out far too short.

What done looks like. Anthropic's prompt-engineering overview assumes three things before any prompting starts: "A clear definition of the success criteria for your use case", "Some ways to empirically test against those criteria", and "A first draft prompt you want to improve" (Anthropic, Prompt engineering overview, read 15 Nov 2026; vendor). Claude Code's guide puts the reason plainly: Claude stops when the work looks done, and without a check it can run, "looks done" is the only signal available; give it something that produces a pass or fail (Anthropic, Best practices for Claude Code, read 15 Nov 2026; vendor; documented practice). The same guide asks for evidence in place of an assertion of success (the test output, the command that was run), and warns that a reviewer prompted to find gaps "will usually report some, even when the work is sound", so a review brief should say to flag only gaps that affect correctness or the stated requirements (same page). In a chat, the check is a list you can run on the output line by line: every item of the brief has its section; every figure names its source; the table has these columns. The templates end with that list, under DONE WHEN.

One more clause belongs in any contract that touches facts: a sanctioned place for what the model could not verify. Anthropic's hallucination guide recommends giving explicit permission to admit uncertainty, which it says "can drastically reduce false information" (no figure given), asking for quotes or sources for each claim, and having the model retract any claim it cannot support with a quote (Anthropic, Reduce hallucinations, read 15 Nov 2026; documented practice, vendor). Academy's method builds this in: before handing in, the writer runs the sourcing checklist and lists up to ten claims it could not verify (Academy case file §2). When Academy's first audit sent three units back for rewriting, one of its two reasons was that the three deliverables did not exist (Academy case file §3). A contract that names the deliverable and says it is built in full gives a check something to find missing.

04Examples and the position of the question: what the evidence says, and what the vendors say

Both topics are full of confident advice. This section keeps the two kinds of support apart: vendor guidance (a model maker's documentation, which says what it recommends and rarely shows the measurement behind it) and research (peer-reviewed work, or a preprint labelled as one).

Examples

Vendor guidance (all read 15 Nov 2026):

  • Anthropic's documentation: "Include 3–5 examples for best results", each wrapped in <example> tags so the model can tell them from instructions (Prompting best practices).
  • Anthropic's prompting article: "Start with one example (one-shot)", and add more only if the output still does not match (Best practices for prompt engineering for 2026).
  • Anthropic's Applied AI team warns against stuffing "a laundry list of edge cases" into a prompt and recommends teams "curate a set of diverse, canonical examples" (Effective context engineering for AI agents).
  • OpenAI's help centre: start with zero-shot, then few-shot (OpenAI Help Center); its API guide asks for examples that show "a diverse range of possible inputs with the desired outputs" (Prompt engineering).

The range runs from none to five, and in every source the deciding variable is whether the output matches. Google's prompt-design page for Gemini was not checked: the fetch was refused, so its advice on examples is left out of this unit.

Research. Sewon Min and colleagues found that randomly replacing the labels in demonstrations "barely hurts performance" on classification and multiple-choice tasks, across 12 models including GPT-3; what drove the effect was the label space, the distribution of the input text and the format of the sequence (Min et al., EMNLP 2022, 11048–11064; measured). On those tasks a demonstration worked mainly by showing the shape of an answer. The reading for a brief (this unit's inference): choose an example for its shape, and expect the shape to be copied. If the example is 200 words, expect 200 words; if it opens with a definition, expect a definition.

Negative examples. None of the vendor pages read for this unit advises putting a labelled bad example into a prompt, and none forbids it. One preprint tested the form directly. Yew Ken Chia and colleagues gave GPT-3.5-Turbo (the 0301 version) a valid and an invalid reasoning demonstration side by side, the invalid one made by shuffling the numbers and names of the valid one, and reported higher accuracy than standard chain-of-thought on every benchmark they ran: GSM8K 69.2 to 79.0, SVAMP 67.2 to 81.6, Bamboogle 40.8 to 56.8 among them (Chia, Chen, Tuan, Poria and Bing, Contrastive Chain-of-Thought Prompting, arXiv 2311.09277, 2023; preprint; measured, on one 2023 model, on reasoning tasks). That is thin support for a general rule, and it is the only measurement found. The rule the templates follow is this unit's synthesis (stated opinion): a bad example appears only beside a good one, labelled as wrong, with the pattern named and the reason it fails, so the model sees the contrast and is never shown a bad shape alone to copy.

Where the question sits in a long prompt

The ask is buried when it sits in the middle of a message, after background and before pasted material. The sources disagree on where it belongs, and they are written for different situations.

Vendor guidance (all read 15 Nov 2026):

  • OpenAI's help centre: "Put instructions at the beginning of the prompt", and mark them off from the context with ### or triple quotes (OpenAI Help Center).
  • OpenAI's API guide: context is "usually best positioned near the end of your prompt", with stable content such as instructions and examples at the start, which also helps prompt caching (Prompt engineering).
  • Anthropic, for inputs of 20k tokens or more: put long documents near the top, above the query, instructions and examples; queries at the end can improve response quality by "up to 30 percent in tests", especially with complex, multi-document inputs (Prompting best practices). That figure is vendor data: the page gives no method, task set or model list behind it.
  • Google's long-context page: performance "will be better if you put your query / question at the end of the prompt" (Google, Long context, Gemini API docs, last updated 15 Oct 2026). A Google Cloud page on prompting Gemini 3 says to place specific instructions or questions at the end, after the data, and that "negative constraints should be placed at the end of the instruction" (Google Cloud, Vertex AI documentation page on prompting Gemini 3, read 15 Nov 2026; its headline was not shown in the copy read).

Research. Nelson Liu and colleagues found performance often highest when the relevant information sat at the beginning or end of a long input, and significantly worse when it sat in the middle, even for models built for long contexts (Liu et al., TACL 12, 2024, 157–173; measured; BW1 §2 gives its models). IFScale found a bias towards earlier instructions (§2). The research agrees on one point only: the middle is the weak position.

The layout the templates use is this unit's synthesis (stated opinion): a one-line TASK first, so the ask is never buried; the long material in tags after the brief; and, when the material is long, the TASK repeated as the last line. That puts the ask where OpenAI's help centre puts instructions and, again, where Anthropic and Google put the question; it departs from Anthropic's advice to place long documents above the instructions as well, which is a trade-off this unit makes so the brief is read before the material.

05How a long conversation drifts, and when to restart

A chat that starts well can end badly with no single bad message in it. Four mechanisms are documented, and they compound.

  1. The window fills. Every turn re-sends the whole history (BW1 §2), so a rule stated at turn 3 competes at turn 30 with everything said since. Claude Code's guide states the constraint most of its advice rests on: the context window "fills up fast, and performance degrades as it fills"; when it is getting full, Claude "may start 'forgetting' earlier instructions or making more mistakes" (Best practices for Claude Code, read 15 Nov 2026; vendor).
  2. Early instructions are summarised away. When a chat grows long, Claude Code, and claude.ai for users with code execution enabled, summarise earlier turns so the conversation can continue, and Claude Code's documentation warns that detailed instructions from early in the conversation may be lost (BW1 §3, with its sources, read 15 Nov 2026).
  3. Failed attempts stay in the window. Each wrong answer and each correction is part of the input from then on (BW1 §4). Claude Code's guide: if you have corrected Claude more than twice on the same issue in one session, "the context is cluttered with failed approaches"; clear it and start again with a more specific prompt that includes what you learned, since a clean session with a better prompt "almost always outperforms a long session with accumulated corrections" (same page; documented practice).
  4. A brief given in pieces does worse than the same brief given whole. Philippe Laban and colleagues took tasks that models do well when fully specified in one turn and gave the same instruction in pieces, at most one per turn, through a simulated user. Across more than 200,000 simulated conversations, all the top open- and closed-weight models they tested did significantly worse in the multi-turn setting, "an average drop of 39% across six generation tasks". The models "make assumptions in early turns and prematurely attempt to generate final solutions", rely on those attempts, and once they take a wrong turn "they get lost and do not recover" (Laban, Hayashi, Zhou and Neville, LLMs Get Lost In Multi-Turn Conversation, arXiv 2505.06120; ICLR 2026; measured). The paper splits the drop in two: aptitude, the best a model does, fell by 15 to 16% on average (the paper's figure and text differ by a point), while unreliability, the spread between its good and bad runs, rose by 112% (§6.2 of the paper). The 15 models tested include Claude 3.7 Sonnet, GPT-4.1, o3 and Gemini 2.5 Pro; none is current.

The third and fourth points describe the same shape from two sides: an early guess, built on and never retracted. The paper's own advice to users is the practical rule (§7.4): consolidating the requirements into a single instruction is "an effective strategy", and starting a new conversation that repeats the same information "might yield significantly better outcomes". It also found that setting temperature to 0 left an unreliability of around 30% (§7.2). That result was measured on GPT-4o and GPT-4o-mini through their APIs in 2025 (Table 3 of the paper). On current Claude models you cannot set temperature at all: Anthropic's API rejects it on Claude 4.7 and later (BW1 §5, read 15 Nov 2026 there), so for those models the question is closed and only the brief remains as a lever.

The case. Academy writes each unit in a new chat or a fresh agent context that sees only its brief and the units already filed, and gives as its reason that leaked context is how a course starts repeating itself (Academy case file §2). When writer agents were lost to container restarts, the third writer of one deep dive resumed from the second's working files (Academy case file §7): the restart worked because the brief and the work were in files, outside the chat. One project; it shows the practice run, and nothing about whether it beats the alternative (Academy case file §9).

When to restart (stated opinion, assembled from the documented practice and the measurement above). Start a new chat, with a brief that consolidates what the old one learned, when any of these holds:

  • the next request is a different task (Claude Code's "kitchen sink session": one task, then something unrelated, then back; Best practices for Claude Code);
  • you have corrected the same issue twice and it is still wrong (same page);
  • the requirements changed partway through, so the chat holds an old version of the brief beside the new one (Laban et al. §7.4);
  • the chat has been summarised, or is long enough that it may be (BW1 §3).

Restarting has a cost: you rewrite the brief. Laban's suggested sentence for the old chat, "Please consolidate everything I've told you so far", gives you a draft of it, which you then check against what you meant before pasting it into the new chat. Claude Code's guide gives the counter-case too: sometimes the history is the valuable part, because you are deep in one complex problem (Best practices for Claude Code). The rule is for when the history has stopped helping.

The decision below draws the rule in the order it is cheapest to check.

06The common failures

Three failures recur in the vendor guidance and the studies below; no source read for this unit counts how often each causes a miss. Each has a fix that the templates build in.

Asking for a plan when you want the thing

The verb of the request sets the artefact. Anthropic's guide: if you ask whether Claude can suggest some changes, it "will sometimes provide suggestions rather than implementing them", even when changes were what you wanted; and if you want more than the task strictly requires, "explicitly request it rather than relying on the model to infer" it (Prompting best practices, read 15 Nov 2026). The failure runs both ways. Anthropic's Sonnet 5.5 guide says an open-ended request "can start building a presentation, report, or video when you only wanted ideas", and that if you want ideas or a plan first you should say so (Anthropic, Prompting Claude Sonnet 5.5, read 15 Nov 2026). OpenAI's guide to its GPT-6 models suggests prompting the model not to stop at proposing a plan or offering to continue, so that you approve "a concrete, reviewable result" (OpenAI, Using GPT-6, read 15 Nov 2026). All vendor guidance. Scope can also overrun on its own: the Sonnet 5.5 guide says that, when coding, the model tends to add tests, documentation and small supporting files nobody asked for, and suggests a system-prompt paragraph that limits changes to what was requested and has the model mention any extra it thinks would help at the end (same Sonnet 5.5 page).

Fix: the first line of every brief is a TASK line that opens with the verb of the artefact and names it ("Write the memo", "Extract a table", "Change the script and add its test"). When you do want a plan first, say so and make it a separate stage: Claude Code's guide separates exploring and planning from implementing, so as not to solve the wrong problem, and skips the plan when you could describe the change in one sentence (Best practices for Claude Code; documented practice). Template 7 is that two-stage brief.

Burying the ask

The ask sits in the middle: a paragraph of background, the request, then pasted material. §4 gives the evidence that the middle is the weak position and the vendor guidance on where the ask belongs. Fix: TASK first, material in tags, TASK again at the end when the material is long.

Stacking unrelated tasks

"Summarise these notes, fix the spreadsheet formula and draft the client email" is three tasks in one window. Each task's material is irrelevant context for the other two, which is the condition Shi and colleagues and Levy and colleagues measured (§2); every added instruction competes for adherence (IFScale, §2); and the three tasks may want different models and efforts (BW2's routing table), while one chat runs them all on one. Claude Code's guide names the pattern as a failure and gives the fix, clearing the context between unrelated tasks (Best practices for Claude Code). Anthropic's general guide says explicit chaining stays useful "when you need to inspect intermediate outputs or enforce a specific pipeline structure" (Prompting best practices). Fix (documented practice): one artefact per brief. Where tasks form a pipeline, each stage is its own brief and its output is read before it feeds the next; BW7 builds that chain.

The case. Every Academy chat works one row of its backlog, and each chat ends by writing the prompt for the next (Academy case file §2): one task per context, with the hand-off written down. One project; it shows the discipline is workable for one person, and nothing about its effect.

What transfers, and what does not (this unit's inference):

  • Direction transfers better than size. Where several studies on different models point the same way (extra material hurts; the middle is weak; long length targets fail; pieces do worse than a whole), the direction is a reasonable working assumption for a current model. The sizes (39%, 68%, the Chia gains) belong to the models and tasks measured, and none should be quoted as a property of the model you use.
  • Vendor figures are not measurements you can inspect. Anthropic's "up to 30 percent" (§4) has no published method. It is a vendor statement, graded as such.
  • Several findings rest on settings you no longer control. Studies of 2022 and 2023 ran models with sampling settings their authors chose. On current Claude models temperature cannot be set (BW1 §5), so a technique that depended on a setting does not carry over as stated.
  • The research tested short, checkable tasks. Arithmetic, classification, keyword inclusion, retrieval. Most project work (a report, a memo, a critique) has no answer key, which is why the templates below put the check in the brief instead of trusting a technique.

09DeliverableTen briefs that hold up

Ten templates for general project work, each ready to paste, each followed by a worked example. Nine examples are worked on the Academy case, using only facts from its case file, cited by section; where the case file lacks a value the brief needs, the example marks it [UNKNOWN: …] and it must be replaced before sending. The voice rewrite (template 8) is worked on a neutral example whose content is invented and labelled so, because the case file holds no voice samples. Product facts are as read on 15 November 2026. BW5 uses the brief form below for every unit of your project; BW6 builds its checker brief from templates 3 and 9; BW7 writes its stage briefs from template 7.

The brief form

Every template has the same parts in the same order, each traced to this unit:

  • TASK: one line, opening with the verb of the artefact (§6).
  • READER: who reads the output, what they know, what they will do with it (§1).
  • OUTPUT: the contract: container, layout, length rule (§3).
  • CONSTRAINTS: positive rules first; prohibitions as named patterns; an absolute only with its scope (§1).
  • EXAMPLES: chosen for shape; a bad example only beside a good one, with why it is wrong (§4).
  • DONE WHEN: checks you can run on the output line by line, including where unverifiable claims go (§3).
  • MATERIAL: pasted inside tags, after the brief (§4).
  • TASK (again): the last line, when the material is long (§4).

Conventions.

  • [FILL: …] is a slot you fill every time; the words after FILL say what goes in it.
  • [UNKNOWN: …] in a worked example marks a real value the case file does not hold. Replace it before sending; never let the model guess it.
  • Tags such as <material> and </material> wrap pasted text, so instructions and material cannot be confused (Prompting best practices).
  • The Model line comes from BW2's routing table, read 15 Nov 2026; where BW2 has no row for the task, the line says so and the pick is an analogy.
  • One brief per chat. If the first output misses, change the brief and open a new chat (§5). In a specified project, where briefs are not reworded once work starts, the miss is raised as a flag and the brief changes at a boundary (BW5 §5, §9).
  • Each avoid-list is a starting choice. Extend it from the first outputs, as Anthropic's Opus 5.5 guide advises for named patterns: check what the first result did instead "and extend the list if needed" (Prompting Claude Opus 5.5).
