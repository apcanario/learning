ATHLETE'S CAREER · PUBLIC · BRIEFS

Managing an Athlete's Career: the briefs

One brief per unit, self-contained: the shared preamble (the reader and the three hats, the frame, the rules and the Portugal layer, money, the not-advice line, minors, anti-doping, real people and companies, the evidence grades, the common claims, the sports, the running case, the deliverables and the closing sections, the tone, the course goal, the sourcing standard, figures) is baked into every open brief, followed by the unit's Aim, Takes and Gives lines. Written units show their note instead. The course itself is read in Course AC.

The job, the map and the evidence

What managing a career consists of when no agency does it, who does each part, and how this course grades what it tells you.

Opus brief
Write one unit of a self-study course in the long-form report format of the Learning Project's markdown courses: a long-form report in Markdown. Length is whatever the topic needs (2,000–6,000 words for the body, up to and including the Self-check; the deliverable is outside that guide); do not pad and do not cut to a target. Quality over quantity: a shorter unit whose every claim was opened and checked is better than a longer one. Reader: a coach of an athlete in an Olympic or federation sport (an individual or small-team sport with little money in it), at a level below the one at which an agency takes interest, who also stands in a second relationship to that athlete: sponsoring them through a business of the coach's own (a kit or equipment label, a gym, a shop), helping them unpaid as their representative to other sponsors and funders (finding money, answering offers, reading contracts, planning the seasons), or both. Takes no payment from the athlete for any of it. Experienced as a coach and, where they have one, as the owner of a small business; not a lawyer, an accountant or a licensed agent. The athlete may be a child. Reads in order (each unit assumes the ones before it), reads on a phone, checks the citations, and wants genuine depth rather than a primer. Address the reader as 'you'. A parent or an athlete doing the same work is a second reader: where their position differs from the coach's, say so in a sentence. This course is written to be shared with anyone; it is an exception to the Learning Project's single-reader rule (decided 21 Nov 2026). Nothing about the Project's owner, his sport, his club or his athletes appears in it. Frame: the reader wears up to three hats for the same athlete (coach, sponsor, representative), all of them meant to help; the course treats the overlap as a conflict of interest that good intentions do not remove, to be declared and managed in writing from AC2 on. Working unpaid removes one conflict (a commission) and leaves the others: the commercial interest of the coach's business in the athlete's image, the power a coach holds over an athlete who is asked to say yes, and fairness to the athletes who are not sponsored. Teach no coaching, psychology or audience-building method: the reader has their craft; Sport Psychology for Coaches holds the athlete's mind and the coach's line with minors, and the public personal-brand course holds how an audience is built on a platform; point to them by title and do not re-teach them. Teach what an athlete has to sell, who pays and why, how the money is found, asked for, contracted and kept, which rules bind it, when a professional takes over, and what the career is planned around. The sponsor's side is taught from the sponsor's seat: what a business buys and how it judges what it bought; in AC9 that seat is the reader's own. Rules: the course never states a rule as the reader's. A rule of a federation, an event, a school system, an anti-doping body or a state is given as that body's own text, read on its own page and dated, as one example of its kind, followed by the question the reader must put to their own federation, event or authority. Say which sport and which country a rule belongs to; never present one as universal. Portugal layer: each unit that treats rules, money or law (AC2 to AC5, AC8, AC9, AC11 to AC14) carries one section headed 'In Portugal', which works the same questions on Portuguese sources (the Diário da República text, the IPDJ, the Comité Olímpico de Portugal, the federation, the Autoridade Tributária, the Segurança Social, the CPCJ), each read on its issuing body's page and dated, labelled as one country's worked example and never as the general rule; DD-AC3 re-reads and assembles them. Money: no fee, rate, commission or 'typical deal' is stated unless a named source published it, with whose figure it is, the sample, the year and the currency; where no public figure exists the unit says so. Every case figure is fictional and marked. Not advice: the unit says once, near its top, that it is information and not legal, tax, financial or medical advice; a legal or tax point is put as a question with the body or professional to consult; nothing advises on investing money. Minors: where the athlete is under 18 the commercial question is a safeguarding question first; from AC5 on, every unit closes its body with a short section 'If the athlete is under 18' saying what changes, by reference to AC4; no unit proposes for a minor a sponsor in alcohol, gambling, supplements or weight management, a sexualised presentation, or the publication of where the child lives, studies or trains. Anti-doping: no supplement is recommended, and a supplement sponsor is treated as an anti-doping risk. Real people and companies: no real athlete, coach or family is named as an example of hardship, exploitation, bankruptcy or failure unless they said it themselves in a cited source or a court or public inquiry names them, and then only as that source states it; no real agent, agency or sponsor is assessed, ranked or recommended; a real company is named only to attribute a dated public statement or rule to it. Nothing teaches how to get around an eligibility or advertising rule: ambush marketing is described as a risk to the athlete, never as a method. Evidence grades: every claim that carries a number, a mechanism or a recommendation carries one grade from the key set in AC1: research (peer-reviewed work, with its sample and design), rule (a rule-maker's or legislature's own text, for what it says, dated), data (a funding body's, statistics office's or event organiser's own published figures; a survey with its sample stated), interested party (an agency, a sponsor, a platform, a vendor, an advocacy group, or an athlete speaking of their own case), practice (a textbook's or practitioner's account of how the work is done, with no measurement behind it). The research in this field is mostly about famous athletes and large sponsorships: a finding applied to an athlete nobody has heard of is labelled as the unit's inference. Common claims: where a claim is common in sport and its support is thin, name the claim and say what the evidence shows; the course's list of such claims is in the seed bibliography, each with the unit that tests it; say 'no independent source found' where none was. Sports: examples come from at least three kinds of sport (a combat or weight-class sport, track and field or swimming, a racket or tour sport, a small-team sport), and no single sport supplies more than a third of them. Running case: two fictional athletes and their coaches carry the course: Sara, 20, 400 metres, and Dani, 14, judo; Milan, 17, football, appears only where a unit needs the contrast with a sport that has licensed agents. They are the athletes of Sport Psychology for Coaches; their facts come only from courses/athlete-career/notes/case-facts.md, which agrees with that course's register; the fictional sponsors, clubs and agents come from the same file; label each '(fictional)' on first use in the unit; a fact the unit invents about them is listed in the notes so that it can be added to the register; the case is never evidence for a claim about the world. Deliverables are built twice: in full on the case the brief names, then as a template the reader fills for their own athlete, each blank saying what goes in it. Every unit ends its body, before the deliverable, with: 'Check before you act' (the bodies to ask and the documents to open in the reader's own sport and country, each as a question); then, from AC5 on, 'If the athlete is under 18'; then 'Self-check' (five to eight questions a sponsor, a federation officer or a lawyer would expect the reader to answer, each with a short answer drawn from the unit's sourced text). Tone: direct, no flattery, no filler, no motivational language, no 'not X but Y' constructions. Course goal: By the end of the course you can help an athlete you coach on the business side without harming the athlete or the coaching: as their sponsor through a business of your own, as their unpaid representative to other sponsors and funders, or both, with the conflicts between those roles written down and managed. You know which rules bind what the athlete may sell, you have found the funding the athlete is entitled to and built a season budget, you can say what the athlete offers a sponsor and prove it, run your own sponsorship of athletes fairly, find other sponsors, agree terms and keep them, read a contract well enough to know what to ask and when to pay a lawyer, tell when a professional should take over, and plan the career alongside and after sport, with a one-year plan written for your own athlete. Sourcing standard: every claim that carries a number, a mechanism or a recommendation is sourced where it is made; acceptable sources are peer-reviewed research and systematic reviews; position and consensus statements of the ISSP, the IOC and bodies of the same kind, labelled as consensus; standard textbooks cited by edition and chapter; a rule-maker's own text on its own page (a federation, an event organiser, an Olympic or Paralympic committee, an anti-doping organisation), dated; legislation and official guidance at the issuing body's page (an official journal, a tax authority, a regulator), dated; a court's judgment or press release at the court's own page; funding bodies' and event organisers' own published figures; surveys with their sample and year; an agency's, sponsor's, platform's, vendor's or advocacy group's own page or report, labelled interested party and dated, as evidence of what that party says, offers or requires and never of how things are; an athlete's own published statement about their own case, labelled anecdote; a law firm's note or a press report is never the source for what a law, a rule or a judgment says: it may lead the writer to the text, which is then read and cited itself; videos, podcasts, social-media posts, unsigned blogs, recruiting-service and lead-generation sites, and press rewrites of a release are not sources, and may be named only as the claim under examination; every reference is opened before it is cited: found by a search, read at its publisher's page, its DOI address or the issuing body's own page, and recorded in the unit's source log with the address, what was read (abstract, summary or full text) and the date; a claim that rests on an abstract alone says so; a reference that could not be opened carries no claim; the seed bibliography (courses/athlete-career/notes/bibliography.md) is a starting list whose entries were checked for existence only, never an authority on what they say; a study's finding is stated with its sample, its setting and its design, and a correlation is called a correlation; a text in Portuguese is cited in Portuguese with the article number, and what it says is given in English as the unit's translation; no uncited figures; no invented names, companies, studies, quotations or numbers; if a claim cannot be sourced, say so and leave it out of the deliverable. A unit that reads as advice without evidence is a failed unit. Links are given as links (the DOI address for a paper), so the course bibliography can collect them. Figures: where a section's content has a shape, declare it as a fenced viz block of a kind kit/viz.py draws (one to four a unit is a guide; none where nothing has a shape); a figure adds no fact, and its caption says what it shows and where its data comes from. Where the unit depends on current facts (rule texts, laws, funding programmes and amounts, prize schedules, regulators' guidance, court rulings), search before writing and date the facts. End with the unit's deliverable, built out in full, not described.
