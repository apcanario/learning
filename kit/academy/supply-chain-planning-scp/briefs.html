SUPPLY CHAIN PLANNING · PUBLIC · BRIEFS

Supply Chain Planning for Product Teams: the briefs

One brief per unit, self-contained: the shared preamble (the reader, the frame, the no-calculation rule, the methods and AI rule, the vendor rules, real companies as cases, the evidence grades and study designs, the case companies, the safety rules, the course goal, the sourcing standard, the tone) is baked into every open brief, followed by the unit's Aim, Takes and Gives lines. Written units show their note instead. The course itself is read in Course SCP.

SCP1

The planning map, the words and the evidence

not written

What supply chain planning is, the decisions it is made of and their horizons, where each kind of system sits, and how to grade what researchers, analysts and vendors say.

Aim draw the planning cascade from memory, place any product module on it, and grade a claim by its source and its design

Takes nothing; it is the course's first unit. Gives the map, the glossary and the grade key every later unit uses.

Shipsplanning map + working glossary + the grade key

Opus brief
Write one unit of a self-study course in the long-form report format of the Learning Project's markdown courses: a long-form report in Markdown. Length is whatever the topic needs (2,000–6,000 words for the body; the deliverable is not counted in that range); do not pad and do not cut to a target. Quality over quantity: a shorter unit whose every claim was opened and checked is better than a longer one. Reader: someone who builds, sells or implements supply chain planning software, or is moving into that work: a product manager, a designer, an engineer, an implementer or a solutions consultant; experienced in their own craft, with no planning background; has never built a forecast, set a safety stock or sat in a sales and operations planning meeting; reads in order (each unit assumes the ones before it), reads on a phone, checks the citations, and wants genuine depth rather than a primer. Address the reader as 'you'. This course is written to be shared with anyone; like the other courses of the Supply Chain track it is an exception to the Learning Project's single-reader rule. Nothing about the Project's owner, his employer, his role or his work appears in it. The course stands on its own and assumes no other course: where the Supply Chain Execution course of this track treats a topic at length (the warehouse, transport, order promising, the buying committee, the stages of a sale, implementation), say so in one sentence naming that course and give here only what planning needs. Frame: assume no planning vocabulary: define each term on first use and add it to the unit's glossary lines. Teach no design, research, engineering or sales method; the reader has their craft. Teach the planning decisions, the people who make them, the data and the software they make them with, who buys that software and what it is said to be worth. The course is for learning the domain. It is not interview preparation and no unit is framed as one. Concepts, no calculation: no formula and no worked calculation appears in any unit. A method is described by what goes in, what comes out, what it assumes and when it fails; a quantity is described by what moves it and in which direction. A number appears only as a sourced finding, in its source's own terms, or as a case fact marked fictional. Where a textbook gives a formula, name it, say what it relates and cite the chapter; do not reproduce or apply it. Methods and AI: statistical forecasting, machine-learning forecasting and mathematical optimisation are methods, treated on their published evidence like any other. Generative AI, language-model assistants, agents and any vendor's AI branding are out of scope: where a source raises them, the unit says so in one sentence and moves on. Where a vendor calls a method 'AI', report that as the vendor's word and name the method if the vendor's own documentation names it. Vendors: no real vendor is assessed, ranked, compared or recommended in this course, and no real vendor's product line is mapped. A real vendor is named only in two ways: to attribute a public statement to the company that made it (dated, graded vendor-reported), or as an entry in a dated list taken from a named analyst report or an audited filing, every vendor on that list given alike. No vendor receives more space, a warmer word or a harder one than another; nothing from inside any vendor appears. Worked product examples use the fictional vendors of the case register (Lathmoor and its two fictional rivals), labelled '(fictional)'; the reader is given the template and the method to map a real product from its public pages themselves. A vendor's description of itself is evidence of what it claims, never of what its product does. Real companies as cases: a real company's planning practice is described only from a peer-reviewed paper, a published teaching or journal case with named authors, or the company's own audited filing, cited and dated; where the company's own staff or its software supplier co-wrote the account, say so and label it an interested party's account. Evidence grades: every claim that carries a number, a mechanism or a recommendation carries one grade from the key set in SCP1: independent (peer-reviewed research; a forecasting competition's published results; an industry association's survey with its sample stated; an audited company filing, for that company's own figures; the text of a standard, for what it says), analyst (a named analyst firm's report, with its date and how it was read), vendor-reported (a vendor's page, release, case study or sponsored survey; a peer-review site's content), practice (a standard textbook's or named practitioner's account of how the work is done, with no measurement behind it; a survey whose sample is not stated). A number a source repeats from another work is graded by its origin, and practice if the origin cannot be opened. Give a number in its source's own terms, with whose figure it is, the sample and the year. Study design: say what kind of evidence a finding is (a forecasting competition, a field experiment, a laboratory experiment, a simulation, a single-company case study, a survey), with its data, setting and year; a simulation result is not a field result, a correlation is called a correlation, and a finding from one industry or dataset applied to another is labelled as the unit's inference. Common claims: where a claim is common in the industry and its support is thin, name the claim and say what the evidence shows; the course's list of such claims is in the seed bibliography, each with the unit that tests it; say 'no independent source found' where none was. Decision pattern (SCP2 to SCP12): treat each planning decision as the decision itself and its horizon, who makes it and how often, the data it needs, what the software does, how it commonly goes wrong, and the measure that shows it improved. Kinds of company: say which kind of company a practice belongs to where it differs (a retailer, a distributor, a manufacturer); do not present one kind's practice as universal; where a term differs by region or by school (sales and operations planning, integrated business planning), give both and say who uses which. Running case: three fictional companies: Corvane, a fashion retailer; Ostrel Foods, a food distributor; Pellward, a maker of small kitchen appliances. Their facts come only from courses/supply-chain-planning/notes/case-facts.md; label each '(fictional)' on first use in the unit; a fact the unit invents about them is listed in the notes so that it can be added to the register; the case is never evidence for a claim about the world; no case company is ever said to use a real vendor's product; where a unit needs one to use a product, it is a fictional vendor's. Deliverables are built twice: in full on the case company the brief names, then as a template the reader fills for a real company or product, each blank saying what goes in it. Every unit ends its body, before the deliverable, with two short sections: 'In the planning meeting' (what to listen for and what to ask when you sit with a planner or in a planning meeting; for the units on products and buyers, what to ask a colleague in sales, services or product) and 'Self-check' (five to eight questions someone who runs that planning process or owns that product would expect the reader to answer, each with a short answer drawn from the unit's sourced text). Safety rules that hold in every unit: no price, discount, revenue or win rate of a private company is stated unless that company published it; no real customer of any vendor is described beyond what a dated public source states; no return, saving or payback is promised to the reader: a reported result is the reporter's, with its grade; no legal, tax or accounting point is stated as the reader's: it is put as a question with the body to consult. Tone: direct, no flattery, no filler, no motivational language, no 'not X but Y' constructions. Course goal: by the end, the reader can take any screen, feature or roadmap item in planning software, say which planning decision it serves and on what horizon, who makes that decision and with what data, how it commonly goes wrong, and argue from that what the product should do. Sourcing standard: every claim that carries a number, a mechanism or a recommendation is sourced where it is made; acceptable sources are peer-reviewed research; published results of forecasting competitions; standard textbooks cited by edition and chapter; industry association reports and surveys with their sample and year; analyst reports, by firm, title and date, read in full, in a public reprint or in abstract, the unit saying which; audited company filings; standards bodies' own pages; and vendors' own pages, releases and documentation, labelled vendor-reported and dated; peer-review sites are evidence of what named kinds of user say, with where the count of reviews was read, never of how a product performs; a book or article by someone who sells the method it describes is an interested party's account and is labelled so; videos, podcasts, social-media posts, unsigned blogs, comparison and lead-generation sites, and press rewrites of a release are not sources, and may be named only as the claim under examination; every reference is opened before it is cited: found by a search, read at its publisher's page, its DOI address or the issuing body's own page, and recorded in the unit's source log with the address, what was read (abstract, reprint or full text), the date, the kind of venue it is (peer-reviewed journal, academic or professional publisher's book, forecasting competition, association, analyst firm, audited filing, standards body, vendor) and any commercial interest its authors have in what they describe; a claim that rests on an abstract alone says so; a reference that could not be opened carries no claim; the seed bibliography (courses/supply-chain-planning/notes/bibliography.md) is a starting list whose entries were checked for existence only, never an authority on what they say; cite the edition or release actually opened; no uncited figures; no invented names, companies, studies, quotations or numbers; if a claim cannot be sourced, say so and leave it out of the deliverable. A unit that reads as advice without evidence is a failed unit. Links are given as links (the DOI address for a paper), so the course bibliography can collect them. Figures: where a section's content has a shape, declare it as a fenced viz block of a kind kit/viz.py draws (one to four a unit is a guide; none where nothing has a shape); prefer the kinds that draw structure (flow, decision, matrix, timeline, states, swimlanes, tree); a figure adds no fact, draws numbers only where the unit's text gives them sourced or marked fictional, and its caption says what it shows and where its data comes from. Where the unit depends on current facts (analyst reports and their vendor lists, market figures, company filings, vendors' public pages), search before writing and date the facts. End with the unit's deliverable, built out in full, not described.

Unit SCP1, The planning map, the words and the evidence. Search before writing. Cover: supply chain planning as against supply chain execution, and why the line matters for product work; planning as a set of decisions sorted by horizon (years, months, weeks, days) and by flow (buying, making, moving, selling), from the advanced-planning textbook's own matrix, named and cited; the cascade from a demand forecast to a released order, at the level of a map only (each later unit takes a part of it); the same cascade for a retailer, a distributor and a manufacturer, and what each leaves out; what a plan is as an object (a quantity, an item, a place, a date, a version, an owner) and why plans come in versions; the systems a reader will hear named (ERP and its requirements planning, advanced planning systems, demand planning, inventory optimisation, sales and operations planning tools, retail planning) and where each sits on the matrix, as categories only; how the software market is described by its analysts, as categories only; the kinds of evidence in this field (forecasting competitions, field experiments, laboratory experiments, simulations, single-company case studies, surveys, textbooks, analyst reports, vendor material, peer reviews) and what each can and cannot show; how an analyst evaluation is made and paid for, from the analyst firm's own published method, saying which pages opened and which refused; how to read a vendor case study that reports an inventory or accuracy gain; the course's four evidence grades, defined so that two readers would give the same grade, including the rulings in the preamble (peer-review sites, unsampled surveys, repeated numbers, the text of a standard) and a public authority's statement of a rule it administers; the common industry claims on the course's list, each stated as it circulates, set against the evidence this unit opens, and marked with the unit that tests it. Deliverable: a one-page planning map (decision, horizon, who decides, system category), built for Pellward and for Corvane; a working glossary of the terms this unit introduces, which later units extend; and the grade key every later unit uses, with one worked example per grade and per kind of study; then the map as a template.

Unit SCP2, Demand and its data. Cover: what demand is as against sales, orders and shipments, and what a stockout hides from the record; the three dimensions every demand number has (item, place, time) and the hierarchies built on them; why the level at which demand is viewed changes what it looks like; the patterns planners name (level, trend, season, intermittent, lumpy, short life cycle), each with the source that defines it and the category scheme the intermittent-demand literature uses; what moves demand that history does not show (price, promotion, weather, events, a competitor, a new product replacing an old one); cleaning history: outliers, stockout periods, one-off orders, and who decides what counts; items with no history; the master data a demand plan rests on (item, unit of measure, pack, launch and end dates, customer and location) and how it commonly fails; where the data comes from (point of sale, orders, shipments, a customer's forecast) and what each is a record of; how a retailer's, a distributor's and a manufacturer's view of the same demand differs.
