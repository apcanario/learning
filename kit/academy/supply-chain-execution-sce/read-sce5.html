011. The roles, one by one

A warehouse's floor work is done by operators; the shift is run by a team lead or supervisor; the stock record is kept by an inventory controller; the work is planned by a planner; the site is run by a site manager. Job titles vary by company and region. This list is the course brief's, and the descriptions below are working descriptions except where a source is cited.

1.1 Operator

Who. The person who receives, puts away, replenishes, picks, packs or loads (SCE2 §3, SCE3 §3). In US federal statistics the largest occupations in the warehousing and storage industry in 2025 were stock clerks and order fillers (457,740 employed), hand laborers and freight, stock and material movers (433,060), and industrial truck and tractor operators (286,750) (BLS, Industries at a Glance, NAICS 493, data extracted 20 Nov 2026) [independent]. These are US figures for one industry classification; this unit reads it as companies whose business is warehousing, with warehouses run by retailers and manufacturers for themselves counted in their owners' industries (not checked against the NAICS definition). No European equivalent was opened.

Measured on. Pace, above all. Bartholdi and Hackman write that most warehouses track picker productivity and so can report average picks per person-hour (B&H §3.3) [practice]. In their bucket-brigade chapter a site's pick rate is reported as a fraction of the work standard, the expected rate for a task (Fig. 11.6) [practice]; Bartholdi and Eisenstein, describing zone picking at one chain retailer's distribution centre, write that individual workers' pick rates "typically range from 50% to 200% of work standard" (1996) [practice: one site, the authors' report]. Accuracy is the second measure: SCE3 §12 lists WERC's picking-accuracy measure and B&H's warning that lines per labour hour is biased by unit of measure.

Pace and conditions. The work is physical and repetitive. Order picking is "one of the most labour- and time-intensive processes" in internal logistics (Grosse et al., 2017, abstract) [independent]. In the United States, warehousing and storage recorded 4.5 injury and illness cases (total recordable cases) per 100 full-time workers in 2025 (BLS, Industries at a Glance, NAICS 493, data extracted 20 Nov 2026), against 2.3 total recordable cases per 100 full-time equivalent workers for private industry as a whole; the release gives warehousing's injury-only rate as 4.4, down from 4.6 in 2024 (BLS, 18 Nov 2026) [independent]. The release does not state the survey's sample in what was read. No source was opened on cold-store conditions (Ostrel's chilled and frozen zones), on noise, or on night work. Which rules on manual handling, working time and cold work apply to a site is a question for the national labour inspectorate (in the EU, also the EU-OSHA guidance for that country) or, in the US, OSHA.

When the system is wrong. The operator meets the error first: a location empty where the record says stock (SCE4 §4), a label that will not scan, a task that sends them to the wrong aisle. Their options are those the device gives them: report a short, skip, or call the team lead (SCE4 §9 traced one short pick at Corvane (fictional) to a service call). Working description.

1.2 Team lead or supervisor

Who. Runs a shift or a zone: assigns people to work, watches the backlog against the cut-off (SCE3 §8), clears exceptions. Working description.

Measured on. The shift's output against its plan, and its departures. The measures are SCE3 §12's (orders shipped on time, lines per person-hour) summed over the team. Working description; no source opened defines a supervisor's measures.

Pace and conditions. On their feet, between the floor and a screen. No source opened describes the role's day.

When the system is wrong. They move people between zones, hold or re-release work, and decide whether a short pick goes out short. The bucket-brigade papers show one thing supervisors do that a system does not: Bartholdi and Eisenstein report that management sequenced workers and had to "persuade workers" that local changes were not improvements (1996) [practice: one site].

1.3 Inventory controller

Who. Keeps the stock record true: plans counts, investigates discrepancies, posts adjustments (SCE4 §5).

Measured on. Inventory accuracy by WERC's measures, with SCE4 §10's caveats about definition and netting.

When the system is wrong. This is the role whose job is the system being wrong. Who may count and who may approve an adjustment (flag F44) was left unsourced in SCE4 §5. The nearest acceptable source opened here is a standard for public bodies: the US Government Accountability Office's draft revision of its internal control standards lists, as a control activity, "separating the responsibilities for authorizing transactions, processing and recording them, reviewing the transactions" and handling any related assets, and names physical control over "cash, securities, inventories, and equipment" (GAO-24-106889, June 2024 exposure draft, para. 10.04) [independent, for what the text says]. GAO issued the final revision (GAO-25-107721) on 15 May 2025, effective from fiscal year 2026, superseding the 2014 edition; its para. 10.04 keeps both passages quoted here (GAO-25-107721, read 24 Nov 2026; the text read in a copy posted by Thompson Grants) [independent, for what the text says]. The standards bind US federal executive-branch entities and may be applied by state, local and nonprofit bodies (draft OV2.01, OV4.17; these paragraphs were not checked in the final text). Applying them to a company's warehouse is this unit's inference: the person who picks from a location should not be the one who approves the adjustment to it, and the person who approves should not be the one who posts it unreviewed. Which separation a company's controls require is a question for its auditors and finance team.

1.4 Planner

Who. Turns tomorrow's expected volume into a labour plan and a release plan: how many people, on which shift, in which zones, released in which order (SCE3 §2). In a site whose outbound is driven by delivery routes, the route plan sets the release (SCE3's register note on Ostrel Foods (fictional): picking is released by route after the cut-off). Working description; no source opened describes the warehouse planner's role.

Measured on. Whether the plan held: hours planned against hours used, work finished by cut-off. Working description.

When the system is wrong. A forecast of volume that is wrong leaves the plan wrong; the planner changes it by calling agencies, moving breaks or changing the release order. Working description.

1.5 Site manager

Who. Answers for the building's cost, service, safety and people.

Measured on. Cost per unit handled, service (on-time, accurate shipments), injuries and staff turnover. Working description; SCE16 takes up cost.

When the system is wrong. Decides whether a workaround becomes the process, and whether to spend on a fix. Working description.

022. Who works the floor, and how long it takes to learn

Headcount and shifts. US employment in warehousing and storage was 1,839.2 thousand in October 2026 (preliminary, seasonally adjusted) (BLS, Industries at a Glance) [independent]. No source was opened on shift patterns, on the share of temporary or agency staff in warehouses, or on peak staffing, so the unit gives no figure. The case register gives one fictional example only: Corvane's (fictional) centre runs two shifts, a third in peak, with about 140 staff in a normal week and about 260 in peak, the difference from agencies. That is a case fact, never a benchmark. Whether agency workers in a given country must be paid or treated like permanent staff is a question for that country's labour ministry or inspectorate.

How long it takes to learn. No source opened gives a training time for warehouse roles across sites. Three narrower findings:

  • Learning happens and can be modelled. Grosse and Glock ran an experimental study at a manufacturer of household products and found that "learning occurs in order picking"; several learning-curve models fitted the data, and two gave unrealistic results (2013, abstract) [independent: one site; the abstract gives no learning rates].
  • Experience reduces search time. Batt and Gallino found the effect of hard search on pick times fell with experience (SCE3 §9, abstract) [independent].
  • Some work methods need almost no training. At the chain retailer, the authors described the bucket-brigade rule to the workers in fifteen minutes, and "within a half-hour they were picking comfortably"; the authors also say temporary workers "can become productive more quickly" under the method (Bartholdi & Eisenstein, 1996) [practice: one site, the authors' report].

For a product team, the gap matters: claims about how fast a new or seasonal worker becomes productive on a device or screen rest, in what was opened, on single sites.

033. Devices as working conditions

A device is where the operator meets the system. Each one fixes what the operator carries, where their eyes and hands are, and how they confirm a task. The descriptions are working descriptions; the research findings are cited.

Device What the operator does with it Working description of the condition it sets
Paper list Reads the next line, marks it done Hands busy with the list; no confirmation until keyed in later
Handheld scanner (RF terminal) Reads the task on a screen, scans location and item to confirm One hand holds the device; eyes move between screen and shelf
Vehicle-mounted terminal Screen fixed on a reach or fork truck Driver reads while seated; the task list follows the truck
Wearable (ring scanner, wrist screen) Scans with a finger-mounted reader Both hands nearly free; small screen
Voice Hears the location, speaks a check digit and quantity Hands and eyes free; the pace is set by the dialogue
Pick-to-light A light on the location shows where and how many; a button confirms Fixed to the rack; suits zones with many picks close together
Desktop Supervisors, planners and controllers at a screen Seated; the place where exceptions are cleared

What the research studied. One field experiment compared the tools directly. De Vries, de Koster and Stam ran 101 participants (academic students, vocational students and professional pickers) through pick by voice, RF-terminal picking and pick to light, with three picking methods. Their results "suggest that (PbV) performs better than RF-terminal picking"; personality traits (Neuroticism, Extraversion, Conscientiousness) and age predicted performance with voice and RF terminals; and higher productivity "appears to be possible without sacrificing quality" (2016, IJPR, abstract) [independent: one experiment, abstract only]. The abstract does not give the size of the difference.

What it has not studied, in what was opened. No opened study measures vehicle-mounted terminals, wearables or desktop screens; none follows a device over months on a working site; none measures fatigue or injury by device. Grosse et al.'s finding that human factors are largely absent from picking research (2017, abstract) [independent] is the general form of this gap. Grosse et al.'s full text, which would say which human factors were studied, was not opened; only its abstract was read.

C5, the voice claim. The claim as it circulates: voice picking brings accuracy to 99.9% or raises productivity by a fixed percentage. The one independent study opened found voice performing better than RF terminals in an experiment, without stating a percentage in its abstract (above) [independent]. No source opened gives 99.9% accuracy for voice; one vendor page that might have stated a figure served no article text (source log). No independent source found for either number.

044. Labour management: standards, tracking, incentives

Engineered standards. A labour standard (or work standard) is the expected time or rate for a task; an engineered standard is one built from measured elements of the task (travel, reach, scan) for each task's conditions. B&H report pick rates as a fraction of the work standard (Fig. 11.6) [practice]. No textbook or study defining engineered standards was opened, so the definition is a working one. On a vendor's website, an article dated 3 June 2021 says labour management systems "based on engineered labor standards have some of the attributes of gamification, but not all of them" (Banker, Manhattan Associates website) [vendor-reported].

Performance tracking. The system records each task's start, finish and user, so a rate per person per hour can be set against the standard (B&H §3.3, picks per person-hour) [practice]. Gartner's glossary definition of a warehouse management system lists "labor management" among its capabilities (Gartner glossary, WMS, read 24 Nov 2026) [analyst; vendors license Gartner's reprints].

Incentives and picking methods: what the research found. De Vries, de Koster and Stam ran a controlled field experiment "in a warehouse erected especially for the purposes of order picking research", crossing three manual picker-to-parts methods (parallel, zone and dynamic zone picking) with two incentive systems (2016, POMS, abstract) [independent]. In the 2015 working-paper version, read in full, the design was 3×2 between subjects with 142 participants in 48 teams of three: 28 university students, 39 professional warehouse employees and 75 vocational logistics students. A competition-based system paid an individual bonus to the top four performers; a cooperation-based system paid a team bonus to the best team. Each team had a 10-minute practice run and a 10-minute recorded run, every team on the same set of orders, with order lines counted per participant (de Vries et al., 2015) [independent].

The findings, in the published abstract: in parallel picking, where each picker completes whole orders, competition raised productivity; in zone picking, where pickers hand orders on, cooperation did; this was "especially pronounced" for pickers with a dominant promotion focus; prevention-focused pickers were more productive in zone picking under cooperation; and "No effects on order picking quality were identified" (2016, abstract) [independent]. The 2015 working paper, the source of the numbers that follow, reported error rates that differed by condition (zone picking: least-squares means of 19.1% of orders with an error under competition, 31.6% under cooperation) and said it investigated quality "from a more exploratory perspective"; whether the published full text keeps these results was not checked. In the working paper's model estimates (least-squares means of order lines picked per participant), parallel pickers reached 53.9 under competition against 46.84 under cooperation, which the paper indexes as a 15.2% gain; zone pickers reached 47.58 under cooperation against 35.37 under competition, indexed as a 34.3% gain; dynamic zone reached 41.03 under competition against 43.91 under cooperation, "hardly any differences" (2015) [independent]. Sample and setting matter: 20 minutes per team, a purpose-built warehouse, and students as well as professionals. Applying the result to a working site with eight-hour shifts is the reader's inference to test, and a bonus scheme's legality and fairness are questions for the employer's legal and HR advisers.

Bucket brigades (flag F44). A bucket brigade changes how work is divided among pickers: each picker carries work forward "until someone takes over your work; then go back for more" (B&H §11.1) [practice]. Sequenced from slowest to fastest, the line rebalances itself (B&H Theorem 11.1); otherwise it may "sputter" (§11.1) [practice]. B&H §11.2 reports a major retailer's national distribution centre where picks per person-hour rose over 30% with no change to layout, equipment or control system, and a major US distributor of recorded music where the average pick rate rose about 20% and its variance fell about 90% (Figs 11.6, 11.7) [practice]. Bartholdi and Eisenstein's paper on the first site says pick rates "reached sustained levels over 30% greater than the previous historical averages", that the change needed "no changes to a typical warehouse management system", and that at the music distributor an operations manager reported an increase in pick rate of over 50% within a day (1996) [practice: one site each, the authors' report]. The two documents give different figures for the music distributor (20% average in the book; over 50% within a day as a manager reported it in the paper); they measure different things over different periods. For software, the point is what the method removes: no one computes a balance of work across zones (B&H §11.2) [practice].

055. Gamification as vendors describe it

What vendors say. Two vendor pages were opened; each gets one sentence here. One vendor page (undated, © 2026) says gamification "uses game mechanics and rewards/recognition tools to digitally engage employees", through leaderboards, badges, points, rewards or challenges, and that its product "can improve warehouse productivity by up to 7%", with no basis stated (a warehouse-software vendor, "What is warehouse gamification?") [vendor-reported]. The article dated 3 June 2021 on another vendor's website (Banker, section 4) quotes Peter Schnorbach, senior director of product management at Manhattan Associates, saying that a labour management system "has no gamification", that a program can be built around one (posting results, rewarding high performers), and that without transparency and real-time feedback such programs "tend to fall short" [vendor-reported]. Both are evidence of what vendors claim. Neither is evidence of what a product does.

What independent work found. Bahr, Mavrogenis and Sweeney screened 47 articles from one database and found "only five" on gamification in warehousing; they interviewed eight UK warehousing managers (one a head of logistics), each with at least four years' experience, and found interest and significant barriers (resources, efficacy over time, ethics). They call the study exploratory and say generalisation "is impossible" (2021, accepted manuscript) [independent]. They report Bräuer and Mazarakis's warehouse experiments with badges and leaderboards as finding that competitive elements could have unintended negative side effects, such as a feeling of incompetence among workers (Bahr et al., §2.4) [practice: origin not opened]. Putz, Hofbauer and Mates surveyed 17 full-time pickers at one Austrian shipping company in 2018 with vignettes; the pickers rated individual and group gamified feedback about alike for fun, performance and motivation (2019) [independent: 17 respondents, stated preferences].

C6, the gamification claim. As it circulates: gamification raises warehouse productivity by a stated percentage. The one percentage opened ("up to 7%") is a vendor's, with no basis given [vendor-reported]. No independent test of gamification's effect on warehouse productivity was opened. The nearest independent evidence is de Vries et al.'s (section 4): the same competitive incentive raised productivity in one picking method and lowered it in another. That supports asking, of any leaderboard, which work method it is used with. No independent source found for a productivity percentage.

066. The machines a warehouse system works with

The review literature describes the machines; it says less about the people next to them. What each changes for people and software is stated from the reviews where possible and otherwise as this unit's inference.

Pallets: the unit the machines are built around (flag F24). ISO 6780:2003 lists six plan sizes for flat pallets in intercontinental handling: rectangular 1200 × 800 mm, 1200 × 1000 mm and 1219 × 1016 mm (48 × 40 in), and square 1067 × 1067, 1100 × 1100 and 1140 × 1140 mm (clause 4.1, Tables 1 and 2) [independent, for what the standard says]. ISO's page says the standard was last reviewed and confirmed in 2026 (ISO 6780:2003, read 24 Nov 2026). The standard gives each size's regional use in its Annex A, which was not in the part read. So this unit does not say which size dominates in Europe or North America; racking, conveyors and robots are specified to the pallet the region uses, and a product that stores pallet dimensions should not assume one (this unit's inference).

Conveyors and sorters. SCE3 §3.5 sets out pick-and-sort: pickers pick in batches to a conveyor and a sorter divides items into orders. For people, picking becomes batch work by article and the order is assembled downstream; for software, the warehouse system releases batches and someone must tell the sorter which lane each item goes to (SCE3 §3.5) [practice].

Goods-to-person. SCE3 §3.7 names parts-to-picker systems: automated storage and retrieval systems (AS/RS), miniloads, vertical lift modules, carousels. Azadeh, de Koster and Roy's review covers newer robotised systems, including shuttle-based storage and retrieval, shuttle-based compact storage and robotic mobile fulfilment (robots that carry storage racks to stations). In their abstract such systems need little space, adapt to changing demand and can run around the clock; many are used in practice with little academic study; and standard decisions (layout, storage assignment, batching, routing) need revisiting for robotised warehouses (2019, abstract) [independent: a review, abstract only, summarised]. For people, the picker stops walking and works at a station at the machine's pace; for software, the system must decide which storage unit comes to which station when (this unit's inference from SCE3 §3.7 and the review's list of decisions). Cost (flag F44): no public figure was found for the cost of goods-to-person equipment; the price of a private supplier's system is not stated.

Mobile robots. Fragapane, de Koster, Sgarbossa and Strandhagen contrast two kinds. In an automated guided vehicle (AGV) system "a central unit takes control of scheduling, routing, and dispatching decisions for all AGVs"; autonomous mobile robots (AMRs) "can communicate and negotiate independently with other resources" and so decentralise decisions (2021, abstract) [independent: a review, abstract only]. For people, robots share aisles with walking pickers; for software, the difference is where the decision lives: in a central controller, or spread across robots that a warehouse system can only give tasks to (this unit's inference from the abstract).

077. The control software, and where the line runs

The terms (flag F5). An analyst at Interact Analysis defines warehouse control systems (WCS) as systems "used to control the flow of goods across fixed automation", and warehouse execution software (WES) as software "used to optimize workflows by controlling the timing and location of order processing"; a WES "orchestrates the timing and location of warehouse workflows in order to optimize throughput" (Scriven, Supply & Demand Chain Executive, 22 Feb 2023) [analyst: a signed article by the firm's analyst in a trade magazine, read in full; not an analyst report, used because no listed kind of source defining either term was found]. No peer-reviewed or standards-body definition of either term was found.

Where the line is drawn. In the same article, WES that "sit above the WCS" are called embedded WES, and "most eWES solutions are truly 'embedded' into the WMS", while some are sold standalone [analyst]. Gartner's glossary definition of a WMS includes "task interleaving, wave planning", "labor management, yard management and automated materials handling equipment interfaces" [analyst]. Read together: the WMS holds the stock record and each order's pick lists and progress, while the customer order itself sits in the order system (SCE1 §3); the WCS drives the equipment; the WES decides when and where work flows; and the two analyst sources place parts of that middle layer inside the WMS. This unit found no source that settles which layer owns release, task assignment or the robot queue.

Where it is disputed. The dispute is over the middle decisions: release and wave planning (SCE3 §2), which station or robot gets which task, and which person does what next. A WMS vendor can claim them as WMS functions (Gartner's list includes wave planning and labour management); an automation supplier can claim them as WES. The AGV and AMR contrast moves the same line further down: with AMRs, some dispatch decisions sit in the robots themselves (Fragapane et al., 2021, abstract) [independent]. This paragraph is the unit's reading of the three sources.

On the case register. Halvern Warehouse (fictional) sells an Automation Connect module; Kestrow WMS (fictional) has none. Case facts, graded nothing; they say nothing about real products. Map a real product the same way: read its public pages for which of release, task assignment and equipment control it claims, and grade each line vendor-reported.

088. C3: is labour the largest cost in a warehouse?

The claim as it circulates. Labour is the largest cost in a warehouse.

What was opened. The US Census Bureau's Service Annual Survey, a probability sample of US firms with paid employees (methodology), reported for the warehousing and storage industry (NAICS 493) in 2012: total expense $23,883 million, gross annual payroll $7,975 million, and all other operating expenses $4,388 million (Census Bureau data as published by FRED: total, payroll, other) [independent]. Payroll was about a third of total expense (this unit's arithmetic: 7,975 / 23,883). The total-expense series was discontinued after 2012. Payroll excludes employers' benefit costs, which the same survey put at $1,895 million in 2012 (FRED, EXPECFEF493ALLEST) [independent]; payroll and benefits together were about 41% of total expense (this unit's arithmetic: 9,870 / 23,883). The other detail lines (rent, depreciation and the rest, $9,625 million together by subtraction) were not opened, so whether labour is the largest single line is not shown.

What the evidence shows. In the one independent series opened, payroll alone was about a third of US warehousing firms' total expense in 2012, larger than the "all other operating expenses" line. The industry is firms whose business is warehousing; in-house warehouses and Europe are not covered, and applying the share to them is inference. SCE3 traced the related claim that picking is over half of operating cost (C2) to textbooks [practice]. No independent source found that ranks labour against every other cost of a warehouse.

10On a site visit

  • Walk one picker's hour. Watch the device: how many hands it takes, where the eyes go, how a task is confirmed, what happens on a short (sections 1.1, 3).
  • Ask what the standard is for that task, and how it was set. Measured elements or last year's average? What rate counts as 100% (section 4)?
  • Ask how bonuses are paid, and to whom. Individual or team, and with which picking method; set the answer against de Vries et al. (section 4).
  • Look for a leaderboard. What it ranks, who sees it, and whether anyone has measured its effect here (section 5).
  • Ask who approves an adjustment, and whether that person ever picks or counts the location (section 1.3).
  • Ask how long a new agency worker takes to reach standard on each device, and how that was measured (section 2).
  • At the automation, ask which system decides which tote goes to which station, and which system owns the order when the machine stops. Ask for the interface list between the WMS and the control software (sections 6, 7).
  • Read the pallet labels and the rack specification: which pallet size is the building built for (section 6)?

11Self-check

  1. What did Grosse, Glock and Neumann (2017) find about the order-picking literature? Management-oriented efficiency criteria dominated it, and there was a clear lack of attention to human factors (introduction and section 3; abstract).
  2. In de Vries et al.'s incentive experiment, which incentive raised productivity with which method, and what happened to quality? Competition-based with parallel picking; cooperation-based with zone picking. The published abstract reports no effects on quality; the 2015 working paper reported error rates that differed by condition (section 4).
  3. What are the sample and setting of that experiment, and why does it matter? 142 participants in 48 teams (working paper), students and professionals, 10-minute recorded runs in a warehouse built for research. A working site with full shifts is a different setting, so the result is a hypothesis there (section 4).
  4. What does the one opened study say about voice against RF terminals? In a field experiment with 101 participants, voice performed better than RF-terminal picking, and higher productivity appeared possible without losing quality. The abstract gives no percentage; no source opened supports 99.9% (section 3).
  5. What does a bucket brigade need from the warehouse system? In Bartholdi and Eisenstein's report, no changes to a typical WMS; the workers balance the work themselves when sequenced slowest to fastest (section 4).
  6. How does Interact Analysis's analyst separate a WCS from a WES? A WCS controls the flow of goods across fixed automation; a WES controls the timing and location of workflows. Most embedded WES sit in the WMS (section 7).
  7. How does an AMR differ from an AGV system for the software above it? In an AGV system a central unit schedules, routes and dispatches; AMRs negotiate independently and decentralise decisions (section 6; Fragapane et al., abstract).
  8. A colleague says labour is the largest cost in a warehouse. What do you answer? In US Census data for 2012, payroll was about a third of warehousing firms' total expense; payroll with benefits was about 41%; the other lines were not opened, so "largest" is not shown (section 8).

The writer's opening note

SCE2 to SCE4 followed goods and records through the building. This unit follows the people: who does the work, what they are measured on, what they hold in their hands, and what they do when the system is wrong. Then it turns to the machines the warehouse system has to work with and the control software between the two. Grades follow SCE1's key (section 10 there): independent, analyst, vendor-reported, practice. Where a line rests on no opened source, it says so and is called a working description: the course's own account, graded nothing, never used as evidence.

One limit holds through the unit. The research opened is thin on the people. Grosse, Glock and Neumann's content analysis of the order-picking literature found that management-oriented efficiency criteria dominated it, with "a clear lack of attention" to human factors in the design and management of picking systems (2017, abstract) [independent]. Most of what follows on roles is therefore a working description or practice, and the unit says which.
