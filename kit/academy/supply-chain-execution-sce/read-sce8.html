011. What a transportation management system is, as analysts define it

The 2026 evaluation's definition. Gartner defines transportation management systems (TMSs) as software that supports "multimodal sourcing, planning and execution" of the physical transport of goods across the supply chain (Gartner, Magic Quadrant for Transportation Management Systems, 30 March 2026, ID G00832918, reprint) [analyst]. Vendors license reprints of Gartner's reports (the course's working assumption, SCE1 §8); this copy is one that a listed vendor chose to post. The report's mandatory features, which every evaluated product must have, are:

  • Freight sourcing and procurement, to procure capacity from third parties "by supporting bid management and optimization" [analyst];
  • Planning, to take in orders and produce a shipment plan, "including order and shipment consolidation, mode selection, route determination and carrier selection" [analyst];
  • Execution, "to take the shipment plan and enable communications with carriers" [analyst];
  • Visibility, to track and trace shipments from pickup to final delivery [analyst];
  • Settlement, to match expected transportation charges against actual charges [analyst];
  • Analytics embedded in the product, availability as a stand-alone, self-service application, and all of this on the vendor's own technology [analyst].

Its common features, which many products have and the evaluation does not require, are analytics measuring key performance indicators such as on-time performance, emissions and costs by mode, customer, product line and location, appointment and dock scheduling, "Private fleet vehicle routing and scheduling/dispatching", parcel carrier selection and manifesting, transportation modelling, forecasting, 3D load building, and direct integration to carrier networks [analyst]. Gartner's review-site definition, updated April 2026, gives the same opening sentence and the same mandatory list (Gartner Peer Insights, "Transportation Management Systems Reviews and Ratings") [analyst], so SCE7 §5, which read that page, and this report agree.

Gartner's glossary entry says something else. It defines a TMS as used "to plan freight movements", to "do freight rating and shopping across all modes", to select the route and carrier, and to "manage freight bills and payments" (Gartner glossary, TMS entry, undated) [analyst]. Two differences matter, as this unit's reading of the two texts:

  • Payment. The glossary puts freight bills and payments in the TMS. The 2026 report's mandatory list stops at settlement, the check of the charge, and a keyword search of its definition, overview and criteria found "payment" only in two vendors' profiles [analyst]. SCE1 placed payment in the ERP as a working placement; the glossary is the first source in the course that places it in the transport system (flag F6; notes).
  • Rating. The glossary names freight rating; the 2026 report's Market Definition names "bid and rate management and optimization" under sourcing, and neither the word "rating" nor "tender" appears in its definition, overview or criteria (same keyword search) [analyst]. Tendering is folded, at most, into "communications with carriers" and into the common feature of direct carrier integration, which exchanges "booking requests and confirmations" (section 5) [analyst]. Where the tender and the carrier's answer are held therefore stays a working placement (flag F64; section 5 adds what the standards body says).

Who counts as a customer, and for whom the evaluation is written. The report's Market Overview opens by saying it "evaluates vendors based on the evolving needs of shippers" [analyst]. Its inclusion rules count a customer as a single legal entity holding a contract for the TMS directly with the vendor, and "Gartner does not count customers of reseller partners or service provider clients" [analyst]. To qualify, a vendor needed, among other thresholds, at least 50 live TMS technology customers, at least $10 million in TMS technology revenue in the previous fiscal year, a three-year compound annual customer and revenue growth rate of at least 20%, customers in at least seven industries (the report gives 3PLs as one example of an industry) and at least 10 live customers headquartered outside its home region; the product had to support truckload, less-than-truckload, parcel and ocean, and its capabilities had to be generally available by 31 October 2025 [analyst]. Two consequences, as this unit's inference: a logistics provider that runs a TMS for nine clients counts as one customer, its clients as none; and a vendor that serves one country, or one mode, is outside the evaluation by rule, as SCE6 §2 found for warehouse software.

Where a textbook stands. A standard transport text, Novack, Gibson and Coyle's Transportation: A Global Supply Chain Perspective (9th edition, Cengage, 2019), treats transportation software and the TMS in chapter 3, according to its publisher's contents page (Cengage); the chapter was not read, so it carries nothing here; the publisher's page also lists a 10th edition (flag F73).

022. What sits beside it

Four kinds of software touch the same shipments. Gartner keeps a separate review market, with its own definition, for three of them; the fourth is in SCE1's glossary.

  • Visibility. Gartner defines the real-time transportation visibility platform (RTTVP) market as platforms that provide real-time location "and status insights into orders once they have left the warehouse"; mandatory features are basic tracking of location and status in transit, road visibility among other modes, messages and alerts on delays and status changes, a predicted arrival time computed from live and historical data and outside factors such as traffic and weather, independent of the estimated time of arrival (ETA) other parties give, and a product sold and implemented on its own (Gartner Peer Insights, RTTVP market, features updated March 2025) [analyst]. The page listed 46 products [analyst]. The TMS evaluation makes tracking mandatory in the TMS itself (section 1), so the same event can be held by two products (this unit's inference).
  • Freight audit and payment (FAP). Gartner describes FAP services as invoice collection, preaudit, processing, payment and analytics of transportation invoices across multiple modes and regions (Gartner Peer Insights, FAP providers market) [analyst]; the page listed 42 products and showed no features date. The word is services: a shipper can buy the audit and the payment as work done by someone else, while the TMS's settlement capability checks the charge (section 1). Which of the two holds the paid record is a seam question for SCE10.
  • Routing for own fleets. Gartner defines a vehicle routing and scheduling (VRS) solution as an application that creates vehicle routes and schedules under multiple constraints and service requirements while minimising fleet costs and mileage (this part of the definition is unverified: the page failed to load at the fresh check and at a later check); its mandatory features are scenario modelling, territory planning, real-time routing, dispatch, dynamic routing and static routing (Gartner Peer Insights, VRS market, features updated July 2026) [analyst]; the page listed 79 products. The TMS evaluation lists private fleet routing and dispatch as a common TMS feature (section 1) [analyst]. SCE7 §8 explains why the problem is different from bought freight.
  • Global trade management (GTM). Trade compliance and international logistics, which Gartner lists beside the warehouse and transport systems in its execution glossary (SCE1 §4) [analyst]. This course does not treat it further.

033. Who works in a transport system

The brief names five roles. Job titles vary by company and region. The US Department of Labor's occupational database, O*NET, describes three of them from its own task statements; it is a US source, and what it says is graded practice (a public authority's account). The rest is working description, and the cards in the deliverable mark each line.

3.1 Transport planner

What the work is. Turns orders into shipments or routes: consolidates, picks the mode and the carrier, or builds the day's routes for an own fleet. Planning in Gartner's mandatory list is exactly that: consolidation, mode selection, route determination and carrier selection (section 1) [analyst]. O*NET has no "transport planner" occupation; "Load Planner" is a sample job title under Cargo and Freight Agents, whose core tasks include to "Negotiate and arrange transport of goods with shipping or freight companies" and to "Determine method of shipment" and prepare the shipping documents (O*NET, 43-5011.00, updated 2026) [practice]; and among logistics analysts' supplemental tasks is to reorganise shipping schedules "to consolidate loads, maximize vehicle usage" (O*NET, 13-1081.02, updated 2026) [practice].

What they do in the system. Release orders into a plan, accept or change the proposed consolidation, choose or confirm the carrier, and send the tender (SCE7 §6, steps 1 to 3). For an own fleet, build and publish routes. Working description.

Measured on. Vehicle fill and freight cost per unit, both the course's definitions (SCE7 §11); for an own fleet, routes built on time and vehicles used. No source opened defines a planner's measures.

In the case. Sofia builds Ostrel Foods' (fictional) next day's routes for 40 vehicles and about 600 delivery points (case register). At Tandric Logistics (fictional), Priya's freight desk tenders loads to carriers (register), so the planner's tender work sits with her there; SCE7 deliverable B has her planning the load (this unit follows that reading).

3.2 Dispatcher

What the work is. Runs the day once the plan is out. ONET describes dispatchers as workers who schedule and dispatch crews, equipment or service vehicles to move materials, freight or passengers, using radio, phone or computer to send assignments and compile progress reports; "Truck Dispatcher" is a sample title, and the tasks include to "Prepare daily work and run schedules" and to monitor equipment locations and use to coordinate service and schedules (O*NET, 43-5032.00, updated 2026) [practice]. ONET's software list for the occupation includes route navigation, mobile location-based services, map creation and geographic information software, and has no software category for transport management systems (same page) [practice].

What they do in the system. Watch vehicles against the plan, change stops or sequence during the day, record exceptions from drivers. Dispatch and real-time routing are mandatory features of Gartner's VRS market (section 2) [analyst], so for an own fleet the dispatcher's screen is likely to be a routing product's (this unit's inference). For bought freight, the dispatcher's counterpart watches carriers' status events instead (section 5).

Measured on. On-time delivery (the course's definition, SCE7 §11). Working.

In the case. Nuno (fictional) handles Ostrel's day's calls from drivers and customers (register); Ostrel's drivers return with refused goods, and some routes still run on paper delivery notes (register).

3.3 Carrier representative

What the work is. The person at the carrier who answers the shipper's or provider's system: accepts or refuses a tender, gives a pickup time, reports status, sends the invoice. No O*NET occupation or other source opened describes the role, so everything about it is working description, except the messages it answers. In X12, the standards body that publishes these electronic data interchange messages, the 214 message "can be used by a transportation carrier" to give shippers, consignees and their agents the status of shipments in dates, times, locations, route and identifying numbers (X12, 214) [independent, for what the standard says]; the 210 is "used both as a motor carrier invoice to request payment" and as detail of a shipment's charges (X12, 210) [independent]. The tender (204) and the answer (990) are section 5's and SCE7 §6's.

Measured on. By the party tendering: whether it accepts, measured as the primary carrier acceptance ratio, and whether it picks up and delivers on time (SCE7 §§4, 11). In the one large study SCE7 read, weekly primary acceptance averaged 81.9% in a soft US truckload market and 68.5% in a tight one (Acocella, Caplice and Sheffi 2020, as read in SCE7 §4) [independent]. By its own employer: no source opened.

In the case. The register names no carrier staff. The card in the deliverable is for the person at a carrier who answers Priya's tenders at Tandric (fictional), unnamed.

3.4 Freight auditor

What the work is. Checks each carrier invoice against what was agreed and releases it for payment, or disputes it. In Gartner's terms the check is the TMS's settlement capability, matching expected against actual charges (section 1) [analyst], and the whole job can be bought as a freight audit and payment service (section 2) [analyst]. O*NET has no freight-audit occupation; its nearest, Billing and Posting Clerks, includes "Pre Audit Clerk" among sample titles and tasks such as computing "shipment charges, or rates for goods or services" to complete billing documents (O*NET, 43-3021.00, updated 2026) [practice]; none of its tasks names freight or carriers (same page).

What they do in the system. Open the invoice, see it matched against the tender, the rate and any recorded extras (waiting time, re-delivery), approve or query (SCE7 §6, steps 9 to 11). Working description.

Measured on. The course's invoice match rate (SCE7 §11). No independent study of freight invoice error rates was found (SCE7 §6, step 10), and none was found here.

In the case. Priya (fictional) checks carriers' invoices at Tandric (register); Tandric's invoices are disputed when activity was not recorded (register).

3.5 Analyst

What the work is. Turns the transport record into measures and recommendations. ONET's Logistics Analysts lists "Transportation Analyst" among sample titles; its core tasks include to "Prepare reports on logistics performance measures", to compute metrics "such as on-time delivery rates", to provide ongoing analyses of "transportation costs" and to "Contact carriers for rates or schedules", and a supplemental task is to maintain freight rate databases (ONET, 13-1081.02) [practice]. APQC, the benchmarking association, keeps an outbound-transport cost measure, "Total Cost to Operate Outbound Transportation per $1,000 in Revenue" (APQC) [practice: the measure's name; its definition was not on the page read] (flag F77).

What they do in the system. Run the embedded analytics that Gartner makes mandatory (section 1) [analyst], export data, and prepare the bid that sets next year's routing guide (SCE7 §3). Working description.

Measured on. No source opened. Working: the reports delivered, and whether the recommendations moved cost or service.

In the case. The register has no analyst. At Tandric (fictional), Olu answers the clients' questions and sends their reports (register); this unit treats that report work as the analyst's role there (this unit's reading, listed in the notes).

044. Two kinds of customer: the shipper and the provider

The shipper. For a shipper, freight is a cost of selling its own goods. It buys carriage (SCE7 §1), or runs its own vehicles, and the measures it watches are what it pays and the service it gets: freight cost per unit, on-time delivery, carrier acceptance (SCE7 §11). The 2026 evaluation is written from this side: it evaluates vendors "based on the evolving needs of shippers" (section 1) [analyst]. Corvane (fictional) books carriers in each carrier's portal, with no transport system; Ostrel Foods (fictional) runs its own fleet with a routing tool (case register).

The logistics provider or broker. For a provider, freight is what it sells. Tandric's (fictional) freight desk arranges loads with outside carriers for clients, owns no trucks, and is paid for freight at a margin over what the carrier is paid (register). In US law a broker is a person, other than a motor carrier, who arranges motor carrier transportation for compensation (49 U.S.C. §13102(2), as read in SCE7 §1) [independent]. ONET lists "Freight Broker" as a sample title of Cargo and Freight Agents, and its Freight Forwarders' core tasks include to "Negotiate shipping rates with freight carriers", to "Prepare invoices or cost quotations for freight transportation" and to "Pay or arrange for payment of freight" (O*NET, 43-5011.01, updated 2026) [practice]: one person both buys the carriage and sells it on. MIT's FreightLab names shippers, carriers and brokers as the three parties its freight research works for (MIT CTL, FreightLab) [practice]. Textbook vocabulary does not settle the categories: Rodrigue's Geography of Transport Systems* calls a third-party logistics provider "an asset-based company" offering logistics services, and a fourth-party provider non-asset-based, mainly providing organisational expertise (Rodrigue, 6th edition, Routledge, 2024, ch. 7) [practice]; by that test Tandric, with a warehouse and no trucks, is asset-based in warehousing and not in freight (this unit's reading).

What the margin does to the record. Because the provider earns the difference between two prices, each load carries two rates (the client's price and the carrier's), two invoices (the carrier's to Tandric and Tandric's to the client) and two disputes (SCE7 deliverable B, rows 2 and 9 to 12). In the United States the margin is also a record the other parties can ask for: the current rule gives "the parties to a brokered freight transaction" a right to review the broker's record of it, and a notice of proposed rulemaking would require brokers to keep those records electronically, itemise all charges and fees, and provide them within 48 hours of a request by the shipper, the motor carrier or any other party (FMCSA, Docket FMCSA-2023-0257, Transparency in Property Broker Transactions) [practice: the agency's account of the current rule and of a proposal published in the Federal Register on 20 November 2024]. Whether the proposal became final was not checked here, and whether Tandric is a broker in law, and what it must disclose, is a question for its legal advisers and the national regulator (SCE7 §1).

What shippers say they want from providers. In the 2025 Third-Party Logistics Study, sponsored by NTT DATA and Penske Logistics, with a survey that closed in July 2024, shipper respondents named as must-have technologies from their 3PLs control tower visibility (68%), transportation management planning (54%) and transportation management scheduling (53%); 55% of 3PL respondents said their IT services include control tower visibility, and the share of shippers satisfied with 3PLs' IT capabilities was 87% (2025 Third-Party Logistics Study) [vendor-reported: a sponsored survey; the total number of respondents was not found in the text read, which gives shares by group: shipper users 44%, non-users 9%, 3PL or 4PL respondents 27%]. For a provider, then, transport planning and visibility are things it sells to its clients, which a shipper only uses (this unit's inference from a sponsored survey).

What small providers can spend. One mixed-methods study of small and medium-sized Italian 3PLs (a postal survey of 1,992 firms with 153 usable responses, focus groups, and seven case companies) found "a relatively low level of ICT expenditure", that only 38% of the sample offered track and trace, and case companies that did not offer it said mainly that customers did not demand it and their systems would not support it, that ICT products and services were seen as not aligned with small 3PLs' needs, and that lack of technology skills was a major constraint (Evangelista, McKinnon & Sweeney, 2013, author's manuscript read in full) [independent]. Applying it to providers outside Italy, or to 2026, is this unit's inference.

Where the two pull apart, as this unit's reading of the sources above and the register:

  • Rates on both sides. A shipper's TMS holds what it pays; a provider's holds what it pays and what it charges, and the margin between them per load.
  • Many customers inside one system. Each of Tandric's nine clients wants its own rules and reports, and a new client is expected to be live within weeks of signature (register). A shipper configures one company.
  • The client as a user. Tandric has a client portal (register); the client sees the provider's data. A shipper's customers see, at most, a delivery date.
  • Billing. A shipper pays invoices; a provider pays and issues them, and its invoices are disputed when activity was not recorded (register).
  • Who the analyst counts. The evaluation counts the provider as one customer and its clients as none, and is written around shippers' needs (section 1) [analyst]. A provider reading it reads a shipper's evaluation.

Deliverable B sets these out row by row. DD-SCE1 develops the provider's business.

055. How the system connects to carriers, and what that means for the people

Three kinds of connection carry the same conversation between the system and a carrier: the tender, the answer, the status and the bill.

Electronic data interchange (EDI). X12's 204 Motor Carrier Load Tender allows "shippers or other interested parties to offer (tender) a shipment" to a full-load (truckload) motor carrier, with scheduling, equipment, commodities and instructions; it is not used for less-than-truckload bills of lading, pickup notifications or manifests (X12, 204) [independent, for what the standard says]. The carrier answers with a 990 (SCE7 §6, step 3) [independent], reports status with a 214, and bills with a 210 (section 3.3) [independent]. IFTSTA, a UN/EDIFACT message used in GS1's EANCOM subset, carries transport status (SCE7 §6, step 7; SCE10 §4) [independent]. Which regions use which standard was not sourced (SCE7 §7). On the standard's wording, the tender is the shipper's or another interested party's message, which places the tender with whoever runs the shipper's side: Tandric's desk, or a shipper's transport system (this unit's reading; flag F64).

Portals. A carrier that does not exchange EDI can be worked through a web portal: the shipper books in the carrier's portal, as Corvane (fictional) does today (register), or the carrier answers in a portal the shipper's or provider's system offers (working description; no source opened describes such portals). Direct integration to carrier networks, a large number of external carriers "connected via API or electronic data interchange [EDI]" and exchanging data such as rates, booking requests and confirmations and tracking events, either in the core product or through partnerships with external carrier networks, is a common feature of the 2026 evaluation (section 1) [analyst].

How many carriers there are to connect. In the United States, of 787,189 active carriers registered with the Federal Motor Carrier Safety Administration at the end of 2023, 418,526 had one power unit (a truck or tractor, in this course's gloss), 123,353 had two, 168,507 had three to ten, 56,715 had 11 to 100, 5,062 more than 100 and 15,026 none or unreported; 735,895 were interstate freight carriers (FMCSA, 2024 Pocket Guide to Large Truck and Bus Statistics, July 2025, data from a 29 December 2023 snapshot) [independent: official registration statistics]. About 53% had one power unit and about 90% ten or fewer (this unit's arithmetic, all carrier types). No source opened says how many of them exchange EDI, and no European figure was opened. A transport system that tenders to small carriers will meet many with no EDI at all (this unit's inference).

What the connection means for each role, as this unit's reading:

  • Planner. With EDI, a tender goes out and an answer comes back into the record; without it, the planner phones or e-mails, waits, and keys the answer. The acceptance ratio (section 3.3) is computable only from answers the system recorded.
  • Dispatcher or tracking desk. Status arrives as 214 or IFTSTA events, or as calls. A missing event leaves the customer told nothing (SCE7 §10).
  • Carrier representative. Works in every customer's channel at once: one shipper's EDI, another's portal, a broker's phone call. Each customer's system is one more place to answer.
  • Freight auditor. A 210 can be matched against the tender automatically; a paper or PDF invoice is keyed first.

066. Halvern's transport line on SCE7's lifecycle

Halvern Systems (fictional) sells order, warehouse and transport software and nothing else, to retailers, distributors and logistics providers, mid-sized to large, by cloud subscription, and says of itself "One vendor for execution" (case register). Its transport line is Halvern Transport (the core system) with five modules: Rating and Tendering, Fleet Routing, Tracking, Freight Audit and Claims (register). Everything in this section is a case fact or a placement of one; none of it is evidence about any real product, and no real vendor is meant.

On SCE7's steps, SCE7 §5 placed the modules: Rating and Tendering at steps 2 to 4, Tracking at step 7, Freight Audit at steps 10 and 11 (step 11 revised by SCE10 §7: Freight Audit holds the approved charge, the payment is in the ERP; working), Claims at step 12, and Fleet Routing in own-fleet routing, outside bought freight (register, added by SCE7). This unit adds one assumption: the core holds the shipment plan of step 1 (consolidation, mode and carrier selection), the first of Gartner's mandatory planning capabilities (section 1) [analyst], as SCE6 placed Gartner's core warehouse capabilities in Halvern Warehouse's core; it is listed in the notes for the register.

What the placement shows, as this unit's reading:

  • Four steps have no module. Booking a dock (5), pickup (6), delivery and its proof (8) and the carrier's invoice coming in (9). Halvern lists a Yard module in its warehouse line and none in its transport line (register; SCE7 §9), so the appointment may sit in the warehouse product. Whether Freight Audit takes in the invoice (step 9) or only checks it is open.
  • Freight Audit's name claims the check. SCE7 placed it at payment too (step 11); the register's name says audit, and Gartner's glossary puts payment in the TMS (section 1). Whether Halvern pays carriers, or hands the approved invoice to the ERP, is a question for its pages, not answerable from the register.
  • Nothing in the transport line bills a client. Halvern's warehouse line has Client Billing, for billing clients by activity (register; SCE6 §7). Tandric (fictional) is paid for freight at a margin (register), so it needs a client price and a client invoice for each load; the register gives no module that holds either. Halvern names logistics providers among its customers (register); whether its line serves the provider's half of the record is the open question deliverable C ends on.
  • Tracking or a visibility platform. Gartner treats tracking both as a TMS capability and as a separate market (section 2); Halvern's Tracking module claims the step and says nothing of predicted arrival times or alerts.

099. What public pages and peer reviews can and cannot show

Peer reviews. Gartner's TMS review market listed 92 products on 27 November 2026 and showed no total count of reviews; each product listing carries its own count of ratings (Peer Insights TMS page) [analyst]. Vendors can invite their own customers to review (SCE1 §7), so this course grades what reviewers say vendor-reported. What a market page can give is what a counted number of reviewers of a named kind (industry, company size, role, where the page shows them) say about products in that market. It cannot give how a product performs, whether it suits a provider or a shipper unless reviewers say which they are, or a ranking (this unit's inference from SCE1 §7).

Vendors' pages and listings. The product descriptions on the same market page claim, between them, load tendering, freight auditing and payment, and automated tendering (Peer Insights TMS page) [vendor-reported]: the vendors put in their TMS products what the analyst's mandatory list leaves unnamed. That is evidence of what they claim (flag F64). The method and the cautions of SCE6 §9 apply unchanged: collect the pages with their dates, list the modules in the vendor's own names, place each claim on lines T1 to T24, grade every row vendor-reported, and write "no public claim found" where no page claims a line.

What the pages cannot show, as this unit's inference, in addition to SCE6 §9's list:

  • Which customer the product was built for. A page that lists shippers, carriers and brokers as users does not say whether a broker's two-sided rate (T22) or many clients in one system (T23) exist.
  • How the carriers are connected. A count of connected carriers does not say by EDI, by portal or by a third party's network, or what a small carrier must do to answer (section 5).
  • Where payment happens. A page that claims "freight payment" may mean the TMS approves, a service pays, or the ERP pays (sections 1, 2).
  • The analyst report itself. Two vendors' landing pages for the 2026 report, opened for this unit, held no report text, only the offer of a copy on request; such a page states only the vendor's own placement, which this course does not repeat.

1010. C11: "A control tower gives end-to-end visibility"

The claim as it circulates. A control tower, one screen fed by every party's data, shows the whole chain from order to delivery (course list, C11).

What the sources opened show.

  • The analyst's definitions bound visibility. Gartner's visibility-platform market covers orders "once they have left the warehouse" (section 2) [analyst]; its TMS evaluation requires tracking from pickup to final delivery (section 1) [analyst]. The visibility each describes starts at the warehouse door or at pickup; neither covers the goods before they leave. The 2026 TMS report says vendors are "enhancing their control-tower-like capabilities", and the phrase "end-to-end visibility" does not appear in it (keyword search of the reprint) [analyst]. Where the report's vendor profiles describe automated agents in control towers, those are AI features, which this course does not treat.
  • Buyers ask for it. In the sponsored 2025 Third-Party Logistics Study, 68% of shipper respondents named control tower visibility a must-have technology from their 3PLs, and 55% of 3PLs said they offer it (section 4) [vendor-reported]. That measures demand and offer; it says nothing of reach.
  • One study looked inside. Staudt et al. combined a scoping review of 32 papers on control towers with semi-structured interviews of four employees at three large multinational companies in Brazil (mining, refrigeration and engines, civil construction). They define a control tower as a technology-enabled platform that integrates real-time data, advanced analytics and organisational knowledge "to provide end-to-end visibility", among other aims; they found that two of the three companies had not yet incorporated real-time tracking or visibility layers, that end-to-end supply chain management was not identified in the cases, and that legacy systems, data quality and interoperability mean "full integration remains a distant goal" (Staudt, Lopes, Corrêa, Bouzon & Di Mascolo, 2026, full text) [independent: qualitative, three companies, one country]. Applying it to other companies is this unit's inference.

What the evidence shows. No source opened shows that a control tower gives end-to-end visibility. The analyst definitions draw visibility from pickup or from the warehouse door onward; the one independent study found visibility layers missing in two of three companies and end-to-end management in none. No independent source found for the claim. What a product team can ask of any "end-to-end" claim is which parties' data feeds it, by which connection (section 5), and from which step to which (SCE7 §6).
