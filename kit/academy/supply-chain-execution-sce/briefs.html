SUPPLY CHAIN EXECUTION · PUBLIC · BRIEFS

Supply Chain Execution for Product Teams: the briefs

One brief per unit, self-contained: the shared preamble (the reader, the scope, the vendor rules, the evidence grades, the case operations, the safety rules, the course goal, the sourcing standard, the tone) is baked into every open brief, followed by the unit's Aim, Takes and Gives lines. Written units show their note instead. The course itself is read in Course SCE.

SCE1

The map, the words and the evidence

written

What supply chain execution is, where each system starts and stops, and how to grade what vendors, analysts and researchers say.

Aim draw the execution map from memory, place any product module on it, and grade a claim by its source

Takes nothing; it is the course's first unit. Gives the map, the glossary and the grade key every later unit uses.

Shipsexecution map + working glossary + the grade key

Read the unit →
Opus brief
Unit SCE1, The map, the words and the evidence. Search before writing. Cover: supply chain execution as against supply chain planning, and why the line matters for product work; the flow of one order from promise to delivery, named step by step at the level of a map only (each later unit takes a part of it); what the order management, warehouse management and transport management systems each hold as their own, and where the ERP stops; the neighbouring systems a reader will hear named (labour management, yard management, warehouse control and warehouse execution, visibility tools) and where each sits; the kinds of company that run these operations (retailers, manufacturers, wholesalers and distributors, logistics providers) and how they differ at the level this unit needs; how the software market is described by its analysts, as categories only; the kinds of evidence in this field (peer-reviewed operations research, industry association surveys, analyst reports, vendor material, peer reviews, textbooks) and what each can and cannot show; how an analyst quadrant is made and paid for, from the analyst firm's own published method; how to read a vendor case study; the course's four evidence grades, defined so that two readers would give the same grade; the common industry claims on the course's list, each stated as it circulates and then set against its evidence. Deliverable: a one-page execution map (steps, systems, owners), built for Corvane; a working glossary of the terms this unit introduces, which later units extend; and the grade key every later unit uses, with one worked example per grade; then the map as a template.

From the truck at the gate to stock in a location: receiving, putaway, storage and replenishment, and what the warehouse system does at each step.

Takes SCE1. Gives the flow-map form SCE3, SCE7 and SCE9 reuse; storage and replenishment, which SCE3 and SCE4 build on.

Shipsinbound flow map + failure list

Read the unit →
Opus brief
Unit SCE2, Inbound and storage. Cover: what a warehouse is for, from the textbooks' own account; the inbound sequence step by step (the purchase order and the advance notice, the appointment and the dock, unloading, checking and counting, quality hold, labelling, cross-docking as the alternative to storing, putaway); the unit loads goods arrive in (pallet, case, each) and why the change from one to another is where labour goes; storage media and when each is used (floor, pallet rack, shelving, flow rack, automated storage at the level of a mention); reserve and forward pick locations and the reasoning for a forward pick area; replenishment and what triggers it; what the warehouse system does at each step (directed tasks, location choice, confirmation by scan) and what data it needs to do it; how each step commonly fails; the inbound measures the benchmark surveys report (their definitions, not their values unless read and dated); what differs for date-coded and temperature-controlled goods. Unit SCE5, The floor: people, devices and machines. Cover: the roles in a warehouse one by one (operator, team lead or supervisor, inventory controller, planner, site manager): what each is measured on, the pace and the physical conditions of the work, and what each does when the system is wrong; shifts, temporary and seasonal staff, and how long training takes, only as far as a source supports a figure; the devices (handheld scanners, vehicle-mounted terminals, wearables, voice, desktops) as working conditions, with what the research on human factors in order picking has studied and what it has not; labour management: engineered standards, performance tracking and incentives, and what the research found about incentives and picking methods, with samples; gamification as vendors describe it, graded as vendor-reported unless an independent test is opened; the machinery a warehouse system has to work with (conveyors and sorters, goods-to-person systems, mobile robots), from the review literature, at the level of what each changes for the people and for the software; the control software between the warehouse system and the machines, and where the line between them is drawn and disputed. Deliverable: five role cards (measured on, conditions, device, top tasks, what goes wrong, what they do about it, what they would ask of the software), built in full for named staff of Corvane and Ostrel Foods from the case register; then the card as a template.

Opus brief
Write one unit of a self-study course in the long-form report format of the Learning Project's markdown courses: a long-form report in Markdown. Length is whatever the topic needs (2,000–6,000 words); do not pad and do not cut to a target. Quality over quantity: a shorter unit whose every claim was opened and checked is better than a longer one. Reader: someone who builds, sells or implements order, warehouse or transport software, or is moving into that work: a product manager, a designer, an engineer, an implementer or a solutions consultant; experienced in their own craft, with no operations background; has never worked a shift in a warehouse or tendered a load; reads in order (each unit assumes the ones before it), reads on a phone, checks the citations, and wants genuine depth rather than a primer. Address the reader as 'you'. This course is written to be shared with anyone; it is an exception to the Learning Project's single-reader rule (decided 19 Nov 2026). Nothing about the Project's owner, his employer, his role or his work appears in it. Frame: assume no logistics vocabulary: define each term on first use and add it to the unit's glossary lines. Teach no design, research, engineering or sales method; the reader has their craft. Teach the operation, the people in it, the software that runs it, who buys that software and what it costs. The course is for learning the domain. It is not interview preparation and no unit is framed as one. Out of scope: artificial intelligence features and any vendor's AI layer are not treated in this course; where a source raises them, the unit says so in one sentence and moves on. Vendors: no real vendor is assessed, ranked, compared or recommended in this course, and no real vendor's product line is mapped. A real vendor is named only in two ways: to attribute a public statement to the company that made it (dated, graded vendor-reported), or as an entry in a dated list taken from a named analyst report or an audited filing, every vendor on that list given alike. No vendor receives more space, a warmer word or a harder one than another; nothing from inside any vendor appears. Worked product examples use the fictional vendors of the case register (Halvern Systems and its two fictional rivals), labelled '(fictional)'; the reader is given the template and the method to map a real product from its public pages themselves. A vendor's description of itself is evidence of what it claims, never of what its product does. Evidence grades: every claim that carries a number, a mechanism or a recommendation carries one grade from the key set in SCE1: independent (peer-reviewed research; an industry association's survey with its sample stated; an audited company filing, for that company's own figures), analyst (a named analyst firm's report, with its date and the note that vendors license its reprints), vendor-reported (a vendor's page, release, case study or sponsored survey), practice (a standard textbook's or practitioner's account of how the work is done, with no measurement behind it). Give a number in its source's own terms, with whose figure it is, the sample and the year. Common claims: where a claim is common in the industry and its support is thin, name the claim and say what the evidence shows; the course's list of such claims is in the seed bibliography; say 'no independent source found' where none was. Operations pattern (SCE2 to SCE5, SCE7, SCE9, SCE10 and DD-SCE2): treat each step as the physical event, the people doing it, what the software does at that step, how it commonly fails, and the measure that shows it improved. Regions: say which region a practice, a document or a term belongs to where it differs (North America, Europe); do not present one region's practice as universal. Running case: three fictional operations: Corvane, a fashion retailer; Ostrel Foods, a food distributor; Tandric Logistics, a logistics provider. Their facts come only from courses/supply-chain-execution/notes/case-facts.md; label each '(fictional)' on first use in the unit; a fact the unit invents about them is listed in the notes so that it can be added to the register; the case is never evidence for a claim about the world; no case operation is ever said to use a real vendor's product; where a unit needs one to use a product, it is a fictional vendor's. Deliverables are built twice: in full on the case operation the brief names, then as a template the reader fills for a real operation or product, each blank saying what goes in it. Every unit ends its body, before the deliverable, with two short sections: 'On a site visit' (what to look for and what to ask, for the units on operations; for the others, what to ask a colleague in sales, services or product) and 'Self-check' (five to eight questions someone who runs that operation or owns that product would expect the reader to answer, each with a short answer drawn from the unit's sourced text). Safety rules that hold in every unit: no price, discount, revenue or win rate of a private company is stated unless that company published it; no real customer of any vendor is described beyond what a dated public source states; no legal, tax or employment-law point is stated as the reader's: it is put as a question with the body to consult. Tone: direct, no flattery, no filler, no motivational language, no 'not X but Y' constructions. Course goal: by the end, the reader can take any screen, feature or roadmap item in order, warehouse or transport software, say what physical work it stands for, who uses it and who pays for it, and argue from that what the product should do. Sourcing standard: every claim that carries a number, a mechanism or a recommendation is sourced where it is made; acceptable sources are peer-reviewed research; standard textbooks cited by edition and chapter; industry association reports and surveys with their sample and year; analyst reports, by firm, title and date, read in full, in a public reprint or in abstract, the unit saying which; audited company filings; standards bodies' own pages; and vendors' own pages, releases and documentation, labelled vendor-reported and dated; peer-review sites are evidence of what named kinds of user say, with the number of reviews stated, never of how a product performs; videos, podcasts, social-media posts, unsigned blogs, comparison and lead-generation sites, and press rewrites of a release are not sources, and may be named only as the claim under examination; every reference is opened before it is cited: found by a search, read at its publisher's page, its DOI address or the issuing body's own page, and recorded in the unit's source log with the address, what was read (abstract, reprint or full text) and the date; a claim that rests on an abstract alone says so; a reference that could not be opened carries no claim; the seed bibliography (courses/supply-chain-execution/notes/bibliography.md) is a starting list whose entries were checked for existence only, never an authority on what they say; a study's finding is stated with its sample, its setting and its design, and a correlation is called a correlation; a finding from one site or one industry applied to another is labelled as the unit's inference; no uncited figures; no invented names, companies, studies, quotations or numbers; if a claim cannot be sourced, say so and leave it out of the deliverable. A unit that reads as advice without evidence is a failed unit. Links are given as links (the DOI address for a paper), so the course bibliography can collect them. Figures: where a section's content has a shape, declare it as a fenced viz block of a kind kit/viz.py draws (one to four a unit is a guide; none where nothing has a shape); a figure adds no fact, and its caption says what it shows and where its data comes from. Where the unit depends on current facts (analyst reports and their vendor lists, market figures, company filings, published selection guides), search before writing and date the facts. End with the unit's deliverable, built out in full, not described.
