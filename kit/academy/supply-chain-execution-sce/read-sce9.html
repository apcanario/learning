011. Where orders come from

The channels. An order reaches the order system from one of four places, in this course's working list: a website (the retailer's own online shop), a store (a customer at a till, or a store associate placing an order for a customer), a marketplace (a third party's online shop on which the retailer sells, which passes the order on; no source on marketplace orders was opened, so this is a working definition) and a business customer (a company buying for its own use or for resale). Corvane (fictional), a fashion retailer, has 60 stores in four European countries and one website, with an order system for the website (case register). Ostrel Foods (fictional), a food distributor, takes orders by phone, by a web shop and from its sales staff, with a cut-off in the late afternoon (case register).

Consumer and business orders. The trade shorthand is B2C (business to consumer) and B2B (business to business). Gartner's companion report to its 2026 evaluation of distributed order management evaluates 14 vendors "across five use cases"; its abstract page lists, without labelling them, five headings that read as those use cases: B2B (Other), B2B (Wholesale/Distribution/CPG), B2B + B2C, E-Commerce, and Unified Commerce (Gartner, Critical Capabilities for Distributed Order Management, 12 Nov 2026, abstract) [analyst, abstract only]. (CPG is consumer packaged goods.) The evaluation's own abstract says "A convergence across B2B and B2C channels is shaping the current market" (Gartner, Magic Quadrant for Distributed Order Management, 12 Nov 2026, abstract) [analyst, abstract only]. The abstract does not say what converges.

The business order as a document. A business order is usually a purchase order (PO): the buyer's document stating what it orders, at what price, for delivery where and when. Business partners can exchange it by electronic data interchange (EDI), structured messages sent system to system. Two standards publish these messages:

  • X12, a standards body (which regions use its messages was left unsourced by SCE7 and SCE8, and is here). Its 850 transaction set "establishes the data contents of the Purchase Order"; it is not to be used for purchase order changes or acknowledgments (X12, 850) [independent, for what the standard says]. The supplier answers with an 855, which "acknowledges each line item": each line accepted, accepted with a change (price, a rescheduled date, a reduced quantity, a substituted product) or rejected; the buyer answers changes with an 860 change request, accepting them or cancelling (X12, Example 02: Purchase Order Acknowledgment with Minor Changes) [independent, for what the published example shows].
  • GS1's EANCOM (SCE7 left unverified whether IFTSTA is a UN/EDIFACT message, and this unit did not verify EANCOM's relation to UN/EDIFACT; SCE10 §4 verifies both: IFTSTA is a UN/EDIFACT message, and EANCOM is GS1's subset of UN/EDIFACT). Its ORDERS message specifies goods ordered "under conditions agreed between the seller and the buyer"; GS1 recommends one purchase order per delivery, location and time, and allows deliveries to several locations in one order, which it calls split deliveries, as well as blanket orders, call-offs and rush orders (GS1, EANCOM 2002 S3, ORDERS) [independent]. The response, ORDRSP, can show the supplier's acceptance "of all, or part, or none" of the order and the changes it needs, which the buyer acknowledges with a new order referring to the original (GS1, EANCOM 2002 S3, ORDRSP) [independent].

What the system does at this step. Takes in the order, checks it for completeness, and records it with its channel. ONET, the US Department of Labor's occupational database, lists among an order clerk's tasks to "Review orders for completeness" and to verify customer and order information (O*NET, Order Clerks, 43-4151.00, updated 2026) [practice]. Common failure: an order that arrives by phone or e-mail and is keyed by hand, or not recorded at all; Ostrel's substitutions agreed by phone and not recorded are the register's case (case register). Measure:* no source opened defines an order-capture measure; the course's working one is orders keyed by hand over all orders.

022. Availability: what can be sold, and where it is

The terms. Available-to-promise (ATP) is the quantity that can still be committed to new orders; available-to-ship is the quantity that can leave now (SCE1 glossary: the terms are named in Gartner's definition; the definitions are the course's). Gartner's market definition makes "Enterprise Inventory for available-to-promise and available-to-ship items in the supply chain" a mandatory feature of distributed order management, with inventory allocated on the basis of on-hand, in-process or in-transit stock (Gartner Peer Insights, "Distributed Order Management Systems (Transitioning to Distributed Order Management) Reviews and Ratings", mandatory features updated July 2025) [analyst]. On-hand stock is on a shelf now; in-process stock is being received, moved or packed; in-transit stock is on its way between locations, from a supplier or to a store (working definitions; the page names the three without defining them).

The physical event. None: availability is a calculation over records held elsewhere. At Corvane, stock sits in one distribution centre and in 60 stores, and a web order can be filled from either (case register, added from the Supply Chain Planning course).

What the system does, as working description: it adds up each item's records by location (the warehouse system's for the centre, each store's for the shelves, open purchase orders and transfers for stock on its way), subtracts what is already promised to open orders, and offers the rest to the website. A system can also hold back a quantity at each location so that a few miscounted units do not become broken promises. No source opened describes or tests such a reserve, so the course calls it an availability buffer and treats it as a working term.

How it commonly fails. Availability is only as good as the location records under it. Corvane's known trouble is web orders promised from stock the centre cannot find (case register); SCE4 §9 follows one such error from phantom stock (units the record shows and the shelf does not hold) to a broken promise. In the research SCE4 opened, store studies found 31% to 65% of records wrong, on different definitions, and no study reported a comparable share for a warehouse, so store and warehouse records cannot be ranked from it (SCE4 §10, as SCE1 C4 summarises it) [independent]. In one study of omni-channel retailers (retailers whose store and distance channels overlap from the customer's point of view; in multi-channel retailing they do not, and the channels are not physically linked: Hübner, Holzapfel & Kuhn's definition, 2016, p. 258) in German-speaking countries, fashion retailers explained that their many sizes and colours leave few units of each in a store, that items are tried on or put in baskets, and that misplaced items are hard to find (Hübner, Holzapfel & Kuhn, 2016, p. 279) [independent: interviews, see section 4 for the design]. The same study names real-time access to store inventory among what store fulfilment requires (p. 279) [independent]. So a store's record is a weak base for a promise: the store studies found between a third and two-thirds of their records wrong, and Hübner et al.'s fashion retailers gave reasons why (this unit's inference from SCE4 and Hübner et al.; no study opened compares store and warehouse records on one measure).

Measure. No source opened defines an availability measure. The course's working measure is the promise failure rate: lines promised that could not be filled from the location chosen, over all lines promised. Gartner's definition ties the system to "targeted service levels for order fill rate" (Peer Insights DOM page) [analyst]; order fill rate, orders filled completely as a share of all, is APQC's measure (SCE3 §12) [practice].

033. The promise: a date and a place

What is promised. A promise has two parts: a date (or a window) and a place (an address, or a store for collection). The system computes the date from where the goods are, the time to pick and pack there, the location's cut-off (the latest time an order is accepted for a given dispatch; SCE3 §8) and the carrier's transit time (working description; no source opened sets out the calculation).

What the research found about the promise itself. Cui, Lu, Sun and Golden worked with an online retailer selling customised photo products across the United States: they varied the delivery estimates shown on the site while keeping physical delivery speed unchanged, and compared outcomes by difference-in-differences (the change in outcomes where the estimates were changed set against the change, over the same period, where they were not; the abstract does not say how the two were formed). A faster promise raised sales and profits; it also raised product returns and lowered customer retention (Cui, Lu, Sun & Golden, 2024, abstract) [independent: one retailer, one product category; abstract only, which gives no effect sizes]. The promise is therefore a lever with a cost on both sides (this unit's reading of the abstract). Applying the finding to fashion or to Corvane is this unit's inference.

What the rules say, by region. Where an operation sells to consumers, the law sets a floor under the promise.

  • United States. The Federal Trade Commission's guide to its Mail, Internet, or Telephone Order Merchandise Rule says a seller must have a reasonable basis for any shipping time it states or implies, and, if it states none, a reasonable basis to expect shipment within 30 days (50 days where the buyer applies for the seller's own credit); if it cannot ship on time it must seek the buyer's consent to the delay, giving a revised date and explaining how to cancel; a required refund must be prompt and full, within seven working days for most payment methods, and store credit cannot replace it; the rule requires no records, but the seller bears the burden of proving it complies (FTC, "Business Guide to the FTC's Mail, Internet, or Telephone Order Merchandise Rule", September 2011, edited January 2025) [practice: the agency's account; the rule's text, 16 CFR Part 435, was not opened]. The guide does not say whether business buyers are covered.
  • European Union. The consumer rights directive requires the trader to deliver "not later than 30 days from the conclusion of the contract", unless the parties agreed otherwise; if it does not, the consumer calls on it to deliver within an additional period appropriate to the circumstances, and if it still does not, may terminate the contract and be reimbursed without undue delay (Directive 2011/83/EU, Art. 18(1) to (3), legislation.gov.uk's copy, corresponding to the consolidated text of 1 July 2018) [independent, for what the text says]. That copy applies no amendment made after 31 December 2020. The European Commission lists three amending directives: Directive (EU) 2019/2161, in application from 28 May 2022; Directive (EU) 2023/2673, from 19 June 2026; and Directive (EU) 2024/825, which the page says "will enter into application on 27 September 2026" (European Commission, "Consumer rights directive", read 27 Nov 2026) [practice]. Directive 2019/2161 inserts an Article 9(1a), under which Member States may extend the 14-day withdrawal period to 30 days for contracts concluded during a trader's unsolicited visit to a consumer's home or an excursion the trader organises, amends Article 9(2) to match, and does not amend Article 18 (Directive (EU) 2019/2161, Art. 4(8), legislation.gov.uk's copy) [independent, for what the text says]. Directive 2023/2673 does not amend Articles 9 or 18; its Article 1(3) inserts an Article 11a under which, "for distance contracts concluded by the means of an online interface", the trader provides a withdrawal function labelled "withdraw from contract here" or an "unambiguous corresponding formulation", "continuously available throughout the withdrawal period" (Directive (EU) 2023/2673, Art. 1(3), read in the text as adopted by the European Parliament and the Council, PE-CONS 37/23, Council of the European Union, 11 October 2023, not in the Official Journal text, which EUR-Lex returned as an empty page on 27 Nov 2026) [independent, for what the text says]. The Commission page gives its application from 19 June 2026 [practice]. Whether Directive 2024/825 changes Articles 9 or 18 was not confirmed on 27 Nov 2026 (not opened). Member States transpose a directive into national law; the national text was not opened.

Which rule binds a given operation, and what it requires of the order record, is a question for the operation's legal advisers and the national consumer authority. What both sources ask of the software, as this unit's reading: a promised date with a basis behind it, a record of when the promise was made and changed, and a way to tell the customer of a delay and to cancel and refund.

044. Sourcing: choosing the location that ships

The decision. Sourcing (in order management, also called order routing or allocation) chooses which location fills each line of an order. Gartner's definition makes "Order Orchestration that optimizes order routing and inventory allocation" mandatory, and describes it as determining "the best fulfillment location based on service level and cost components", using business rules configured in a hierarchy (the page's definition paragraph) so that fulfilment can be optimised by "channels, consumers, geographies and other criteria" (Peer Insights DOM page, July 2025) [analyst]. The page does not list the rules.

The rules, as the course's working list; no source opened lists them, so this is the course's working description, with no source behind it: exclude locations that cannot ship the item or the delivery method; prefer a location that holds every line, to avoid a split, and, for a centre-first retailer, try the distribution centre before any store; prefer the nearest location to the address, or the cheapest to ship from; protect a store's last units, or its display stock; cap how many orders a store takes a day; and break ties by stock level or by the date stock is expected.

What the research on online fulfilment decisions found.

  • Cost now against cost later. Acimovic and Graves, working with an online retailer, frame the decision as where each item ships from, by which shipping method, and whether a multi-item order is split. A rule that takes the cheapest option for each order ignores what that choice does to the stock left for later orders. Their heuristic adds to the immediate outbound shipping cost an estimate of future shipping costs, taken from the dual values of a transportation linear program; on industry data it captured 36% of the gap to a clairvoyant benchmark (one that knows all future orders), for outbound shipping costs about 1% lower, with no loss of customer service and no rise in holding cost, and the partner has implemented the metric and a version of the heuristic, which it applies to every fulfilment decision for its North American stock-keeping units (Acimovic & Graves, 2015, abstract) [independent: one retailer's data; abstract only].
  • Reconsidering the decision. An earlier MIT working paper with Graves as co-author calls real-time assignment of orders to fulfilment centres or drop shippers "necessarily myopic", because it cannot see future orders or replenishments, and studies re-evaluating assignments periodically with heuristics that minimise the total number of shipments, tested on a major e-tailer's data (Xu, Allgor & Graves, 2005, MIT IMST working paper, abstract) [practice: a working paper; its 2009 journal version could not be opened]. A drop shipper is a supplier that ships direct to the retailer's customer.
  • Where retailers ship from. In Hübner, Holzapfel and Kuhn's exploratory study of 28 omni-channel retailers (non-food, headquartered in German-speaking countries; 43 managers interviewed over about six months, plus five logistics providers), nearly three-quarters supplied store and customer orders only through distribution centres, nine of ten used one central centre for online orders, and about one-tenth regularly shipped online orders from stores; integrated centres (one stock for stores and online) and separate ones were equally common (2016, pp. 267–268, 274, 277–278) [independent: interviews, 28 retailers, one region, not quantified by design]. Ishfaq, Defee, Gibson and Raja, from 50 interviews with supply chain executives of large retailers and secondary data, found online sales, the size of the distribution network, sales associates per store and years in online retail strongly associated with the fulfilment method a retailer used, and retailers with large store networks integrating store and centre inventory (2016, abstract) [independent: an association; abstract only].

The trade-offs, as this unit's inference from these sources and SCE4 §9:

  • Cost against speed. The nearest location ships fastest; the deepest stock or the cheapest rate may be further away. Acimovic and Graves add a third term: what this order's choice costs later orders.
  • Splitting. A split shipment sends one order in more than one delivery (SCE4 glossary). It can keep the promise date when no single location holds every line, at the cost of two shipments, and the customer receives two parcels at two times (SCE4 §9).
  • Store against centre. A store is close to the customer, holds a record the store studies found often wrong (section 2), and is a less efficient place to pick than a centre in Hübner et al.'s account (section 6).

Corvane's sourcing decision for one web order, drawn from the working rules above:

What the system does. Applies the rules to each line, records the location chosen and the reason, and sends the line to that location (step 5). Common failure: the rule picks a store whose record shows a unit the shelf does not hold, and the line comes back (sections 2 and 6). Measure: the course's split rate, orders sent in more than one shipment over all orders, and outbound shipping cost per order, the measure Acimovic and Graves's heuristic lowered [independent, abstract].

055. Backorders and substitutions

When no location can fill a line by the promised date, the order system has three answers: wait, offer something else, or cancel the line.

Backorder. A backorder is an ordered line waiting for stock (SCE4 glossary); WERC, the warehousing benchmark association, names backorders as a percentage of total lines among its survey measures (SCE3 §12, SCE4 §10) [practice]. For a consumer order, a backorder is a delay to the promise, and the rules of section 3 apply: in the FTC's account, the seller seeks the buyer's consent to the delay and offers cancellation with a prompt refund [practice]; under the EU directive, delivery after 30 days or the agreed date opens the consumer's route to termination (Art. 18) [independent]. For a business order, the supplier reports the change in its acknowledgment: in X12's published example a line is "accepted but the quantity has been changed (reduced)" or accepted with its required-by date rescheduled, and the order is held until the buyer answers (X12 example 02) [independent]. In EANCOM, the response can accept part of the order (ORDRSP) [independent].

Substitution. A substitution sends a different item in place of the one ordered. In business ordering it is a recognised line outcome: X12's example shows a line accepted with "a line item substitution", a different part number, which the buyer accepts or rejects with its 860 (X12 example 02) [independent]. In consumer ordering, the one study opened on it is Hoang and Breugelmans's simulated online purchase experiment with UK online grocery shoppers (3,405 households in two waves, a hypothetical retailer): shoppers accepted a substitute more often when it matched the attribute that dominates the category (flavour in categories whose products differ on attributes that cannot be ranked, such as cereals or crisps; brand in categories whose products can be ranked by quality, such as margarine or ketchup: the authors' horizontally and vertically differentiated categories), and much more often when they had bought the substitute before, 73.05% against 39.80% in the first wave and 84.04% against 56.24% in the second (Hoang & Breugelmans, 2023, full text) [independent: a simulated purchase, not field data; the authors note the hypothetical setting could bias answers]. The study measures acceptance only, not cancellations, returns or whether customers came back. That a customer's purchase history should drive the substitute offered is this unit's inference from it, for grocery.

Case. Ostrel Foods (fictional) shows the business side: substitutions agreed by phone and not recorded are one of its known troubles (case register), so the order record says one item and the delivery carries another. Corvane's (fictional) register gives no substitution practice.

What the system does. Holds the line with an expected date and tells the customer (backorder); offers or applies a substitute under a rule or with the customer's consent, and records both items (substitution); or cancels the line and refunds. Common failure: a substitute applied with no record, as at Ostrel, so that invoicing, returns and the next availability check all read the wrong item (this unit's inference). Measure: backorders as a percentage of total lines (WERC) [practice]; substitutes accepted over substitutes offered (the course's).

066. Store fulfilment: collect in store and ship from store

The two services. Collect in store (also click-and-collect, or buy online, pick up in store, BOPS): the customer orders online and collects at a store. The goods come either from the store's own shelf or from a distribution centre delivered to the store. Click-and-reserve is the variant in which the store holds items from its own stock for the customer to see and pay for. Ship from store: a store picks an online order from its shelves and ships it to the customer's address. (Definitions as Hübner et al. 2016 use the services, sections 6.2.2 ("Retailers' outlets") and 6.3 ("Archetypes of omni-channel destination concepts"), page range not confirmed [independent]; the names vary by retailer.)

How common they were. In Hübner, Holzapfel and Kuhn's 28 retailers, almost 70% had store pickup, about one-fifth used click-and-reserve from store stock, and about one-tenth regularly shipped online orders from stores; all retailers' same-day orders shipped from stores, though only about one-fifth prioritised same-day delivery (pp. 278–279, 282) [independent: interviews, German-speaking countries, non-food]. Retailers usually charged no shipping fee for store pickup, to bring customers into the stores, and former mail-order retailers saw it as costly, bearing the shipping cost instead of earning a fee for home delivery; one fashion retailer called it its "major growth driver" (p. 282) [independent: the authors' account and an interviewee's view].

What it asks of a shop. From the same study, store fulfilment needs extra handling effort by store staff, dedicated space for picking and packing that is then not available to show goods, real-time access to store inventory and an integrated ERP system; pickup adds in-store handling effort and, where the retailer charges no fee for it, a shipping cost the customer would have paid for home delivery (p. 279; for pickup, section 6.3, p. 282, page of the handling-effort point not confirmed) [independent]. The authors judged in-store picking less efficient than picking in a distribution centre, and a logistics manager at a department store retailer told them: "In-store picking is our main cost driver" (p. 279) [independent: an interviewee's statement]. The study did not assess who performs the operations, or their profitability (p. 291) [independent], so it gives no time per order and no cost per order. Ishfaq et al. found the number of sales associates per store among the attributes strongly associated with which fulfilment method a retailer used (2016, abstract) [independent: an association, abstract only].

What it asks of the staff. O*NET's Retail Salespersons occupation, with "Sales Associate" among its sample titles, lists 24 tasks, none of them about online orders; the nearest are to "Place special orders or call other stores to find desired items" and to "Exchange merchandise for customers and accept returns" (O*NET, 41-2031.00, updated 2026) [practice]. In the US occupational description, then, picking and shipping web orders is outside the associate's listed work (this unit's reading). At Corvane (fictional), store staff are asked to ship web orders with no process for it, and Freya is the store associate who ships web orders from a store (case register).

077. What sharing availability with customers was found to do

The study. Gallino and Moreno studied a retailer's introduction of buy online, pick up in store, using the retailer's own data. After the service started, online sales fell and store sales and store traffic rose. They identify two effects: cross-selling, as collecting customers bought more in the store, and channel shift, as some online customers moved to the stores and non-customers became store customers. They attribute the shift to more research online, purchase offline (ROPO) behaviour, supported by changes in cart abandonment and conversion rates in both channels, and argue that analysing one channel's data alone can mislead (Gallino & Moreno, 2014, abstract, read at RePEc) [independent: one retailer; abstract only, read at a bibliographic index because the publisher's page refused the fetch]. The paper's title calls the mechanism sharing reliable inventory availability information; that a customer who sees the local store holds the item goes and buys it there is this unit's reading of the title.

The setting. Kellogg Insight, the Kellogg School's publication, described the study in July 2013, before the paper appeared: a home-goods retailer (housewares, home accessories, furniture) with more than 80 stores in the United States and Canada, which began offering buy online, pick up in store across the US in October 2011, while its four Canadian stores did not; one year of data; two comparisons, shoppers near a store against shoppers far from one, and US stores against Canadian stores (Kellogg Insight, "Shop Online, Pick Up in Store?", 1 July 2013) [practice: the school's account; no percentages given]. Total sales rose, as the store gains exceeded the online decline (same page) [practice].

What it does and does not show, as this unit's reading: one home-goods retailer in North America, studied by comparing groups with and without the service (in Kellogg Insight's account); it shows what offering the service, and with it store availability, did to where customers bought, and says nothing of what a wrong store record does (section 2). Applying it to Corvane (fictional), a fashion retailer in Europe, is inference. The same availability figure that sources an order (section 4) is also a message to the customer, and a wrong one sends a customer to an empty shelf.

088. Changes and cancellations after the order

What can change. After the order is placed, the customer may change the address, the delivery method, a size, or a quantity, or cancel a line or the whole order; the seller may change the date, split the order, substitute or cancel (sections 3 and 5). A cancellation is the end of an order or a line before it is handed over; after hand-over, the customer's route is a return (section 9). These are working definitions.

What the rules and standards say. In the FTC's account of the US rule, a buyer told of a delay can choose to cancel before shipment, and a required refund is prompt and full [practice]. In business ordering, a buyer's change travels as an X12 860 change request, accepting the supplier's changes or cancelling (X12 example 02) [independent]; in EANCOM, the buyer acknowledges a supplier's changes by sending a new order that refers to the original (ORDRSP page) [independent].

Why the step matters. 099. Returns, from the customer's side

SCE4 §7 took returns from the warehouse's side: receiving, grading, disposition. This section takes the customer's.

The right to return, by region. In the EU, a consumer has "a period of 14 days to withdraw from a distance or off-premises contract", and for goods the period runs from the day the consumer, or a third party other than the carrier indicated by the consumer, acquires physical possession of the goods (Directive 2011/83/EU, Art. 9(1), 9(2)(b), legislation.gov.uk's copy) [independent, for what the text says]. Where goods ordered in one order are delivered separately, the period runs from possession of the last of them (Art. 9(2)(b), same copy) [independent], so a split shipment (section 4) moves the end of the window. Article 16 lists exceptions to the right, among them goods made to the consumer's specifications or clearly personalised, goods liable to deteriorate or expire rapidly, and sealed goods not suitable for return for health protection or hygiene reasons once unsealed after delivery (Directive 2011/83/EU, Art. 16(c) to (e), legislation.gov.uk's copy, which showed no version statement) [independent, for what the text says]. The article on reimbursement (Art. 13) could not be opened, so this unit gives no refund period under the directive. This unit's searches found no US federal rule giving a right to return goods bought online, and opened none; US return terms are not treated here; whether a state law or the seller's own policy binds a sale is a question for the operation's legal advisers. In the EU, which Member State's transposition binds a sale is a question for the operation's legal advisers and the national consumer authority.

How retailers take returns back. In Hübner, Holzapfel and Kuhn's 28 retailers, all offered returns by parcel carrier and about half also accepted online orders returned in stores; almost three-quarters processed returns only at the distribution centre, fewer than 20% used a dedicated returns centre, and about one-tenth mixed store and centre processing (2016, pp. 285–286) [independent: interviews, 28 retailers, not quantified by design]; the authors also write that some fashion retailers post return rates of 40–50%, giving no source or measurement for the figure (p. 283) [practice]. Processing returns in the store puts the item back on sale faster and saves transport, at the cost of space, IT requirements and higher processing cost than in a centre, and needs the ERP synchronised so that store inventory is updated (section 7.3, Table 6 on p. 287) [independent]. The authors name a quick refund across all channels as the target (p. 290) [independent: the authors' recommendation, from interviews].

1111. What a distributed order management system is, as analysts define it

The market definition. Gartner defines distributed order management (DOM) "as the software that orchestrates and optimizes the order fulfillment process"; DOM "utilizes inventory throughout the supply chain network to deliver targeted service levels for order fill rate" and "provides optimal on-time, cost-effective order fulfillment", with orchestration relying on business rules configured in a hierarchy by "channels, consumers, geographies and other criteria", using available-to-promise and available-to-ship inventory levels, and tracking order status across the supply chain (Peer Insights DOM page) [analyst]. Its mandatory features, updated July 2025, are three (same page) [analyst]:

  • Order orchestration, which "optimizes order routing and inventory allocation" and determines the best fulfilment location on service level and cost;
  • Enterprise inventory, for available-to-promise and available-to-ship items, allocated on on-hand, in-process or in-transit stock;
  • Order visibility, tracking order status through the supply chain and communicating it to other systems and to customers.

The page shows no common features. It lists 71 products and shows no total count of reviews (read 27 Nov 2026) [analyst]; what reviewers there say is graded vendor-reported, because vendors can invite their own customers to review (SCE1 §7).

The 2026 evaluation. Gartner's Magic Quadrant for Distributed Order Management, published 12 November 2026, read in abstract on Gartner's own page: "Distributed order management systems help optimize and manage the customer fulfillment process"; "A convergence across B2B and B2C channels is shaping the current market"; and the research is for supply chain technology leaders evaluating vendors in the DOM market (Gartner abstract) [analyst, abstract only]. The page names no analysts and no document ID. Its companion, Critical Capabilities for Distributed Order Management, same date, "evaluates 14 DOM vendors across five use cases" (section 1) [analyst, abstract only]. Vendors license reprints of Gartner's reports (the course's working assumption, SCE1 §8); no reprint was opened here.

What the definition leaves out, as this unit's reading of the page set against sections 1 to 9: it names no promise date (only availability), no backorder or substitution handling, no store fulfilment work (the store appears only as inventory), no changes or cancellations, and no returns or refunds. A product that does all of these is still a DOM by the definition; one that does only the three mandatory features is also one. Gartner's execution glossary generally excludes order management from supply chain execution (SCE1 §1) [analyst], while it evaluates DOM in an evaluation of its own: the course places order management on the line between planning and execution, as SCE1 §1 did. No source opened describes a reporting or analytics feature for DOM.

16Self-check

  1. How does a business order differ from a consumer order in the messages exchanged? A business order is a purchase order, often sent by EDI (X12 850, EANCOM ORDERS), answered line by line with an acknowledgment that can accept, change (price, date, quantity, substitute) or reject each line (X12 855, EANCOM ORDRSP), and changed or cancelled by the buyer with an 860 or a new order; a consumer gets a confirmation and a promised date (section 1).
  2. Which stock does Gartner's definition say distributed order management allocates from? On-hand, in-process or in-transit stock, as available-to-promise and available-to-ship, across the supply chain network (sections 2, 11).
  3. What did Cui, Lu, Sun and Golden find when a retailer promised faster delivery without delivering faster? At an online retailer selling customised photo products across the United States, where the shown delivery estimates were varied, the faster promise raised sales and profits, and also raised returns and lowered customer retention (section 3; abstract only).
  4. What do the US and EU rules opened here require when goods cannot be delivered as promised? In the FTC's account of the US rule: a reasonable basis for any shipping time, 30 days if none is stated, a delay notice seeking consent with a way to cancel, and a prompt full refund. Under the EU directive: delivery within 30 days unless agreed otherwise, then an additional period, then the right to terminate and be reimbursed (section 3).
  5. What did Acimovic and Graves's fulfilment heuristic add to the cheapest-option rule, and what did it achieve? An estimate of the future shipping cost of each choice, from a transportation linear program; on industry data it captured 36% of the gap to a clairvoyant benchmark, about 1% lower outbound shipping cost, with no loss of service and no rise in holding cost (section 4; abstract only).
  6. What does store fulfilment ask of a shop, according to Hübner, Holzapfel and Kuhn, and how common was ship from store? Extra handling effort, space for picking and packing, real-time store inventory and an integrated ERP; about one-tenth of their 28 retailers shipped online orders from stores regularly, almost 70% offered store pickup, and one manager called in-store picking the main cost driver (section 6).
  7. What did Gallino and Moreno find when a retailer let customers buy online and pick up in store, and where? At one retailer, online sales fell and store sales and traffic rose, through cross-selling and a shift of customers to stores, attributed to research online, purchase offline (abstract only); in Kellogg Insight's account of the study, the retailer sold home goods in North America and total sales rose (section 7).
  8. When does the EU withdrawal period for goods start, and how do the 28 retailers studied take returns back? On the day the consumer, or a third party other than the carrier indicated by the consumer, acquires physical possession of the goods, for 14 days; all retailers offered parcel returns, about half accepted online orders in stores, and almost three-quarters processed returns only at the distribution centre (section 9).

The writer's opening note

SCE1 drew the order system at the top of the map: take the order, promise, source. This unit opens those three steps up and follows the order to the customer's door and back. Grades follow SCE1's key (section 10 there): every claim that carries a number, a mechanism or a recommendation ends with [independent], [analyst], [vendor-reported] or [practice]. A public authority's own account of a rule is graded practice, as SCE7 grades it, until flag F18 is decided; the text of a standard or a law is graded independent for what it says. Lines marked as this unit's inference or reading, or as working descriptions, carry no grade; they rest on the graded sources they name. Every reference was opened on 27 November 2026; the source log (sources.md) gives what was read at each.

Two limits hold through the unit. Order management has less research behind it than warehousing or transport, and much of this unit rests on the course's working description, with no source behind it: where a section does, it says so. And the analyst evaluation of this market, Gartner's Magic Quadrant for Distributed Order Management of 12 November 2026, was read only as its abstract page; the unit states nothing about it beyond what that page states.

The unit uses twelve steps for one order, numbered so that the deliverable and SCE10 can refer to them:

  1. Take the order. 2. Check availability. 3. Promise. 4. Source. 5. Release to the location. 6. Pick. 7. Pack and label. 8. Hand over (to a carrier, or to the customer at a counter). 9. Track and inform. 10. Deliver. 11. Return. 12. Refund. A change or cancellation (row C in the deliverable) can arrive at any step before 8.

Steps 1 to 4 refine SCE1's first three map steps. At a distribution centre, steps 5 to 8 are SCE3's outbound steps (release to staging and loading); steps 8 to 10 are SCE7's steps 6 to 8 (pick up, track, deliver). This is the course's own sequence; no source opened sets out an order lifecycle in these terms.
