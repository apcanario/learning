011. What a warehouse management system is, and how the definitions differ

The textbook's definition. Bartholdi and Hackman define a warehouse management system (WMS) by what it knows and records. It "knows about every item in the warehouse", receives customer orders and transforms them into pick lists, and tracks the assembly of each order (ch. 4 introduction) (Bartholdi & Hackman, Warehouse & Distribution Science, release 0.98, 2017) [practice]. Its most fundamental job is to record receipt of inventory into the warehouse and register its shipment out (§4.1) [practice]; to that it adds a stock locator system, which manages an inventory of storage locations as well as of product, "down to and including the forks of individual forklift trucks" (§4.2) [practice]. Their summary is one sentence: the core of a WMS "is a database of skus and a stock locator system" (§4.6) [practice]. In the same chapter they say that most systems manage transactions and do not optimise decisions; the user, or a consultant, chooses rules from the modules supplied, or the software is customised (§4.6.1, read through a summary of the passage) [practice].

The analyst's definition. Gartner's glossary defines a WMS as "a software application that helps manage and intelligently execute the operations of a warehouse, distribution or fulfillment center", and lists fifteen capabilities: receiving, put-away, stock locating, inventory management, cycle counting, task interleaving, wave planning, order allocation, order picking, replenishment, packing, shipping, labor management, yard management and automated materials handling equipment interfaces (Gartner glossary, "Warehouse Management System (WMS)", undated, read 24 Nov 2026) [analyst]. Gartner's review market page adds two things. "WMS operations natively exploit mobile devices along with bar codes" and potentially RFID (radio-frequency identification: tags read by radio) "to form the transactional foundation of warehouse management", which enables directed work; and Gartner splits core capabilities (to receive, put away, store, count, pick, pack and ship) from extended ones, "additional integrated functionality offered by WMS providers beyond core WMS", such as managing labour or optimising where inventory is located (Gartner Peer Insights, "Warehouse Management Systems Reviews and Ratings", features updated May 2026, read 24 Nov 2026) [analyst].

How they differ, as this unit's reading of the three:

  • Record or execution. The textbook puts the record at the centre (a database and a locator) and says most systems do not optimise. Gartner's definitions put execution at the centre ("intelligently execute", "directed work activity") and fold optimisation into the category.
  • A fixed core or a moving boundary. The textbook gives one core. Gartner's extended capabilities are defined by what "WMS providers" offer, so the category grows when vendors add modules. Labour, yard and equipment interfaces sit inside Gartner's glossary list; in the textbook labour and yard management are high-end features, and equipment interfaces are not on its menu (section 6).
  • The device. Only Gartner's review page writes the mobile device and the bar code into the definition. SCE5 §3 treats the device as a working condition.

For a product team the practical consequence, as this unit's inference: when a buyer, a seller and an analyst say "WMS" they may mean three different scopes. Ask which definition a requirements list, a contract or an evaluation uses before comparing two products on it.

022. The tiers of the market, as the analysts describe them

What the analyst material opened uses instead of tiers. You will hear products sorted into tiers (entry, mid-market, enterprise). The one analyst evaluation read here does not use that word. Gartner's Magic Quadrant for Warehouse Management Systems of 1 June 2022 (read in a reprint; vendors license Gartner's reprints, the course's working assumption from SCE1 §8) divides the market two ways (Gartner, Magic Quadrant for WMS, 1 June 2022, ID G00755323, reprint) [analyst]:

  • By kind of vendor. It says the market "breaks down into five types of vendors": "Application mega vendors", "SCM suite vendors" (SCM: supply chain management), "Specialist WMS suite vendors", "Material handling equipment/automation vendors" and "Independent WMS component vendors"; the first four are covered by the Magic Quadrant and the fifth is not (Market Definition/Description) [analyst].
  • By the warehouse. It grades warehouse operations on a complexity model of five levels, "with Level 1 being the most basic and Levels 4 and 5 being the most complex and automated"; it says "the preponderance of warehouse operations are Level 3 or below", and that warehouses at Level 3 and below "do not require, nor would they normally use, the most advanced functionality" (Market Considerations) [analyst]. The part of the report read does not define Levels 2, 3 and 4 one by one.

Two points follow, as this unit's inference. The analyst sorts by who sells and by how complex the building is; it does not sort products by the size of the customer, which is what "entry" and "enterprise" usually mean in a sales conversation. Customer size appears in two places: several vendor entries describe a vendor's fit for midmarket or small and midsize customers, and the Honorable Mention section names no vendor and points to a separate Gartner piece, "Midmarket Context", which was not opened (Vendor Strengths and Cautions; Honorable Mention) [analyst]. And because most operations sit at Level 3 or below, most warehouse operations, in Gartner's model, do not need the most advanced functionality that an evaluation of the most capable products rewards.

How big the market is, and how spread. The 2022 report forecast the market "to grow to over $4 billion by 2026", with a five-year compound annual growth rate "of over 13%" (Market Considerations) [analyst]. That is Gartner's forecast as of June 2022. This unit opened no later figure; the seed bibliography lists a Gartner market share analysis dated 20 May 2026 (A4), which was not opened. Bartholdi and Hackman wrote, in the 2017 release, that "there are over 300 WMS vendors in the US alone" and that the largest companies held less than 20% of the market (§4.4) [practice: the text gives no source for either figure]. Gartner's review market page listed 140 products on 24 November 2026 (Peer Insights) [analyst]. An evaluation covers far fewer: the 2022 Magic Quadrant gave strengths and cautions for 17 vendors (section 8), and its inclusion rules required, among other things, a share of revenue or customers outside the vendor's home region and sales and implementation resources in three or more regions, and excluded standalone component providers and vendors concentrated in one geography (Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria, read through a summary) [analyst]. So a vendor that sells only in one country, or only one module, is absent from the list by rule, whatever its product does.

The fictional vendors on these types. The case register gives three: Halvern Systems (fictional) sells order, warehouse and transport software and nothing else; Varnby Software (fictional) sells an ERP with warehouse and transport as modules of it; Kestrow (fictional) sells one warehouse system, to smaller distributors and online sellers on a single site (case register). On the 2022 types, Halvern is read here as nearest the SCM suite vendors, though it sells execution software only, Varnby as nearest the application mega vendors, its warehouse system a module of its ERP, and Kestrow as nearest the specialist WMS suite vendors, though it sells one system (this unit's placement; the register does not use the analyst's types). These are case facts and evidence of nothing in the world.

033. Deployment: cloud, hosted, on your own servers

Three ways of running a warehouse system recur in sales and in evaluations. The standards body's definitions give the vocabulary.

  • Cloud subscription, shared (multi-tenant software as a service). In NIST's definition of software as a service (SaaS), the customer "does not manage or control the underlying cloud infrastructure including network, servers, operating systems, storage", nor individual application capabilities, apart from limited user-specific configuration settings; and among the essential characteristics NIST gives for every cloud model, SaaS or other, the provider's resources are "pooled to serve multiple consumers using a multi-tenant model" (Mell & Grance, The NIST Definition of Cloud Computing, NIST SP 800-145, September 2011, section 2) [independent, for what the standard defines].
  • Hosted, dedicated. NIST's private cloud is infrastructure "provisioned for exclusive use by a single organization", run by the organisation, a third party or both, on or off premises (section 2) [independent]. Gartner's 2022 report names the same split for warehouse software: among the factors behind a vendor's position it lists "cloud architecture and deployment model options", for example "dedicated or multitenant cloud SaaS" (Market Considerations) [analyst].
  • On the customer's own servers (on-premises). The customer runs the software in its own building or data centre. Corvane (fictional) runs its warehouse system "about twelve years old on its own servers", near the end of its vendor's support (case register).

What each means for upgrades, from the sources opened:

  • Multi-tenant SaaS. Because the customer controls neither the servers nor the application's capabilities (NIST) [independent], the provider applies a new version to everyone on the shared service; the customer does not choose whether the code changes, only, at most, the settings it controls (this unit's inference from NIST's definition; no source opened states how warehouse-software providers schedule releases, beyond Gartner's 2022 note that some vendors' architecture "allows continuous upgrades" (section 10)).
  • Dedicated hosting. NIST's private cloud is for one organisation's exclusive use (NIST SP 800-145, section 2) [independent], so a hosted instance can run a version of its own (this unit's inference); whether a given provider lets a hosted customer stay on an older version is a contract question, unsourced here.
  • Own servers. The customer, or a partner it pays, installs each upgrade. In the case, Corvane (fictional) runs its system on its own servers near the end of its vendor's support (case register); the register does not say whether upgrades were skipped, and the case is an illustration, evidence of nothing about how often systems on a customer's own servers fall behind.

What the analyst wrote about the choice, in 2022: in Gartner's estimate (no sample stated), close to 70% of new customers preferred cloud "if the economics are reasonable", while "a little over a quarter of completed deployments are cloud, with around 8% multitenant cloud" (Context) [analyst]; and "the impact of deployment models has diminished as the majority of WMS vendors now offer some form of cloud strategy", while "there remains a continuing debate among vendors and buyers on whether multitenant SaaS is demonstrably better than dedicated cloud" (Evaluation Criteria) [analyst]. It also wrote that service "contributes to implementation success and overall customer satisfaction, as well as impacting upgrade cycles and TCO" (total cost of ownership) (Evaluation Criteria) [analyst]. Common claim C8, that cloud software means no more upgrades, is set against these sources in section 10.

044. Configuration and customisation, and why the line matters years later

The two words. Configuration changes what the software does by setting the parameters its maker provides: a rule, a threshold, a screen layout, a label format. Customisation (also modification) changes or adds code, so the software does something its maker did not ship. A peer-reviewed systematic review of ERP customisation (26 papers, from 1,542 database hits, 2024) describes configuration as "Parametrization": "Configuring functions through standard software parameters" (Abendroth, Bender & Gronau, ICEIS 2024, pp. 17–27, read at SciTePress) [independent]. The review reports that Brehm, Heinzl and Markus (2001) used "tailoring" to cover both configuration and modification and set out seven tailoring types, adding extended reporting, workflow programming and interfaces (Abendroth et al., section 2) [practice: Brehm et al. not opened here, reported by the review]. The boundary is not sharp in practice: the review's own definition of technical customisation takes in configuration changes as well.

Why the line matters to the customer years later. Three sources point the same way.

  • Light's two case studies of ERP projects found that "to avoid invalidating maintenance and development support from the ERP vendor", most organisations reengineer their processes to fit the software; some customisation is typically required; and while customisations can give real benefits, whether one is viable needs careful consideration "given its potential impact upon future maintenance" (Light, 2001, Journal of Software Maintenance and Evolution 13(6), 415–429, abstract) [independent: two cases, abstract only].
  • The 2024 review states that heavy technical modification leads to increased cost in implementation and in the maintenance phase after it, and that cloud ERPs, "especially SaaS solutions, often have limited opportunities for deep customization"; across 20 years of studies it finds "a shift from ERP code modification in on-premises systems to interface and integration customization" (Abendroth et al., sections 1, 5.5, abstract) [independent].
  • For warehouse systems specifically, Bartholdi and Hackman say that a WMS often outlives the language it was written in, so many combine several languages and are hard to maintain or customise, and that most vendors will customise for a price and usually keep the intellectual property in the customisation (§4.6.1, read through a summary; "Most vendors will customize their WMS for the right price" is their sentence) [practice].

Both studies are of ERP software; applying them to warehouse systems is this unit's inference, supported in direction by the textbook's one-paragraph account. What follows for the customer, as this unit's inference from the three: every customisation is a piece of code that must be carried through each later upgrade, tested again, and, where the vendor owns it, paid for again; and on a shared SaaS service, where the customer controls little beyond settings (NIST), much of what was once customised becomes either configuration or a separate program joined through an interface. A product that offers more configuration moves cost out of the customer's future upgrades; a product that needs customisation to fit an operation moves cost into them. SCE14 takes up how implementers decide, and SCE16 prices it.

055. The integrations every site needs

A warehouse system is never alone. Four connections recur at every site; for each, what crosses, and what a source says.

The ERP. The ERP (enterprise resource planning system; SCE1's working definition: the company's financial and master-data system) sends what should arrive and what must be shipped, and takes back what did. Bartholdi and Hackman: the receipt "drives the paying of bills to suppliers upstream", and the shipment drives "the sending of invoices downstream to the consignee" (§4.1) [practice]; WMS vendors have the advantage that they are "already connected to financial systems" (§4.5) [practice]. In SCE2 §7 the warehouse system receives the expected receipt (purchase order lines) from the ERP and needs the item master (identifiers, pack, dimensions). The textbook lists "EDI capability" (electronic data interchange, the exchange of business messages in standard formats between companies' systems) among advanced features (§4.3) [practice]. Varnby's (fictional) "No integration to build" is the case's example of a vendor whose warehouse system is a module of its ERP saying no integration is needed (case register; evidence of nothing about real vendors); SCE10 sets it against the seams.

Carriers. Goods leave with a carrier's label and on a carrier's manifest (the list of parcels or units handed to the carrier). Bartholdi and Hackman list "Carton manifesting" and "Trailer manifesting" among high-end features and "Parcel shipping" among advanced ones (§4.3) [practice]. Corvane (fictional) books carriers through each carrier's own portal (case register), so in SCE3 deliverable C its labels come from the portals, a gap a carrier connection closes (SCE3's reading). The carrier connection sits on the boundary with the transport system (SCE7, SCE8).

Automation. Gartner's glossary includes "automated materials handling equipment interfaces" in the WMS (glossary) [analyst]; its 2022 report lists "Automated material handling equipment (MHE) interfaces" among extended capabilities and "material handling automation integration" among the things it assesses (Market Definition/Description; Evaluation Criteria) [analyst]. SCE5 §7 sets out the layers between the WMS and the machine (WMS, WES, WCS, equipment) and the dispute over which owns release and task assignment. For the product, the integration is the line where that dispute is settled for one site.

Labels and printing. Every logistic unit leaving or arriving needs a label the next party can scan. In GS1's guideline, the SSCC (serial shipping container code) is "the single mandatory element for all logistic labels" (§3.2); usually "the shipper of the goods will create the logistic label"; a carrier or receiver that adds to a label is responsible for its own data (§9.1); a label holds up to three segments, for carrier, customer and supplier (§2.3); and the despatch advice (advance ship notice, ASN) should list each unit's SSCC and contents, so that a scanned SSCC can be matched with it (§§9.3.7, 9.5.3) (GS1 Logistic Label Guideline, release 1.3, July 2019) [independent, for what the guideline says]. The warehouse system therefore issues the SSCC, prints the label at the packing or palletising station, and sends the same number in the electronic notice; the printers themselves, and the format each takes, are a configuration task at every site (this unit's inference; no source on printer formats was opened). GS1 is a global standards body; which message names a region uses (despatch advice or ASN) was not checked here (SCE2 §3.1).

  • Labour on W24 (labour management, SCE5 §4).
  • Voice on W18 (voice picking, SCE3 §3, SCE5 §3).
  • Yard on W3 (yard management, SCE2 §3.2).

099. Placing a real product line on the map yourself

The course maps no real vendor's product line. It gives you the method and the form (deliverable C) to do it from a vendor's public pages.

The method, in this unit's steps (the course's own; no source tests them):

  1. Collect the pages. The product line's overview, each module's page, the documentation index or release notes if public, and any dated release about the line. Record each address and the date you read it.
  2. List the modules as the vendor names them. Copy the names exactly; note which are sold as separate products and which as modules, where the pages say so.
  3. Place each module's claims on the capability list. One row per capability a page claims, with the module that claims it. A claim with no capability on the list gets a row of its own, marked as new.
  4. Grade every row vendor-reported and date it. A vendor's description of itself is evidence of what it claims, never of what the product does (course brief; SCE1 §10).
  5. Mark the gaps. Every capability line no page claims is written as "no public claim found", which is different from "not offered".
  6. Check elsewhere what can be checked. An analyst report that covers the product, read and dated, can confirm that the analyst assessed it, at analyst grade; a peer-review site can give what a counted number of reviewers say, graded vendor-reported (SCE1 §7).

What such pages cannot show, as this unit's inference from the grade key and the sections above:

  • Whether the feature works as described, at what volume, or on which device. A page is the vendor's claim (SCE1 §7).
  • Whether a capability is standard, configured or customised. The pages rarely say whether a customer had a feature built for it; section 4 explains why that matters for every later upgrade.
  • Which deployment a capability needs. A capability may exist in the on-premises version and not in the shared cloud service, or the reverse; check that each page describes the version you are mapping.
  • Price. For a private company, no price, discount or revenue appears in this course unless it published it (course brief). Where you need one, write "no public figure".
  • Who uses it. Named customers on a vendor's pages are the ones the vendor chose (SCE1 §9).
  • Where its boundary with the vendor's other lines runs. Halvern's returns and carrier labels (section 7) show the kind of question pages leave open.

1010. C8: "Cloud software means no more upgrades"

The claim as it circulates. Cloud software means no more upgrades (course list, C8). One instance, named here only as the claim under examination: a trade-magazine article by Roberto Michel in Modern Materials Handling says cloud warehouse systems bypass "the need for big upgrade projects" and that vendors "regularly send out updates to the system to keep users on the latest software" (Michel, "The pluses propelling cloud warehouse management systems (WMS)", no date shown, read 24 Nov 2026). The same article says the software still changes, through updates. These quotations are as a fetch tool returned them to the writer; the page refused the fetch on review, so they are unverified.

What the sources opened show.

  • NIST's definition places control of the application with the provider in SaaS; the customer keeps only limited user-specific configuration settings (section 3) [independent]. The upgrade still happens; who performs it and who decides when move to the provider (this unit's inference).
  • Gartner wrote in 2022 that service affects "upgrade cycles and TCO" and that whether multitenant SaaS is "demonstrably better than dedicated cloud" is a continuing debate (section 3) [analyst]. That is an analyst's view; it reports no measurement. The same report's Market Overview says some vendors had rewritten their WMS on a microservices architecture "that allows continuous upgrades" in a multitenant cloud [analyst]; read with NIST, the upgrades arrive continuously from the provider (this unit's inference).
  • The ERP review finds that SaaS limits deep customisation and that customisation has moved to interfaces and integrations (section 4) [independent]. Each interface is something the customer must keep working through the provider's changes (this unit's inference).
  • Light's two cases found that a customisation needs careful consideration for its potential impact on future maintenance (section 4) [independent: abstract only].

What the evidence shows. No source opened says upgrades end in the cloud. The sources describe a change in who performs the upgrade, who chooses its timing, and what the customer must test and maintain (its configuration and its interfaces). No independent source was found that measures upgrade effort or cost for warehouse systems in the cloud against those on a customer's own servers. No independent source found for the claim.

14Self-check

  1. How do Bartholdi and Hackman define the core of a WMS, and how does Gartner's glossary definition differ? The textbook's core is a database of SKUs and a stock locator system, and it says most systems manage transactions without optimising. Gartner's glossary defines a WMS as software that helps manage and intelligently execute a warehouse's operations and lists fifteen capabilities, including labour, yard and equipment interfaces (section 1).
  2. What does Gartner's review page call core and what extended? Core: receive, put away, store, count, pick, pack and ship. Extended: additional functionality WMS providers offer beyond the core, such as managing labour or optimising the locating of inventory (section 1).
  3. Does the 2022 Magic Quadrant sort the market into tiers? No. It names five types of vendor (application mega vendors, SCM suite vendors, specialist WMS suite vendors, MHE and automation vendors, independent component vendors; the last not covered) and grades warehouses on five complexity levels; most operations are Level 3 or below (section 2).
  4. In NIST's definition of SaaS, what does the customer control? Neither the infrastructure nor the application's capabilities, only limited user-specific configuration settings. Pooling resources across consumers in a multi-tenant model is one of NIST's essential characteristics of every cloud model, not part of the SaaS definition itself (section 3).
  5. Why does a customisation matter at every later upgrade? Light's ERP cases found customisations need careful consideration of their impact on future maintenance; the 2024 review finds heavy modification raises maintenance cost and that SaaS limits deep customisation; Bartholdi and Hackman say vendors customise for a price and usually keep the intellectual property (section 4).
  6. What must the warehouse system do so that a scanned pallet label matches the electronic notice? Issue the SSCC, print it on the label (the one mandatory element), and list each unit's SSCC and contents in the despatch advice, so the scan can be matched with it (GS1; section 5).
  7. Which lines of the capability list do Halvern's (fictional) six modules meet, and what does the register leave open? Labour W24, Voice W18, Yard W3, Slotting W13, Automation Connect W25, Client Billing W26. The register names the core but not its functions; this unit assumes the core capabilities there, and leaves fifteen lines open, among them returns and carrier labels, which may sit in the order or transport line (section 7).
  8. A colleague says cloud software means no more upgrades. What do you answer? No source opened says so. NIST puts control of the application with the provider, so the upgrade moves to the provider; the customer still keeps its settings and interfaces working; no independent source measures upgrade effort in the cloud against on-premises (section 10).

SCE2 to SCE5 followed goods, records and people through the building.
