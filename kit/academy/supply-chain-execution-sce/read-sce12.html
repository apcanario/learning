011. The stages, and where the stage model comes from

A selection is the work a company does from deciding it may need new software to signing a contract for one product. This course names eight stages, in the order a buyer usually meets them: the trigger, the long list, the request for information, the request for proposal, the scripted demonstration, reference calls and site visits, a proof of concept where there is one, and negotiation and contract. The list and the order are the course's, taken from the brief; the sources below say how far the order holds.

The classic model is a sequence. A review of business-to-business buying by twelve authors describes the "BuyGrid" of Robinson, Faris and Wind (1967) as a sequential series of decision-making stages, and the classic view, which it credits to Robinson et al. and to Webster and Wind (1972), as covering information gathering, product evaluation and negotiation, and purchase and usage; it links that process to "creeping commitment", where each choice removes some alternatives (pp. 195, 200–201) (Grewal et al., 2015, read in full) [practice: a review with no measurement of its own]. The same review argues that modern buying is not like that: "These processes are contemporaneous rather than sequential" (p. 195) [practice].

The analysts' model is a set of jobs done in any order. Gartner's page on the business-to-business buying journey names six buying jobs (problem identification, solution exploration, requirements building, supplier selection, validation and consensus creation) and says the journey "doesn't play out in any kind of predictable, linear order" (Gartner, "The B2B Buying Journey", undated) [analyst: the page states no sample or date]. Info-Tech Research Group's page on running a request for proposal gives seven steps: identify need, define business requirements, gain business authorisation, perform the request for information or proposal, negotiate agreement, purchase, and assess and measure performance (Info-Tech, "Drive Successful Sourcing Outcomes With a Robust RFP Process", published 11 Aug 2020, last modified 24 Jul 2023) [analyst].

One ERP case shows the stages looping. Verville and Halingten followed one ERP acquisition at a holding company for a gas and electric utility and a non-utility energy business; it took about six months, and they found six iterative, recursive and interrelated processes, an approach the abstract calls "non-typical of their normal procurement practices" (Verville & Halingten, 2002, Management Decision, abstract) [independent: one case]. Their article proposing a six-stage model of the buying process for ERP software (Industrial Marketing Management 32(7), 2003; DOI 10.1016/S0019-8501(03)00007-5, as a library record gives it) was not opened at its publisher's page and carries nothing here. An empirical study comparing ERP selection in small or medium-sized and large organisations addressed the packages considered, the weights given to selection criteria, the size and structure of the decision team, and the methods and effort used (Bernroider & Koch, 2001, abstract) [independent]; the abstract gives none of the results, so none is reported here.

What this unit takes from the three, as its inference: the eight stages are a checklist of the work a selection contains, and a useful order for a sheet, but a real selection runs several at once and goes back. A buyer can reopen requirements after a demonstration, or drop a vendor after a reference call.

022. The trigger

What it is. The trigger is the event that makes a company start a selection: a system reaching the end of its vendor's support, a new building, a merger, a failure the business can no longer absorb. It ends when someone senior agrees to sponsor the work and fund it. Info-Tech's steps put "Gain Business Authorization" before any request goes out (Info-Tech, RFP process page) [analyst]; Gartner's first buying job is problem identification (Gartner, B2B buying journey) [analyst].

What decides it. Whether a sponsor can get the money approved. Gartner's survey of 1,120 respondents in late 2021 found technology purchases funded at least partly outside IT in 74% of cases and shared funding delaying the budget for 75% of those sharing it (SCE11 §2, Gartner press release of 13 April 2022) [analyst]. No source opened measures what triggers warehouse-system replacements or how often each cause occurs: no independent source found.

What it hides. The trigger sets the question, and the question sets what every later stage compares. A company that starts from "our system's support ends" is choosing a replacement; one that starts from "web orders are promised from stock we cannot find" is choosing a fix to a seam between systems (SCE10), which a warehouse system alone may not hold (this unit's inference).

The case. At Corvane (fictional), a fashion retailer, the warehouse system is about twelve years old, runs on Corvane's own servers and is near the end of its vendor's support; web orders are promised from stock the distribution centre cannot find; and Corvane is deciding whether to replace the warehouse system and whether to buy order and transport systems from the same vendor [register]. Edda, vice-president supply chain, sponsors the replacement [register]. The second question widens the trigger: it brings the order system's owners into a warehouse decision (SCE11 §4).

033. The long list: analyst reports and peer reviews, as their publishers describe them

What it is. The long list is every product the buyer considers before asking any vendor for anything; the shortlist is the few it invites to propose or to demonstrate. The long list is built from what the buyer already knows, from colleagues at other companies, from analyst evaluations and from peer-review sites.

How the analyst firms describe their use. Gartner's page on using a Magic Quadrant (its graphic evaluation of a market's vendors, SCE1 §8) calls it "a first step to understanding the technology providers you might consider", and says "focussing on the leaders' quadrant is not always the best course of action", since it depends on how a provider aligns with your business goals (Gartner, "How do I use a Gartner Magic Quadrant?", undated) [analyst]. Its 2024 methodology note, read in abstract by SCE1, advises using an evaluation to narrow a list of providers and "don't base your decision on this evaluation only" (SCE1 §8) [analyst]. Forrester calls its Wave "a guide for buyers considering their purchasing options in a technology marketplace" (Forrester, "The Forrester Wave Methodology", last modified 28 Apr 2026) [analyst]. A vendor must buy reprint rights to post a Forrester Wave on its site, and the course's working assumption is the same for Gartner; a copy found on a vendor's site is one that vendor chose to post (SCE1 §8) [analyst].

Who is absent by rule. An evaluation covers only vendors that meet its inclusion criteria. Gartner's 2022 warehouse evaluation, the latest edition SCE6 could read, required sales and implementation resources in three or more regions and excluded vendors concentrated in one geography (SCE6 §2) [analyst]. A buyer whose long list was the 2022 Magic Quadrant's vendor list had left out every single-region vendor before looking at a product (this unit's inference from SCE6; the 2026 edition's criteria were not read). Common claim C10, that the vendor in the Leaders quadrant is the best choice, is not made by the analysts (SCE1 §11) and is tested in SCE17.

How the review sites describe their use. Gartner's Peer Insights invites buyers to "Choose solutions with confidence based on verified peer reviews and ratings" and says users go through a verification process (Gartner Peer Insights, "About us", undated) [analyst]. G2's scoring page says its Grid "helps buyers understand the competitive landscape for a category"; a product needs at least 10 reviews in a category to appear on a Grid report; vendors may ask their customers for reviews, but "vendors should not segment customers to solicit only positive reviews", and reviews gathered without an incentive weigh more in its scores (G2, "Research Scoring Methodologies", updated 26 Oct 2026) [vendor-reported: a review site where vendors can invite reviewers, SCE1 §7]. Gartner's own review rules let vendors invite reviewers through a custom link (SCE1 §7) [analyst].

What buyers report using. Three surveys, each from a firm with an interest in the answer:

  • Gartner Digital Markets, a business unit of Gartner that serves software providers as well as buyers (Gartner Digital Markets, "About", undated), surveyed 3,500 "global software buyers" (roles, dates and company sizes not given on the page; Gartner owned the Capterra, Software Advice and GetApp review sites, and on 29 January 2026 G2 announced that it had agreed to acquire them (G2, 29 Jan 2026) [vendor-reported]). In that survey, reviews were voted the most influential information source during shortlist research, 83% of buyers changed their initial list after research, and buyers actively engage with "only 2.5 vendors on average" (Gartner Digital Markets, "2025 Software Buying Trends Report", undated) [analyst].
  • Salminen, R. T. & Möller, K. (2006). Role of References in Business Marketing – Towards a Normative Theory of Referencing. Journal of Business-to-Business Marketing 13(1), 1–51, as the copy on the IMP Group's site gives it (its landing page lists Moller first) (abstract, on that page; the journal publisher's record was not opened). https://impgroup.org/2006/01/01/role-of-references-in-business-marketing-towards-a-normative-theory-of-referencing/
  • TrustRadius, a review site, surveyed 1,862 technology buyers and 444 vendors worldwide in January 2026: 74% of buyers use reviews to inform purchase decisions, and 13% used analyst reports to make purchase decisions, which it gives as a 63% fall since 2022 (TrustRadius, release of 15 Jul 2026) [vendor-reported: a review site's survey of its own subject]. Its headline finding concerns AI research tools, which this course does not treat.
  • Gartner surveyed 646 business-to-business buyers in August and September 2025: 67% said they prefer a buying experience without a sales representative (Gartner press release, 9 Mar 2026) [analyst]. The release's other main figures are on AI use, which this course does not treat, and on decision confidence: confident buyers were twice as likely to report a high-quality deal.

None of the three is about execution software, and none separates large enterprise purchases from small ones. What they show, read together and as this unit's inference: a long list is formed largely before a vendor's salesperson is involved, from sources the buyer reads alone.

What decides it. What the committee already believes, and which sources it reads. What it hides. Every product the sources do not cover, and the difference between a product the analyst assessed and one that would suit this operation (SCE6 §2: most operations, in Gartner's 2022 model, do not need the most advanced functionality) [analyst].

044. The request for information and the request for proposal

The two documents. A request for information (RFI) asks vendors to describe themselves and their products, so the buyer can learn the market; it commits the buyer to nothing. A request for proposal (RFP) sets out the buyer's requirements and asks each vendor for a priced, written offer the buyer can compare and can hold the vendor to. Info-Tech advises an RFI when you lack in-depth knowledge of the market, the solutions or the viable vendors, and an RFP when you have defined requirements, approved funding and enough time (Info-Tech, RFP process page) [analyst].

One RFI, read in full. Washington State's Department of Enterprise Services published an RFI for an asset management software product on 7 February 2024. It states that "Release of this RFI in no way obligates the State of Washington", lists draft requirements for reference, asks how the product is hosted and how it authenticates users, asks for the product's roadmap "for enhancements, upgrades, and end of life/support", and invites selected responders to a 50-minute virtual demonstration (Washington DES, "Request for Information (RFI) Survey: Asset Management Software Solution", 7 Feb 2024) [practice: one public buyer's document]. It asks for no prices.

Three RFPs, read in full or nearly. Each was published by a US public body and read in the copy its issuer posts:

  • The Alabama Alcoholic Beverage Control Board's RFP for warehouse automation with software integration (17 July 2024) covers warehouse system requirements (cross-docking, perpetual cycle counting), automation, integration with the board's ERP, a cloud-based solution, training, support, a completion window of 14 months, itemised fixed prices, five reference projects, and a six-year purchase-to-own agreement; it scores proposals in five criteria of 20% each: design fit, meeting the specification, cost per year, ability to meet the timeline, and experience with similar projects (Alabama ABC Board, "Request for Proposal: Warehouse Automation with Software Integration", 17 Jul 2024, §§3.2, 4, 5, 6.0, 7) [practice: one public buyer].
  • The Pinellas County Sheriff's Office's RFP 24-01 for an inventory management system (12 January 2024) asks for software, implementation, training, hosting and support; it weights functional requirements 40%, technology requirements 20%, implementation services 20%, vendor information 10%, and pricing with the total five-year cost 10% (§2.4.B); it may hold interviews (§2.4.A) and request a best and final offer (§1.14) (Pinellas County Sheriff's Office, "Request for Proposal 24-01, Inventory Management System", 12 Jan 2024) [practice: one public buyer; the copy read was truncated after its response instructions, so the requirement rows were not read].
  • The Mississippi Department of Education's technical requirements for food distribution software (Attachment A to RFP 4459, undated) run from functional requirements through data migration, implementation work, acceptance, security, and maintenance and support with an availability target of 99.98%; vendors answer each point "WILL COMPLY" or "EXCEPTION" (Mississippi Department of Education, "Attachment A to RFP No. 4459: MDE Food Distribution Software Technical Requirements") [practice: one public buyer].

Pinellas's weights, drawn:

What decides the RFP stage. The written answers, scored against weights the buyer set before reading them. The UK government's bid evaluation guidance says "Bids should be independently scored by at least two evaluators", that finance models should be evaluated independently of the technical specification, that a moderator who did not score should lead the panel to one agreed score, which "should not simply be an average", and that "Clarification is not an opportunity for bidders to improve their bids"; on price against quality, "It is not about the lowest upfront price", and an 80/20 quality-to-price split signals that quality matters most (§§3.3.2, 3.3.4, 5.1, 9.2.1, 9.3.2) (UK government, "Bid Evaluation guidance note", May 2021; the page does not name its issuing department) [practice: public-sector guidance, UK]. In the two RFPs with weights, price carries 10% (Pinellas) and 20% (Alabama) of the score (above) [practice].

Who sits on the panel. Two public bodies describe it. The Virginia Information Technologies Agency (VITA) names the business owner, the procurement lead and contract risk management as the key roles of an IT procurement's project and evaluation teams, and recommends that "these four corners of expertise" (business, technical, legal and financial) be represented on the evaluation team; outside experts "may provide information to the ET but cannot score"; the procurement lead "Leads the negotiation process", and the business owner "Identifies negotiation objectives and participates in negotiations" (VITA, "24.3.1 Putting together the procurement project team (PPT) and evaluation team (ET)", undated) [practice: US state guidance]. New Zealand Government Procurement says "The panel should be 3–5 people", may include technical, cultural, financial or legal expertise, and that each member declares conflicts of interest; experts can advise without being panel members (New Zealand Government Procurement, "Setting up the evaluation panel", published 5 Oct 2017, modified 9 Feb 2021) [practice: NZ government guidance]. Neither page mentions end users. Set against SCE11's seven roles, VITA names business, technical, financial and procurement members, as SCE11 §3 does, and New Zealand names technical and financial expertise; both add legal, which SCE11 has no card for; neither names a site manager or an outside integrator (this unit's comparison; how far public-sector practice describes a retailer's committee is this unit's inference).

What the RFP stage hides. How the product behaves. A written answer is the vendor's claim, graded vendor-reported however it is scored (SCE1 §10). A weight table rewards what it weights: in Pinellas's, 90% of the score goes to written answers and plans (functional 40, technology 20, implementation 20, vendor 10) and 10% to price, and none of the five lines is the demonstration (this unit's reading of §2.4.B).

055. What a requirements list contains, and what kind of product it rewards

A requirements list (requirements matrix, capability matrix) is the table at the centre of an RFP: one row per thing the buyer needs, one column for the vendor's answer. The lists opened share a form.

What the rows contain. The Alabama RFP's warehouse rows are capabilities named by operation, such as cross-docking and perpetual cycle counting, beside rows for integration, hosting, training and support (§3.2) [practice]. The Mississippi list runs from functional rows to data migration, an implementation plan, a user acceptance testing plan, security, support levels and service credits (Sections II to IX) [practice]. A city's ERP requirements matrix published by Birmingham, Alabama groups its rows by function (finance, grants, procurement and supply chain with an inventory subsection of 11 rows, human resources, payroll, projects, e-government services, reporting, general system) (City of Birmingham, "RFP Section 4: Requirements Matrix", 2024) [practice: one public buyer; the page shows no title, date or issuer beyond the address, and the copy read was truncated]. ARC Advisory Group's warehouse selection guide lists criteria under business goals, product functions and features, and service requirements for the project and the lifecycle, and names total cost of ownership, integration, usability, implementation support and training and the supplier's long-term viability among the issues (ARC Advisory Group, "Warehouse Management System Selection", undated) [analyst]; its second page of the same title adds sections of supplier analysis and supplier profiles, names no supplier in the text read, and invites readers to engage ARC (ARC, second address) [analyst].

How the answers are coded. The Birmingham matrix asks each vendor to mark every row "S = Standard", "F = Available with Configuration", "C = Available with Customization", "P = Available through a 3rd-party" or "N = Not Available" [practice]. Pinellas asks for one of four options per row, a blank or a double mark read as "No" (Attachment B) [practice]. Mississippi asks only "WILL COMPLY" or "EXCEPTION" [practice].

What kind of product it rewards, as this unit's inference from those forms:

  • Breadth claimed in the vendor's own words. A list scored row by row rewards the product that can answer yes to the most rows. A yes costs the vendor a mark on a form; whether the capability works at the buyer's volume is not on the form (SCE6 §9: a vendor's page cannot show it either).
  • The product built for the buyer's vocabulary. Rows are written in the buyer's words, and SCE6 §1 shows that a textbook and an analyst draw different boundaries round "warehouse management system". Forrester's abstract on warehouse-system functions says vendors' marketing messages and a lack of standardisation amplify the difficulty of telling which product solves a business problem (SCE11 §6) [analyst].
  • Configuration over customisation, where the codes separate them. A list that asks for S, F, C, P or N lets the buyer see which rows need code changes, which SCE6 §4 shows carry cost into every later upgrade [independent, for the ERP studies there]. A list that asks only "will comply" does not.
  • The integrated product, where integration has its own row. In one survey of Turkish manufacturing companies (sample size not given in the abstract), fit with the parent or allied organisation's systems was "the most important selection criterion", followed by cross-module integration, compatibility with other systems and vendor references (Baki & Çakar, 2005, abstract) [independent; ERP, one country].

What it cannot show. How the operation's peak, exceptions and people would use it (SCE5, SCE15). A list counts capabilities; it does not weigh how often each is used or by whom (this unit's inference).

066. The scripted demonstration: what it shows and what it cannot

What it is. In a scripted demonstration the buyer writes the scenarios and the vendor shows its product working through them, in place of the vendor's own standard presentation. Info-Tech's ERP demonstration template says it gives vendors "scenarios that prompt them to display how the tool will support" the buyer's particular needs (Info-Tech, "ERP Vendor Demonstration Script Template", published 10 Apr 2023, modified 15 Oct 2026, public page; the template itself is paid) [analyst]. An older Info-Tech note warns that many businesses neglect the planning demonstrations need, which "can be a very valuable input to the final selection process" when done properly, and lists the steps: choose which vendors to invite, set the length and the requirements to cover (use cases, scripts, bake-offs, that is, vendors run through the same scenarios one after another), prepare a "well-defined, time-boxed demo agenda", prepare the stakeholders, and build a scoring strategy and debriefs (Info-Tech, "Master the Enterprise Application Vendor Demo", A. Hay, published 18 Dec 2007, modified 3 Apr 2012) [analyst; the page is nearly twenty years old].

How the public documents place it. Washington's RFI invited selected responders to a 50-minute virtual demonstration (section 4) [practice]. Pinellas's shortlisted vendors may give a "virtual evaluation committee presentation" and "should be prepared to demonstrate all aspects of their service" (§1.13) [practice]. The UK guidance asks departments to "carefully consider the use of bidder presentations, interviews, scenario tests or site visits" and to set out in the procurement documents how each will be evaluated (§6.3.7) [practice]. In neither RFP with weights is the demonstration a weighted line of its own (section 4).

What a scripted demonstration shows, as this unit's inference from those sources: that the product can do the scenario, on screen, with the vendor's people at the keyboard; how many steps and screens the scenario takes; how the vendor answers questions it did not prepare for, if the script leaves room for them.

What it cannot show, as this unit's inference from the units named:

  • Volume and peak. A demonstration runs a handful of orders on a prepared system. Corvane's (fictional) web volume in peak is about four times a normal week [register]; nothing on the screen shows the system at that load.
  • The configuration behind the screen. The vendor configured the demonstration system. How much configuration or customisation the buyer's own operation would need is the question SCE6 §4 raises, and the demonstration does not answer it.
  • The seams. A scenario that stays inside the product cannot show a cancellation reaching released work in another system, or which system holds the stock figure (SCE10 §§2, 7).
  • The shift. The people who will use the system every shift may not be in the room; nothing opened says how often they are (SCE11 §5). A scenario watched on a projector is not a picker's shift on a handheld (SCE5 §3). In Howcroft and Light's case of one small consultancy choosing a customer relationship management package, salesmanship directed by the vendor or consultant at senior management's interests "marginalises user needs and ultimately secures the procurement of the software package" (Howcroft & Light, 2008, abstract) [independent: one case, not in a warehouse].

Common claim C13: "Buyers decide on the demonstration." In this unit's reading (no source opened states it in this form), the claim is that the demonstration, more than the written proposal or the references, is where the deal is won. What the sources opened show:

  • TrustRadius's 2026 survey (above) names product demonstrations and free trials, with user reviews and prior experience, among the most influential resources, and gives no percentage for demonstrations [vendor-reported].
  • Gartner Digital Markets reports that 62% of the 3,500 software buyers it surveyed say a product trial is their top factor in the final decision (section 3) [analyst]. A trial is the buyer using the product, which is not the vendor demonstrating it, and the sample's company sizes are not given.
  • Gartner's buying-journey page says buyers spend only 17% of their buying time meeting potential suppliers, and only 5% or 6% with any one supplier's representative when comparing several (Gartner, B2B buying journey) [analyst: no sample stated]. Every demonstration sits inside that share.
  • In the one conjoint study of IT managers' criteria seen (Keil and Tiwana, 2006, as Olson et al. report it), reliability and functionality had the largest effects and ease of use a smaller one (SCE11 §2) [practice, origin not traced]. In Baki and Çakar's survey, the top criteria were fit with existing systems, integration and vendor references (section 5) [independent]. Neither study measured demonstrations.
  • In the two public RFPs with weights, the demonstration carries no weight of its own (section 4) [practice].

What the evidence shows. No source opened measures how much a demonstration decides a selection of execution software, or of enterprise software in general. The surveys that name demonstrations or trials rank them among several influences, give no number for demonstrations, and come from a review site and from a Gartner business that served software providers and whose parent then owned review sites (section 3). No independent source found for the claim.

077. Reference calls and site visits, and why references weigh

What they are. A reference customer is a customer a vendor names to speak to a buyer (SCE11 glossary). A reference call is the buyer's conversation with one; a site visit (reference visit) is the buyer's committee going to see the product running at a reference customer's operation.

What the public documents ask for. Alabama asks for five reference projects of similar services, each with a contact and a summary of the software and system configuration (§4) [practice]. Pinellas asks for at least five clients equal or greater in size and complexity, each with the project's scope, dates, contract length and unanticipated delays (Section C.5) [practice]. The UK guidance lists site visits among the activities a department must set out in advance if it evaluates them (§6.3.7) [practice].

Why references weigh. In Baki and Çakar's survey, vendor references ranked fourth among the ERP selection criteria (section 5) [independent; one country; sample not given in the abstract]. A study of suppliers, based on 38 interviews with managers at four process and information technology companies, found that suppliers use references to gain status from reputable customers, to signal they passed a customer's selection, to "concretize and demonstrate complex solutions" and to "provide indirect evidence of their experience" (Jalkala & Salminen, "The Practices and Functions of Customer Reference Marketing – Leveraging Customer References as Marketing Assets", IMP Group conference, Marseille, 2009, abstract) [practice: a conference paper whose peer review the page does not state; supplier-side interviews, not buyers]. An earlier paper by Salminen and Möller (the IMP Group's page lists Moller first; the copy it hosts gives Salminen first) notes that "there is very little reported academic research on references" (Salminen & Möller, "Role of References in Business Marketing – Towards a Normative Theory of Referencing", Journal of Business-to-Business Marketing 13(1), 2006, abstract on the IMP Group's page) [practice: abstract only, on the IMP Group's page; the journal's record was not opened, so the paper's peer-reviewed form is not confirmed].

What they hide. The vendor chooses the references. Forrester's own method shows the pattern at the analyst level: "Forrester interviews or surveys the reference customers provided by the vendor" (Forrester, Wave methodology) [analyst]. A reference is therefore evidence of a customer the vendor was willing to put forward (SCE1 §9; this unit's inference). It does not show the customers the vendor left out, and a reference running a different flow (pallets, not single items; one country, not four) says little about the buyer's own (SCE1 §9: what would transfer). A site visit shows a running operation, at the reference's volume and with the reference's configuration, which is more than a demonstration shows and still not the buyer's operation (this unit's inference).

088. A proof of concept, where there is one

What it is. A proof of concept (pilot) is a test of the product on the buyer's own data or processes before the contract, run by the vendor, the buyer or both. VITA's page on the procurement and evaluation teams mentions "evaluation-phase tests/pilot programs", in which subject matter experts may assist the business owner (VITA 24.3.1) [practice]. None of the three RFPs read asks for one; Alabama makes the delivered system subject to any test the board may determine, which this unit reads as acceptance of what is delivered after award; the clause does not state its timing (§7.1) [practice].

What it shows and hides, as this unit's inference: it shows the product on the buyer's items, locations or orders, which no earlier stage does. It is usually narrow (one process, one flow, a sample of data), it costs both sides time, and who pays for it is a commercial question with no public figure. Gartner Digital Markets' 62% figure on trials (section 3) [analyst] suggests buyers value using the product themselves; it is not a measurement of proofs of concept in execution software.

099. Negotiation and contract: the plan and the partner are part of what is bought

What is negotiated. Price, and much else. The public documents show what else is put in writing before signature (each [practice: one public buyer]):

  • Price form. Alabama asks for itemised fixed prices and a firm fixed cost (§4.5, §5.12); Pinellas asks for a fixed price broken out by component, each marked one-time or annual, will not consider time-and-materials pricing, ties payments to milestones from kick-off to final acceptance, asks that software maintenance not increase during the first five years of live operation, and sets an initial term of five years (§1.15; Section B, 2.2.O to 2.2.R).
  • A best and final offer. Pinellas may request one from the shortlisted vendors (§1.14).
  • The implementation plan. Mississippi asks for a preliminary project management plan, a system design document, a data migration plan and a user acceptance testing plan with the proposal, and a train-the-trainer programme for three to five administrators (Sections IV, V, IX). Alabama sets a 14-month completion window (§5) and asks for profiles of the supplier's key personnel (§4). Pinellas weights implementation services at 20% of the score, as much as the technology (§2.4.B).
  • Who negotiates. In VITA's account, the procurement lead leads and the business owner sets the objectives and takes part (section 4) [practice].

Why the plan and the partner are bought too. A warehouse system arrives configured, integrated and loaded with the operation's data, or it does not run. Who does that work is part of the offer: Halvern Systems (fictional) implements through its own services team and through partners [register], and SCE11 §3.6 describes the integrator, the outside firm that implements alongside or instead of the vendor. ARC's guide puts implementation support and training, and service requirements for the project and the lifecycle, among the criteria (section 5) [analyst]. As this unit's inference: when the buyer signs, it buys a product, a plan, a team named in that plan and a price for the plan's work. SCE14 takes the plan into implementation.

What negotiation hides. The terms of other customers' contracts, and any price a private vendor has not published: no public figure. Which contract terms protect the buyer, and whether they are enforceable where the buyer operates, are questions for its legal counsel.

The case. At Corvane (fictional), Helene, chief financial officer, can veto on cost, and a procurement function, unnamed, runs the request and the contract (SCE11 cards 3 and 5, working). The register gives Corvane's peak as mid-November to early January [register]; when a go-live could fall is a question for the plan, not settled here.

1010. Why deals are won or lost: what the public evidence is

The evidence is thin, and most of it is told by vendors or by firms that sell to them.

  • Win rates and loss reasons of real vendors are not public. No source opened gives a win rate for any vendor of execution software, and the course states none for a private company (course rule). The "win/loss" reports this unit's searches returned were published by firms that sell win/loss interviews or sales software (working description of the search results); none was opened, and under the course's key they would be vendor-reported.
  • Regret. Gartner's survey of 1,120 respondents at manager level or above, involved in evaluating or selecting technology, in North America, Western Europe and Asia/Pacific, fielded in November and December 2021, found 56% reporting a high degree of regret over their largest technology purchase of the past two years, regret highest among those who had not yet started implementation, and high-regret purchases taking 7 to 10 months longer on average to complete; 67% of the people involved in technology buying were outside IT (Gartner press release, 12 Jul 2022) [analyst]. Gartner Digital Markets reports 59% of its 3,500 software buyers regretting at least one purchase in the past 18 months (section 3) [analyst].
  • Conflict in the committee. Gartner's survey of 632 business-to-business buyers, fielded in August and September 2024, found 74% of buyer teams showing "unhealthy conflict" during the decision, and buying groups that reached consensus 2.5 times more likely to report a high-quality deal (Gartner press release, 7 May 2025) [analyst]. That is a correlation in buyers' own reports, not a cause.
  • Selection as a social process. Howcroft and Light's longitudinal study of one small organisation argues against reading selection as a rational search for the "best technology solution" and shows how various actors shape it (Howcroft & Light, 2010, abstract) [independent: one case].

What follows, as this unit's inference. A deal can be lost to another vendor, to no decision at all, or to a committee that cannot agree; the public sources measure the last two only in buyers' general reports, not in execution software. Anyone who tells you why a particular deal was lost is giving an account, and the account is the seller's unless it came from the buyer.

12On a site visit

This unit is not about an operation. Its questions are for your own colleagues in sales and in services, about selections they took part in, because the answers are not public (sections 7 and 10).

  • Which stage did we lose most deals at last year, and how do we know? Ask whether the reason came from the buyer or from our own team (section 10).

  • Which scenarios do we decline or steer away from in a demonstration, and why? (Section 6.)
  • Who chooses our reference customers, how often is each asked, and which customers do we not offer? (Section 7.)
  • How often is a proof of concept asked for, who pays, and what was its scope? (Section 8.)
  • Who writes the implementation plan in a proposal, who is named in it, and how often does the plan change after signature? Ask services, then SCE14 (section 9).
  • When we lose to no decision, what does the buyer say happened? (Section 10.)

13Self-check

  1. Name the eight stages this course uses, and say why their order is a convention. Trigger, long list, RFI, RFP, scripted demonstration, references and site visits, proof of concept, negotiation and contract. Grewal et al. (2015) argue buying processes run at the same time, Gartner says the buying jobs follow no linear order, and Verville and Halingten found one ERP acquisition's processes iterative (section 1).
  2. How do Gartner and Forrester describe the use of their evaluations? Gartner: a Magic Quadrant is a first step to understanding the providers you might consider, the Leaders quadrant is not always the best course, and the 2024 note says not to decide on the evaluation alone. Forrester: a guide for buyers considering their options. A Forrester Wave on a vendor's site is one the vendor bought reprint rights to post; for Gartner that is the course's working assumption (section 3).
  3. What is the difference between an RFI and an RFP? An RFI asks vendors to describe themselves and binds the buyer to nothing (Washington's RFI says so); an RFP sets out requirements and asks for priced offers the buyer can compare. Info-Tech advises an RFP once requirements, funding and time are in place (section 4).
  4. What do the response codes of a requirements matrix tell you, and what kind of product does a row-by-row list reward? Codes such as Birmingham's (standard, configuration, customisation, third party, not available) show which rows need code changes; a list scored row by row rewards the product that can answer yes to most rows, which is not evidence of how it runs (section 5, inference).
  5. What can a scripted demonstration not show? Volume and peak, the configuration behind the screen, the seams with other systems, and a user's whole shift; in one case, salesmanship marginalised user needs (section 6).
  6. What does the evidence say about C13, "buyers decide on the demonstration"? A review site's and an analyst's surveys rank demonstrations or trials among several influences, with no number for demonstrations; Gartner says buyers spend 17% of their buying time with suppliers; no source measures the demonstration's weight. No independent source found (section 6).
  7. Why do references weigh, and what do they hide? Buyers ranked vendor references among the top ERP criteria in one Turkish survey; suppliers use them as indirect evidence of experience. The vendor chooses them, as Forrester's own reference customers are provided by the vendor (section 7).
  8. What, besides the product, does a buyer sign for? A plan, a named team and a price for the plan's work: the public RFPs ask for implementation plans, data migration and testing plans, key personnel, milestones and a completion window, and one weights implementation as heavily as technology (section 9).

The writer's opening note

SCE11 described the people on a buying committee. This unit follows them through the selection itself, stage by stage: what happens at each stage, what decides it, and what it cannot show the people deciding. Grades follow SCE1's key (section 10 there): independent, analyst, vendor-reported, practice. As in SCE5 and SCE11, a line that rests on no opened source is a working description: the course's own account, graded nothing and never used as evidence. Case facts are marked [register]; facts this unit adds to the case are listed in the notes. Every web page cited was read on 28 November 2026 unless another date is given.

Three limits hold through the unit.

  • No study of a warehouse, transport or order system selection was found. The research opened is on enterprise resource planning (ERP) and other packaged software, or on business-to-business buying in general, and most of it was read in abstract only. Applying it to execution software is this unit's inference.
  • The published requirement lists opened are public bodies' tenders, all from the United States; the UK and New Zealand pages read are guidance on evaluation, not tenders. A public body buys under procurement rules that a private retailer such as Corvane (fictional) does not follow; what transfers is the shape of the documents; the rules stay with the public body (this unit's inference). Which rules bind a given company is a question for its legal counsel.
  • The free selection templates found were published by software vendors, by integrators or by trade magazines, which this course does not use as sources (SCE11 §6). The analyst firms' templates are paid, so only their public pages were read; no Gartner buying guide, toolkit or market guide was read here (flag F130). Robinson, Faris and Wind (1967) and Webster and Wind (1972) were not opened either (flag F129); section 1 gives them as Grewal et al. (2015) describe them.

The brief

Aim: describe a selection stage by stage, what decides each and what each cannot see
Takes: SCE6, SCE11.
Gives: the stages SCE14 follows into implementation and SCE15 sets against the shift.

Unit SCE12, The sale. Search before writing. Cover: the stages of a selection (the trigger, the long list, the request for information and for proposal, the scripted demonstration, site visits and reference calls, a proof of concept where there is one, negotiation and contract), from the research on enterprise software buying and from published selection guides, each attributed; what a requirements list contains, read on at least two published templates, and what kind of product it rewards; what a scripted demonstration shows and what it cannot; the place of analyst reports and peer reviews in a long list, as the analyst firms and the review sites themselves describe their use; references and why they weigh; the implementation plan and the partner as part of what is bought; what public evidence exists on why deals are won or lost, said plainly to be thin and mostly vendor-told; what a product team can change at each stage and what it cannot. End the body with the questions the reader should take to their own sales and services colleagues, since the answers are not public. Deliverable: a stage-by-stage sheet (stage, who is in it, what decides it, what it hides, what a product team can affect, what to ask internally), built in full for Corvane replacing its warehouse system; then the template.
