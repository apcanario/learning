COURSE SCE · Supply Chain Execution (public) · IN PROGRESS

Supply Chain Execution for Product Teams

Learning the domain: how warehouses, transport and orders run, who works in them, how the software that runs them is bought, and what it costs. Operations first, then people, then money, with the warehouse given the most room; AI features are out of scope, no real vendor is assessed, and the worked examples use three fictional operations and three fictional vendors. Eighteen core units in an opening and three parts, and two deep dives.

What you will be able to do

By the end, the reader can take any screen, feature or roadmap item in order, warehouse or transport software, say what physical work it stands for, who uses it and who pays for it, and argue from that what the product should do.

  • Outcome 1 · Operations. Walks one order from promise to delivery and names, at every step, the physical event, the system, the user role and the common failure, without notes (SCE1 to SCE10, DD-SCE2).
  • Outcome 2 · Buyers and users. For a given feature, writes a two-sided case: each buyer card's reason to pay and each role card's reason to use or avoid it, with the grade of the evidence (SCE5, SCE8, SCE9, SCE11 to SCE15, DD-SCE1).
  • Outcome 3 · The market and the money. Builds a competitor sheet and a total-cost model for a real market from public sources, and writes a product strategy memo that rests on them (SCE6, SCE16, SCE17, SCE18).

Every unit is sourced to operations research, standard textbooks, industry surveys, analyst reports, audited filings and vendors' own dated pages, grades every claim (independent, analyst, vendor-reported, practice), assesses no real vendor, and ends with a deliverable built in full on the fictional case and then as a template.

Frame

Pick

Operations first, then people, then money. Warehouse gets the most room.

  • A screen cannot be improved without knowing the physical event behind it.
  • Product work needs buyers and costs, not only workflows.
  • The warehouse has the deepest research and the most roles.

Override if: readers turn out to work mostly on transport or orders; then Part 2 grows and Part 1 loses a unit.

  • What the course is for. Learning the domain: how warehouses, transport and orders run, who works in them, how the software that runs them is bought, and what it costs. It is the first course of a new public track, Supply Chain. No unit is interview preparation.
  • Out of scope. AI features and any vendor's AI layer. They sit on top of the three systems; the course teaches the systems and the work underneath them.
  • No real vendor is assessed. Worked product examples use fictional vendors. Real vendors appear only as the source of a public statement or as entries in a dated list from an analyst report or a filing, all alike. The reader gets the method and the templates to map and compare real products themselves.
  • Where the evidence pushes back. The operations research on warehouses is solid and old: much of it predates e-commerce at scale. Transport and order management have less of it. Almost everything about the software itself comes from vendors or from analysts whose reports vendors license. Private vendors publish no prices, revenue by product or win rates. Why deals are won or lost has almost no independent evidence. The course grades every claim, so "vendor-reported" and "practice" will appear often, and some sections will say "no independent source found".
