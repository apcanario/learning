011. What a face or a posture tells you about a feeling

The popular claim: "A coach can read what an athlete feels from the face." SP1 set this claim against its evidence and handed the rest to this unit. The review behind SP1's verdict examined whether particular configurations of facial movement reliably and specifically signal six emotions (anger, disgust, fear, happiness, sadness and surprise) across people, situations and cultures, drawing on studies of adults in industrialised and in small remote cultures, of infants and children, and of people blind from birth. Its authors conclude that people do sometimes scowl when angry or smile when happy, but less often than the common view holds, that how people express emotion varies substantially across cultures and situations, and that a facial configuration such as a scowl "often communicates something other than an emotional state" (Barrett et al., 2019, publisher's summary page and the first part of the full text read on the authors' laboratory site). They recommend neutral, descriptive words for what is seen ("a smile", "a scowl") in place of emotion labels where the evidence does not support the label. The review sits outside sport.

Posture. In a set of experiments on real-life photographs of people at peaks of intense positive and negative emotion, observers could tell positive from negative situations from the body alone and could not from the face alone; when a face was paired with a body of the opposite valence, what observers saw in the face shifted with the body (Aviezer et al., 2012, abstract read on Princeton University's research record). The abstract does not say who the observers were or how many; the study was a laboratory study. It shows that posture carries information that the face alone did not, at peak intensity. It does not show that a posture tells you which emotion an athlete is feeling.

How accurate coaches are. One study filmed 40 coach–athlete dyads in a training session; each person then watched the footage, reported what they had been thinking or feeling at chosen moments, and inferred what their partner had been thinking or feeling. Coaches in individual sports were more accurate than coaches in team sports, a difference accounted for by how much attention the two shared; coaches who ran longer sessions were more accurate (Lorimer & Jowett, 2009, abstract read on Abertay University's research record). The abstract gives no accuracy figures, ages or sports, so how often coaches were right cannot be stated here. In a study of one karate coach and three athletes aged 18 to 20 at national or international level, coach and athletes each rated, from video of training and of tournament fights, the emotions the athlete wanted to feel and the emotions the athlete felt. Both linked a smaller gap between wanted and actual emotion to better rated performance; but the athletes put anger and enthusiasm at the centre of what they wanted in competition, and the coach put anxiety and calmness (Friesen et al., 2018, full text read on the University of Jyväskylä's repository). Three athletes and one coach: the authors call it a case study and give the small sample as a limitation. It is the only study opened in which coach and athlete answered the same question about the athlete's emotion, and they did not answer it the same way.

What follows for you.

  • Describe what you see, in behaviour words: "sitting apart, head down, has not picked up her racket", not "she's lost her nerve" [weak, against reading emotion from the face; a review outside sport, rule 5; for athletes under 18, inference].
  • Treat what the face and the body show as a prompt to ask the athlete [practice, for the step from the evidence to the instruction; no study opened tests it].
  • Ask the athlete what state helps them perform; do not assume it is the one that would help you [weak; one case study of three adult karate athletes, rule 5; for athletes under 18, inference].
  • Coaches in individual sports, who shared more of their athlete's focus, read the athlete more accurately than coaches in team sports; in a team sport, expect to misread more often [weak, for the association; one study in coach–athlete dyads, abstract read, rule 5; ages not given, so for under-18s, inference].

022. Observe, compare, ask: the athlete's baseline

A baseline is what ordinary looks like for one athlete: in training, before competing and after it. The same behaviour means different things in two athletes. A volleyball setter who is always quiet before a match is at baseline when quiet; a talkative one who goes silent has changed. The baseline is the comparison that lets you see a change without guessing at what is inside.

What the sources support. The IOC's recognition tool, SMHRT-1, was developed for athletes and their entourage, coaches among them, as one of two tools built from reviews, a survey of 360 people at two athletes' forums (athletes, coaches in elite sport and representatives of governing bodies, by the methods; the abstract names only athletes) and a modified Delphi consensus process (Gouttebarge et al., 2021, consensus, abstract read, methods read on PubMed Central). The International Shooting Sport Federation's page for the tool (read 18 Nov 2026) says it is for recognising mental health problems, not diagnosing them, and among the red flags that call for immediate help it lists "Other highly uncharacteristic behaviours, emotions and appearances" (ISSF, The IOC Sport Mental Health Recognition Tool, a federation's reproduction of the IOC tool, undated (footer © 2025), read 18 Nov 2026; the IOC's own copy not opened). "Uncharacteristic" means against the person's own baseline. No study opened tests whether coaches who keep a written baseline notice change sooner or refer better [practice; IOC consensus; the studies behind it were not opened].

What a baseline records. Behaviour you can see and the athlete's own words, at fixed points, kept for weeks before you lean on it:

  • In training: how they arrive, who they talk to, how they take correction, what they do after a mistake, whether they stay after.
  • Before competing: what they do in the last hour (headphones, talking, routine), what they ask you, and SP4's check-in: "From 0 to 10, how nervous are you?" and "What's on your mind about today?".
  • After competing: how they are after a win and after a loss, how long it lasts, what they want from you.
  • Pressure sessions: SP6's debrief question "From 0 to 10, how much pressure did that feel like?" goes in its own line. SP6 says it measures how much pressure a drill created and is not SP4's nervousness rating; the baseline sheet keeps them in separate rows, never averaged or compared one with the other [practice; SP4, SP6].

A deviation, and asking. When what you see differs from the athlete's baseline, the next step is a question, in private, that names what you saw and asks what is going on, without supplying the answer: "I've noticed you've left straight after the last few sessions. How are things?" Then listen and write down their words. What you do next depends on what they say and on SP4's normal-or-refer table; a red flag goes the same day by the urgent route on your SP1 scope card [practice; the course's scope line; the IOC red flags as above].

Three things a baseline is not. It is not a test: a coach does not score an athlete's mood, run a questionnaire or name a condition (SP1). It is not surveillance: it records what you see in your sessions and what the athlete chooses to tell you. And it is not permanent: growth, injury, a move or a new school change what ordinary looks like (SP2), so you rewrite it.

033. How athletes regulate their own emotions, across the four bands

The studies opened measure two strategies, and the unit uses them in the studies' sense: cognitive reappraisal, changing how one thinks about a situation so that it changes how one feels, and expressive suppression, holding back the outward sign of a feeling. Both are measured by questionnaire.

What goes with what, in athletes. In 93 athletes aged 15 to 17 at one Swedish sports upper secondary school, followed over about 12 months in three waves, reappraisal went with better general mental health on a 12-item health questionnaire (β = −0.33, where higher scores mean worse health) and suppression with worse (β = 0.34); both strategies rose over the year (Fröjdö Regborn et al., 2025, full text read). The authors note that the suppression scale was unreliable at the first wave, so its rise needs replication, and that "causality cannot be inferred". In 424 competitive athletes aged 16 to 36 (mean 23.1), individual and team sports, mostly at regional level, measured once, satisfaction of the needs for autonomy and relatedness went with more pleasant emotion partly through more reappraisal, and competence and relatedness satisfaction with less suppression (Robazza et al., 2023, full text read). These are correlations in two samples [weak, for the association, rule 5].

How regulation develops. The studies opened are outside sport, and they do not agree on a simple line:

  • About 10 to 12, and 13 to 15. In 254 school pupils aged 9 to 16 in eight schools in the Valencian Community, Spain, measured once by self-report, age had a larger effect than gender on an overall emotion-regulation score; girls scored higher than boys at 9 to 12 and lower at 13 to 16 (Sanchis-Sanchis et al., 2020, full text read). SP2 read the same paper and reported that its abstract and its table point in different directions for age; this unit does not settle that.
  • 10 to 22, in the laboratory. An imaging study compared older children (10 to 13), adolescents (14 to 17) and young adults (18 to 22) on a task that separates how strongly people react to negative images from how well they can reappraise them. It found no age differences in reported reactivity, and "linear and quadratic relationships between reappraisal ability and age" (McRae et al., 2012, abstract read on MIT's repository). The full text (PubMed Central, read 18 Nov 2026) gives 38 participants: 12 older children, 10 adolescents and 16 young adults, the last aged 18 to 23 there. It does not say in words which way the curve bends, so this unit states only that reappraisal ability changed with age across 10 to 22 and not in a straight line.
  • 15 to 17, in athletes. Both reappraisal and suppression rose across a year at the Swedish sports school above; one year in one school cannot separate age from the change of school.
  • 19 to 25. No study opened compares this band with younger ones in athletes. The sample of 424 competitive athletes aged 16 to 36 (section 3) covers it without analysing age.

So, by band [weak, for the description; studies outside sport and two correlational studies in athletes, rule 5; for athletes under 18, inference where the sample was adult or non-athlete]: at 10 to 12 and 13 to 15, expect regulation still to be changing and to differ between children of the same age; at 16 to 18, reappraisal and suppression were both in use and rising in the one athlete sample; at 19 to 25, nothing in the studies opened says the capacity is still developing. None of this tells you that a particular athlete cannot regulate; it tells you not to assume they can do it alone because of their age. Teaching reappraisal and the other skills is SP5's.

044. When one person regulates another's emotion, and why it matters more with younger athletes

Regulating someone else's emotion is called interpersonal emotion regulation. A teammate who jokes with a nervous gymnast before her beam routine, a captain who calms a rower after a crab, a coach who says "breathe, then the next point" to a tennis junior: each is trying to change another person's feeling.

In teams, in athletes. In 451 athletes from 38 teams in a study of adolescent athletes, efforts to improve a teammate's emotional state went with the athlete's own greater enjoyment and commitment, and strategies that worsened the athlete's own emotional state went with less enjoyment; the team's climate of such regulation mattered too (Tamminen et al., 2016, abstract read on the University of Toronto's repository). The abstract does not give ages, sports or design beyond a multilevel model, so cause cannot be read from it. In 509 athletes from 50 interdependent sport teams in Canada and the UK (mean age 19.0), surveyed after competitions about the competition just played, the regulation athletes received from teammates was not linked to their anxiety in competition or their goal achievement once their anxiety before competing was taken into account; their own regulation was. With the athletes' own regulation set aside in the model, the regulation received was associated with both (Tamminen et al., 2021, abstract read on the University of Portsmouth's research portal). A cross-sectional, retrospective survey. Read together: what teammates do goes with how athletes feel, and athletes who regulate themselves depend on it less [weak, for the associations; two survey studies in athletes, rule 5].

Neither study measured coaches. No study opened measures what a coach's regulation of an athlete's emotion does to the athlete; section 5 covers the nearest thing, what a coach's own displayed emotion goes with.

Younger athletes. A review of the family's part in children's and adolescents' emotion regulation proposes three routes: children learn by watching others; parents' practices around emotion shape it directly; and the family's emotional climate shapes it through parenting style, attachment, how much emotion the family expresses, and the parents' relationship; it also discusses how these change as children become adolescents (Morris et al., 2007, abstract read on Oklahoma State University's research record). It is a narrative review of family research outside sport; no grade rests on it. Applied to a coach, it is this unit's inference: a 10- to 15-year-old in your squad watches how you handle your own emotion, and with the youngest you will often be the regulator for a while, before handing the job back.

What follows [practice; the step from a family review and two team surveys to the coach's behaviour is the unit's; no study opened tests it]:

  • About 10 to 12. Do more of the regulating, briefly and visibly: name what you see in plain words, give one thing to do, stay nearby. Model the routine you want them to learn.
  • About 13 to 15. Offer before you do: "Do you want a minute, or do you want me here?" Hand over one step at a time.
  • About 16 to 18 and 19 to 25. Ask what they want from you, and do that unless it feeds the anxiety (section 6). Your job is to protect the conditions in which they regulate themselves.

055. Your own expressed emotion, and what it has been found to do

What the studies show. Two field studies in competitive team sports, reported together, asked coaches how much happiness and anger they had expressed, asked players about their own emotions and their inferences about how well they were playing, and recorded team performance; the first was cross-sectional, in baseball and softball, the second in football with measures before, during and after a game. Coaches' expressions of happiness and anger predicted players' happiness and anger, players' inferences about their performance, and team outcomes; the authors report that "coaches' expressions of happiness were conducive to team performance", and anger was not (van Kleef et al., 2019, abstract read on the University of Amsterdam's repository and the University of Haifa's research record). The abstract gives no sample sizes or ages. These are associations, one of them measured over a game; they cannot show that a coach's anger lowered performance.

In the Swedish sports school study in section 3, athletes aged 15 to 17 who reported a more controlling coaching style reported more suppression (β = 0.20) and, which the authors call paradoxical, more reappraisal (β = 0.20); a need-supportive style went with more reappraisal (β = 0.19) (Fröjdö Regborn et al., 2025). Suppression went with worse general mental health. Observational, self-report.

The claim "Harsh coaching builds toughness." SP1 graded it [weak, against the claim] from three surveys, and SP3 regraded it. Nothing opened for this unit changes that grade.

What follows for you.

  • Your displayed emotion is information to your athletes about how they are doing. Expect them to read your face and voice the way you read theirs: as signs they will interpret [weak, positive, for the association between a coach's displayed emotion and players' emotions and inferences; two field studies, abstract read, rule 5; ages not given, so for under-18s, inference].
  • On the bench or the touchline, keep anger out of what you show during competition; show what you want them to feel and do [weak; same source; the step to your behaviour is the unit's].
  • Do not fake happiness you do not feel. No study opened tests faked against genuine displays by coaches, so this is [practice]: say what you can honestly say ("good press, keep it") rather than performing a mood.
  • Notice your own state before a competition as you would an athlete's. Milan's coach (fictional) is autonomy-minded in training and anxious and loud on the touchline: that gap is what SP3's self-audit observes, and SP8's protocol returns to it.

066. What an athlete asks for, and what serves them

Athletes in a hard moment ask for something, in words or not. Four requests come up again and again: reassurance, to be left alone, to be told what to do, and to be let off. Each can serve the athlete or keep their difficulty going. The decision is made each time, by three questions drawn from SP3 and SP4:

  1. Autonomy. Does my answer leave the athlete choosing, or does it choose for them?
  2. Competence and relatedness. Does it leave them more able to deal with this, and still connected to me and the squad?
  3. Anxiety. Does it help them face the situation, or help them avoid it, so the fear is never tested (SP4's avoidance cycle)?

None of the four answers below has been tested as a coach's response. SP3 found no study testing any single autonomy-supportive or controlling behaviour on its own, only styles and climates as bundles, so this unit does not grade single behaviours from SP3's list as tested. The grades are [practice] unless a source is named.

"Will it be OK? Do you think I'll win?" (reassurance). In a study of 100 children aged 5 to 10 during a blood draw, children rated their parents as more fearful when the parents reassured than when they distracted, both when watching clips of their own parents and in staged video vignettes (McMurtry et al., 2010, abstract read on Dalhousie University's repository). Outside sport, in young children: for athletes, an inference. SP4 found no study testing whether a coach's reassurance keeps an athlete's anxiety going, and none was found here. The reasoning: you cannot know the result, so a promise is a prediction you cannot make, and repeated promises may become something the athlete needs before every event. Answer what you know (whether they are starting, what you want them to try, that you will be there afterwards) and ask what is on their mind [practice; weak, for the narrower finding that reassurance can signal the adult's fear, outside sport, inference].

"Leave me alone." Respect it when it is a request for time and the athlete has a plan for that time; say when you will come back. Stay closer when the athlete is young, distressed beyond their baseline, or alone in a way that is new for them; a request to be left alone, from an athlete who has been withdrawing for weeks, is a sign for SP4's table and your scope card, not a request to grant [practice].

"Just tell me what to do." Before competing, one clear instruction is often what a frozen or over-aroused athlete can use; give one, from what they have already practised (SP5's cue, SP6's routine), and afterwards ask them what they would choose next time. In training, turn the request back into a choice between two options [practice; the autonomy-support evidence behind the direction is SP3's, graded there as a bundle].

"Can I sit this one out?" (to be let off). Ask why first. A sore hamstring goes to the medical plan; illness, exhaustion or a red flag goes to your scope card. When the reason is fear of the event, letting off removes the test and the relief rewards the avoidance; SP4 described family accommodation going with more severe anxiety in children outside sport. Offer a smaller step instead (a lower level of SP6's ladder, one round, the warm-up and then a decision), agreed with the athlete [practice; the link from SP4's evidence to a coach's answer is the unit's].

077. The common states as a coach sees them

The seven states below are named as a coach sees them from outside: frozen, tearful, angry, flat, over-aroused, overconfident, withdrawn. They are descriptions of behaviour, not diagnoses, and the same behaviour in two athletes may come from different places. The deliverable's state-response table gives each one: what you see, what to ask, what to do, what not to do, the grade of that advice, and when it leaves your part.

What the table rests on:

  • Ask before you act, and compare with the baseline [practice; section 2].
  • What to do: a practised step, never a new one. A what-to-do cue from SP5 [practice, as SP1 grades this step]; slow breathing to settle the body between efforts, which changes heart rhythm in the studies and has not been shown to improve competition performance [weak, positive, for heart-rate variability; weak, no benefit shown in competition; SP5's grades]; SP6's step-down rule for pressure sessions [practice].
  • What not to do. "Relax", "calm down", "don't freeze": instructions about what to avoid, set by SP1 against the ironic-effects review [weak, positive, for the ironic effect; samples mostly adults, for under-18s inference; SP1's grade]. Anger in return (section 5) [weak]. Public comment on a state in front of the squad [practice].
  • When it leaves your part. SP4's normal-or-refer table, SP1's scope card, and SP9's red-flag table when it is filed: a state that lasts, spreads beyond sport or costs the athlete what matters to them goes to referral [practice; the IOC criteria behind SP4's table; the studies behind it were not opened].

Two states need a word more. Flat and withdrawn are the two that a coach is most likely to miss, because neither interrupts a session. A flat athlete still trains; a withdrawn athlete still turns up. Sara (fictional) has been flat for six weeks, has stopped staying to talk after training, and has slept about five hours a night: her development note (SP2) and SP4's table already call for referral. Overconfident is the state a coach is most tempted to puncture; no study opened tests what a coach should do with it, and every row of the table on it is [practice].

088. Feedback after an error

What has been tested, as a package. The coach programme in SP3, the Mastery Approach to Coaching, tells coaches to "Give encouragement immediately after mistakes", to give corrective instruction in an encouraging way, focusing on what following it will gain the athlete, and to give encouragement alone when the athlete knows how to fix the mistake; it lists punishing mistakes, including by tone of voice or other signs of disapproval, among the things to avoid (Smoll et al., 2007, full text read). In 151 basketball players aged 9 to 15, a non-randomised comparison of two matched community leagues, anxiety fell over a season in the league whose coaches and parents took the workshops and rose in the other. The guidelines on mistakes were part of a package with a parent workshop; their own effect cannot be separated, and what coaches actually did was not recorded [weak, positive, for the package; one quasi-experimental study, rule 5; for 16 to 25, outside the sampled ages].

Feedback after good trials or after poor ones. In 28 schoolchildren (mean age 10.6) throwing beanbags at a target, those told their results after their three most accurate throws in each block of six learned more, on a test a day later, than those told after their three least accurate, and reported more intrinsic motivation (Saemi et al., 2011, abstract read). In 40 children (ages not given on the page read) on the same kind of task, the two groups did not differ at retention (Chiviacowsky et al., 2007, abstract read). The adult study both followed found better learning with feedback after good trials (Chiviacowsky & Wulf, 2007, abstract read on the BISp's sport literature database). Three laboratory studies of a throwing task, outside sport, with mixed results in children [contested; one failed replication in children (Chiviacowsky et al., 2007) against one positive study in children and one in adults; rule 4; for athletes, inference].

What follows [practice, for the order and wording; the package above for its direction]:

  1. The athlete talks first. "What did you see there?" A judoka thrown in the last ten seconds, a hockey goalkeeper beaten at the near post, a gymnast who fell from the beam: each usually knows more about what happened than you saw. If they know the fix, encouragement alone is enough.
  2. One correction, framed as what to do next, and what it will give them.
  3. Mix in what went well. Feedback on good attempts is at least as useful to learning in the studies above, and in one of them went with more motivation.
  4. Never punish the mistake, in words, tone, extra running or selection [weak, positive, for avoiding punishment as part of the package above].
  5. In private when the error was public. No study opened tests this; it follows from section 5 and SP3's ego-involving climate [practice].
  6. Not straight after a loss, unless they ask. After a defeat, the first job is the athlete's state (the state-response table); technique can wait until the debrief SP6 describes [practice].

099. Differences between athletes, between sexes and between cultures

Between athletes. Everything in this unit points the same way: the same behaviour means different things in different athletes, which is why the unit builds the response on a baseline and on the athlete's words, never on a type. Some athletes want to be fired up before competing, some want calm (Friesen et al., section 1); some are helped by teammates' regulation, some regulate alone (Tamminen et al., 2021, section 4).

Between sexes. Two studies opened report differences, and both are small and conditional. In the Spanish pupils aged 9 to 16, girls scored higher than boys on emotion regulation at 9 to 12 and lower at 13 to 16, and the authors report that gender had no major effect on the overall score (Sanchis-Sanchis et al., 2020). In 698 adolescent athletes aged 12 to 18 in three secondary schools in Shandong, China, in basketball, football, swimming and badminton (the abstract calls the data multi-wave, the limitations call the design cross-sectional), the link between emotion regulation and resilience was slightly stronger in girls than in boys in team sports (interaction β = 0.057, ΔR² = 0.009) and did not differ by gender in individual sports (Wei et al., 2025, full text read). The authors call the effects modest and the design cross-sectional. Neither study shows that boys and girls need different responses from a coach, and the deliverable makes no distinction by sex [weak, for the description; rule 5].

Between cultures. The review in section 1 found that how people express emotion varies substantially across cultures and situations, and that the evidence on how people in small, remote cultures move their faces during emotion was, in its authors' judgement, inconclusive (Barrett et al., 2019). That is a further reason to ask rather than read. No study opened compares how athletes from different cultures want a coach to respond, so the unit says nothing more, and the deliverable makes no distinction by culture.

1010. Athletes with a diagnosis that affects attention or emotion

Some of your athletes will have a diagnosis, such as attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD), autism or an anxiety disorder, that affects attention or emotion. You do not assess, diagnose or treat any of them (SP1). What you need is what the athlete and their family choose to tell you, and what helps in your sessions.

What the consensus says, for ADHD. The IOC's consensus statement on mental health in elite athletes says: "There are scant data regarding the prevalence of ADHD in elite athletes." It describes poor focus, oppositional behaviour, frustration, low self-esteem and labile mood as features that may affect performance, recommends psychosocial interventions, including education for athletes, families and coaches, and notes that stimulant medication is prohibited in competition by the World Anti-Doping Agency, so an athlete who needs it for medical reasons needs a therapeutic use exemption (Reardon et al., 2019, consensus, full text read on a copy hosted by the University of Wisconsin's Department of Psychiatry). The statement concerns elite athletes, mostly adults; it was written in 2019, and anti-doping rules change. In the part read, it says nothing about autism; no source on autism in sport was opened for this unit, so nothing is said about it here.

Questions for the athlete and the family, not assertions [practice; IOC consensus for the coach's part as education; the studies behind it were not opened]:

  • What would you like me to know about how this affects you in training and competition, if anything?
  • When you are overwhelmed, frustrated or cannot focus, what does it look like from outside, and what helps? What makes it worse?
  • How do you prefer instructions: one at a time, written, shown, said?
  • Is there anything about competition days (noise, waiting, changes of plan) that I should plan for?
  • Who else should I talk to, with your agreement: a parent, a doctor, a teacher? What may I share with the squad, if anything?
  • If you take medication, is there anything about timing, training or competition rules I need to know? (If a therapeutic use exemption might be needed, ask your national anti-doping organisation; this unit states no rule as yours.)

Write the answers on the athlete's baseline sheet in their words. They change what ordinary looks like for that athlete; they do not change the scope line.

1111. Getting it wrong, and repair

You will misread athletes: take tears for weakness, anger for disrespect, flatness for laziness, or answer a request in a way that made things worse. What the research says about repair between coach and athlete is thin.

What the studies show. A review of conflict among athletes and coaches found 80 papers, and "only a small number (6) studied interpersonal conflict directly" (Wachsmuth et al., 2017, abstract read on Loughborough University's repository). An interview study of 22 high-performance coaches and athletes by the same authors describes conflict prevented by good relationships and by explicit strategies, and managed, once it arises, by personal and interpersonal strategies and by seeking outside help, with the coach's role central (Wachsmuth et al., 2018, abstract read on Loughborough University's repository). In 18 community sport coaches interviewed about conflicts they had caused with people they worked with, repair took the form of accounts, apologies and demonstrations of concern, and whether it worked depended on whether the other person accepted it (Gale et al., 2023, abstract read on Northumbria University's research portal). The abstract describes the other parties only as significant working others and does not say whether athletes were among them. None of the three tests a way of repairing.

What follows [practice; no study opened tests repair with athletes]:

  1. Name what you did, in behaviour words: "I shouted at you after the third round in front of everyone."
  2. Say it was wrong, without an explanation that excuses it. An explanation can come later if they ask.
  3. Say what you will do differently, and do it; the change is what the athlete will judge.
  4. Ask how it was for them, and listen. They may not accept the repair at once; the community-coach study found that acceptance decided whether repair worked.
  5. Do it soon, in private, and once. With a minor, ask the club's welfare or safeguarding officer whether the parents should be told what happened; for a concern about how an adult has treated a minor, ask that officer, or your national sport body, what the reporting route is (SP1, DD-SP1).

A coach who repairs a misreading also models what section 4 asked of the youngest athletes: watching an adult handle their own mistake.

C. Five asking scripts

Each script is said in private unless it says otherwise, and the athlete's answer goes on the baseline sheet in their words. The scripts are this unit's own wording [practice; no study opened tests any of them].

  1. What helps you. Asked in an ordinary week, before you need it.
  2. I've noticed. Asked when something differs from the baseline.
  3. After an error or a loss. The athlete talks first (section 8).
  4. When they ask for something. Reassurance, to be left alone, to be told what to do, to be let off (section 6).
  5. Repair. After you have got it wrong (section 11).

Dani (fictional), 14, judo

  1. "When you've lost a contest, what helps most: me nearby, a few minutes on your own, or something to do next? And before a contest, is there anything you'd like me to say or not say?"
  2. "I've noticed you've been sitting on your own in the warm-up area. That's not like you. How are things?" (Anything she says about eating, weight or body: "Thank you for telling me. I'd like to make sure you get the right help with that," and it goes by her scope card, with no comment from her coach on food or body.)
  3. "What did you feel in the grip fight there?" Then one thing: "Next time, first grip before she moves." Then: "Your pull on the left side was much better today."
  4. Reassurance ("Will I win?"): "I don't know; nobody does. I know you've practised the grip fight, and I'll be at the mat edge." To be let off: "Tell me why first." If it is fear: "Let's agree a smaller step: the warm-up, then one contest, then we talk."
  5. "I raised my voice at you after the second round, in front of the squad. That was wrong. Next time I'll wait and talk to you on your own. How was that for you?" (Her coach tells her parents what happened if the club's welfare or safeguarding officer advises it.)

This unit is information, not legal, medical or psychological advice. Every technique and recommendation carries a grade in square brackets from SP1's grade key (section 9 of SP1: five rules applied in order; "several" means three or more; the labels direction and inference). Inference marks evidence from adults, students or non-athletes applied to an athlete under 18, as SP1 words it; where a study sampled younger people than the band it is applied to, the unit says "outside the sampled ages", as SP5 does. Where the only source opened is a consensus statement whose studies were not opened, the grade is [practice] with the note "the studies behind it were not opened", as in SP2 to SP6. Age bands are SP2's: about 10 to 12, 13 to 15, 16 to 18 and 19 to 25. Where a claim rests on an abstract alone, the citation says "abstract read".

The three case athletes return: Dani (fictional), 14, judo; Milan (fictional), 17, football; Sara (fictional), 20, 400 metres. They illustrate; they are never evidence.

One warning holds for the whole unit. Most of what follows about responding to a particular state, in a particular athlete, at a particular moment, has never been tested. The research opened tests narrower things: whether faces carry emotion reliably, how regulation strategies go with outcomes in surveys, what a coach's displayed emotion goes with in two field studies. The unit says, at each step, which parts of its advice rest on a study and which on practice.

Unit SP7, Reading and responding to an athlete. Cover: what the science of emotion supports about reading a feeling from a face or a posture, and what follows for a coach; observing behaviour instead of inferring feeling: an athlete's baseline, a deviation from it, and asking; emotion regulation strategies and how they develop across the four age bands; how one person regulates another's emotion, and what that means with younger athletes; the coach's own expressed emotion and what it has been found to do to athletes; what an athlete asks for (reassurance, to be left alone, to be told what to do, to be let off) against what serves their autonomy, competence and relatedness (SP3) and does not feed their anxiety (SP4), as a decision made each time, with the reasoning shown; the common states as a coach sees them (frozen, tearful, angry, flat, over-aroused, overconfident, withdrawn), each with what to ask, what to do, what not to do and the grade of that advice; feedback after an error; differences between athletes, between sexes and between cultures, only as far as a source supports them; athletes with a diagnosis that affects attention or emotion, put as questions for the athlete and the family, with nothing asserted that is not sourced; getting it wrong, and repair. Deliverable: a baseline sheet per athlete (what ordinary looks like in training, before and after competing); a state-response table of the seven states; and five asking scripts; built in full for Dani, Milan and Sara, then as templates.
