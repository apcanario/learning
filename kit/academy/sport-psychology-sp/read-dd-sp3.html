011. What the noise is

In this unit noise is anything that pulls attention away from the next action the athlete has to take. It comes from two places.

Inside. Worry (what if I miss, what will they think); watching oneself (attending to how a movement feels while doing it); thoughts about the result (the score, the ranking, selection). SP4 set out the two accounts of choking these map onto: worry taking up attention, and attention turned onto a movement that normally runs on its own (SP4, section 5) [weak, for the accounts as explanations; SP4's grade]. Milan's (fictional) words at a check-in, "If I give the ball away early, they'll decide I'm not good enough" (SP4), are a thought about the result in his own words.

Outside. The crowd; the opponent (what they say, how they look at you); officials (a call against you, a long wait for a video review); parents (a voice from the stand, a father at the edge of the mat, as with Dani (fictional)); and the coach. SP8, section 11 named the coach's own part of this noise on the day: a raised voice, a change of plan, a long talk in the last minute. This unit does not repeat it; it points back to SP8, section 11, and to SP8, section 2's one-cue rule [practice; SP8].

Outside noise works through inside noise. In one experiment, insults from a confederate opponent raised anger and reported distraction without directly changing free-throw scores (section 13). The techniques below act on what the athlete does with the noise: none of them removes the crowd, the opponent or the referee.

Examples in this unit are drawn from many sports: a diver on the board, a tennis player at the change of ends, a swimmer in the call room, a gymnast waiting for a score, a fencer after a touch against her, a handball goalkeeper facing a penalty, an archer between ends, a cyclist on a climb, a volleyball server, a judoka between contests, a high jumper between attempts, a basketball free-throw shooter, and the case's footballer and 400 m runner.

022. Why "don't think of it" brings it back, and the rule that follows

The popular claim: "Telling yourself not to think about it works." The idea that suppressing a thought or a movement keeps it away is the claim; the evidence below points the other way for movements under pressure.

Outside sport and in novices. Two experiments on golf putting and a hand-held pendulum found errors that were the ironic opposite of the intention, especially when people told to avoid an error tried to do so under mental or physical load, and more so when they could watch their movement (Wegner et al., 1998, abstract read). The abstract describes no sample. Wegner's 1994 paper setting out the theory could not be opened.

In athletes. In 40 male university hockey players (mean age 20.25), each shooting 30 penalties in a low-pressure and a high-pressure condition (a £100 prize; order counterbalanced), all told to be careful not to hit the ball to the right of the right-hand post, target hits fell from 11.70 to 9.85 under pressure and hits in the to-be-avoided zone rose from 6.95 to 8.77, while misses elsewhere did not change. A second study, in 73 adults who had rarely or never thrown darts, found the same pattern (Woodman et al., 2015, accepted manuscript read in full on Bangor University's repository). Both are within-subject comparisons of the same people under low and high pressure; neither compares an avoid-instruction with a what-to-do instruction.

The review. A systematic review found 24 studies in 17 articles (5 experimental, 19 quasi-experimental; 1,152 participants; mean age 21.78). By skill level: 12 studies in novices, 3 in trained, 8 in highly trained and 1 in elite athletes. Twenty-two of the 24 were consistent with Wegner's theory; two were inconclusive. Every study used avoidance instructions as part of the pressure; the review notes that ironic errors can occur even with positive instructions that contain a word for the forbidden target, and recommends simple process goals (Bartura et al., 2024, full text read at the publisher's page, without its per-study table). Only one study included under-18s: in 43 female volleyball players aged 13 to 16 (mean 14.51), serving under low and then high pressure in a fixed order with no control group, overall performance did not change across conditions and the authors report that the results did not support Wegner's prediction (Gorgulu & Gokcek, 2021, full text read).

Regrading SP1's entry (flag F6). SP1 graded "phrase instructions as what to do" [weak, positive] by rule 5 and asked this unit to regrade it from the primary studies. Rule 1 does not apply: no review opened disagrees on whether the effect exists. Rule 4 needs three or more controlled studies in athletes that agree. The review lists 12 studies with trained, highly trained or elite participants, but its per-study table did not come through, and its quasi-experimental studies (79% of the 24) compare the same people under two pressure levels without a separate control group. Of the two athlete studies opened here, one supports the effect (adult hockey players) and one does not (13- to 16-year-old volleyball players). Three agreeing controlled athlete studies are not shown, so rule 5 holds. The grades:

  • Under pressure, an instruction about what to avoid makes that error more likely: [weak, positive, in adults (rule 5); for 13 to 16, weak, no effect shown in the one study opened; for 10 to 12, outside the sampled ages].
  • Every cue says what to do: [weak, positive; the opened studies show the cost of avoid-instructions under pressure, and none opened compares the two kinds of instruction directly; samples mostly adults, so for athletes under 18 an inference]. The grade is the same as SP1's; what has changed is that it now rests on primary studies opened, and that the one youth study found nothing.

The rule, worded for practice. A cue names the action and the place it goes, and leaves out the thing to avoid: "Through the far corner", not "Don't hit the net"; "Tall off the board", not "Don't pike early". Because the review found ironic errors even after positive instructions that mention the forbidden target, a cue also leaves out the word for that target ("Away from the keeper" still names the keeper) [weak, positive; the review's summary of two studies not opened here; inference under 18]. This applies to your words as much as the athlete's: "Don't freeze" and "Don't go out too fast" are avoid-instructions (SP8, section 2).

033. Cue words in competition: written by the athlete or assigned by the coach

What self-talk does in sport studies. A meta-analysis of 32 studies (62 effect sizes) found a moderate positive effect of self-talk interventions on sport-task performance (ES = .48), larger for fine than for gross tasks and for novel than for well-learned tasks, with instructional self-talk more effective than motivational for fine tasks, and interventions that trained the self-talk more effective than those that did not (Hatzigeorgiadis et al., 2011, abstract read). The abstract does not describe the participants. SP5 graded a planned self-talk phrase, practised for weeks [weak, mixed], from two randomised studies in athletes; the positive one, in 117 athletes aged 13 to 23, had athletes develop their own phrases over eight weeks (SP5, section 3.2). The self-talk systematic review (Tod et al., 2011) could not be opened; nothing here rests on it.

Written or assigned (flag F68). One study opened compared the two. In 81 collegiate cross-country runners, given instructional, motivational or combined self-talk that was either assigned or freely chosen, a one-mile time trial a week later showed no significant differences between groups; performance improved overall, and within groups the combined self-set, motivational self-set and instructional assigned groups improved significantly (International Journal of Sport and Exercise Psychology, DOI registered 2012, abstract read; the page showed no authors or title). Outside self-talk, a meta-analysis of pre-task music found larger benefits on two outcomes for music participants chose themselves than for music chosen for them (section 12). Nothing opened compares chosen and assigned cues in athletes under 18.

So: the athlete writes the cue, and you check it against section 4's tests [weak, no difference shown between assigned and freely chosen self-talk in one study of collegiate runners, abstract read, rule 5; inference for under-18s]. The reasons for preferring the athlete's own words are the course's own reading: the positive eight-week trial used athletes' own phrases (SP5), and choosing fits the autonomy support SP3 graded [moderate, for the association, as SP3 grades it]. Your part is to offer two or three examples, labelled as examples, and to send back any draft that names what to avoid.

055. Talking to yourself as "you" or by name

In sport. In 16 physically active men (mean age 21.99), playing rugby, boxing, soccer, Gaelic football or rock climbing at university or club level, each rode two 10 km cycling time trials in random order, using motivational statements they had written themselves, once in the first person ("I can do this") and once in the second ("You can do this"). They were 2.2% faster with second-person statements (1045 s against 1068 s; d = .24); 13 of 16 were faster; perceived exertion did not differ. There was no condition without self-talk (Hardy et al., 2019, accepted manuscript read in full on Bangor University's repository).

Outside sport. In undergraduates and young adults, reflecting on negative images or memories using their own name in place of "I" lowered one brain measure of emotional reaction in 29 people and self-reported negative feeling in 50 (d = 1.01), without engaging measures of effortful control (Moser et al., 2017, full text read). No performance was measured. The larger series of studies on distanced self-talk (Kross et al., 2014) could not be opened.

Grade. Phrasing self-talk as "you" or with one's own name: [weak, positive; one randomised crossover study in 16 recreational male athletes on an endurance task, and laboratory studies outside sport on feelings, rule 5; for athletes under 18 an inference]. It is a change to the wording of a cue the athlete already uses, so it costs one session to try: "You: high toss, reach", or the athlete's first name followed by the cue.

066. Reading arousal as readiness

What it is. The athlete is told, and practises telling themselves, that a fast heart and quick breathing before a start are the body getting ready and can help. SP4 described challenge and threat states; this technique tries to move an athlete's reading of the same body signals towards challenge.

The evidence opened.

  • In 50 adults with no golf putting experience (mean age 20.24), randomly assigned to reappraisal instructions or a control task before one putt under pressure, the reappraisal group was more accurate (radial error 8.64 cm against 24.92 cm, d = 0.93) and rated their arousal as more helpful; the cardiovascular difference was not significant (Moore et al., 2015, post-print read in full on the University of Gloucestershire repository). One putt per person.
  • In 54 undergraduate novice darts players (mean 21.72), randomised, the same kind of instructions produced a more challenge-like cardiovascular response, higher ratings of resources and self-confidence, and no difference in the change in accuracy under pressure (group × time, p = .72); the authors write that the intervention did not improve performance beyond the control group (Sammy et al., 2017, post-print read in full on the University of Gloucestershire repository).
  • The review of choking interventions in sport classed reappraisal cues as showing a null effect on performance under pressure; the page read does not name the study or give its sample (Gröpel & Mesagno, 2019, abstract and parts of the text read at the publisher's page). SP6 recorded the same result and left its weight to this unit (flag F83).
  • The two reappraisal papers outside sport in the course list (Brooks, 2014; Jamieson et al., 2010) could not be opened.

Weighing it (flag F83). One randomised study in novices found better performance; one randomised study in novices found changes in appraisal and heart response without a performance gain; the review in athletes counts reappraisal cues null. Rule 1 needs two reviews that disagree or a failed preregistered replication; neither is present. SP6 graded reappraisal cues [weak, no benefit shown under pressure] from the review alone; with the two randomised studies opened here, the direction is mixed. Grade: reading arousal as readiness [weak, mixed; two randomised studies in novice adults, laboratory tasks, and a review in athletes reporting a null result, rule 5; for athletes under 18 an inference]. What the studies support more consistently is the change in how people rated their arousal and resources, which is not the same as playing better.

How to use it, and its limit. Teach it as a way to describe the body before a start ("Heart's up: ready") and never as a promise of a better result [practice]. It names sensations (heart, breath, hands) and nothing about weight, shape or food; DD-SP2's language card applies to everything said about the body (flag F214). Do not use it to talk an athlete out of a symptom that is already referred, such as Milan's nausea on match mornings or Sara's (fictional) short sleep: those stay with the people they were referred to (SP4, SP9) [practice; the course's scope line].

077. If-then plans for the moments that go wrong

What it is. An if-then plan (an implementation intention) links a cue to a response in advance: "If I lose the first point, then I bounce the ball twice and pick my target." The cue is a moment the athlete can recognise; the response is an action, worded as what to do.

The evidence opened. A meta-analysis of 94 independent tests, not limited to sport, found a medium-to-large effect of forming implementation intentions on goal attainment (d = .65), including for shielding goal pursuit from unwanted influences (Gollwitzer & Sheeran, 2006, abstract read on the University of Konstanz repository). The abstract does not describe the samples. In sport, 107 tennis players aged 14 to 68 (mean about 34) from league tournaments were randomised to no goal, a goal (to play each ball with full concentration and effort to win the match) or the goal plus four if-then plans, each pairing an inner state they chose from a list (such as feeling angry, feeling exhausted, not concentrating enough) with a response chosen from a list. After a critical match, combined self and trainer or teammate ratings, made without knowing the group, were higher for the if-then group than for either other group. The groups differed before the match (the if-then group reported more competition anxiety than the goal-only group), and the authors did not measure how the plans worked (Achtziger et al., 2008, Study 2, full text read on the University of Konstanz site).

Grade. If-then plans for the moments that go wrong in competition: [weak, positive; one randomised study in tennis players of 14 to 68 on rated performance, and a meta-analysis outside sport, rule 5; the athlete sample was mostly adult, so for 14 to 18 an inference, and below 14 outside the sampled ages].

Writing them. Three to five plans, each for a moment the athlete has met before: a lost point, a bad call, a long wait, the opponent's remark, a false start. The "then" part is one action, worded as what to do, and short enough to be a cue (section 4) [practice, for the number and the form; weak, positive, for the plans]. Examples, not tested wording: a fencer, "If a touch goes against me, then I walk back to the line and set my feet"; an archer, "If an arrow lands outside the gold, then I lower the bow, breathe out and start my routine from the top"; a gymnast, "If the score is late, then I sit, sip and run the first skill of my next piece in my head".

088. Pre-performance routines and short resets after an error

Routines. A meta-analysis of 33 articles (61 samples, 112 effect sizes, 15 sports, athletes aged 11 to 40) found pre-performance routines improved performance in experiments against controls under low pressure (g = 0.64, 95% CI 0.45 to 0.83) and under pressure (g = 0.70, 0.24 to 1.16, from only 19 effect sizes), and a small effect in pre-post designs (0.31, 0.18 to 0.44) where smaller studies reported larger effects; routine type, age, gender and skill level did not moderate the effect, and most samples had fewer than 20 participants (Rupprecht et al., 2024, abstract and parts of the text read at the publisher's page). SP6 graded it from the same paper, and this unit keeps the grade: a routine practised under pressure [moderate, positive; rule 4; for 10-year-olds outside the sampled ages]. SP6's routine builder is the place to build one.

Resets after an error. A reset is a routine cut down to seconds: what the athlete does between the error and the next action. No study opened tests a reset after an error as such; SP8 graded the reset between rounds [practice] (SP8, section 4). This unit gives the error reset the same three parts in miniature [practice; no study opened tests it]:

  1. Close it. One physical act that ends the last action: the tennis player turns to the back fence; the fencer walks back to the line; the handball goalkeeper touches both posts.
  2. One breath out. Not a breathing exercise; a marker (section 9 says what breathing has and has not been shown to do).
  3. Next cue. The athlete's cue for the next action, said once.

An official's decision against the athlete is handled with the same reset: it is something that has already happened and cannot be changed by the athlete in the next action [practice]. What the rules of the sport allow an athlete or a coach to say to an official is a question for the competition's rules: check it, and write down the date and where you read it, as SP8, section 5 does for coaching.

099. Breathing

SP5 graded slow breathing (about six breaths a minute, longer out-breath) [weak, positive] for heart-rate variability, and [weak, mixed in the laboratory, no competition outcome tested] for performance: the studies it opened measured heart rhythm and laboratory tasks, and none measured competition performance (SP5, section 3.4). No new study on breathing was opened for this unit. Following flag F73, this unit makes no performance claim for breathing. On the day, breathing goes early in a routine (the call room, the warm-up area, between contests), as a way to settle the body, and not in the last seconds before a reaction start [weak; one laboratory study in students, as SP5 grades it; inference]. The breath out in a reset (section 8) is a marker inside a routine, graded [practice], and is not the breathing technique.

Breathing touches the body. Say what it does (it changes heart rhythm), say nothing about weight, shape or food, and use DD-SP2's language card for anything else about the body (flag F214). An athlete who feels dizzy or short of breath stops and breathes normally (SP5).

1010. Attention placed outward

What it is. A cue that points attention at the effect of the movement on the world (the target, the ball's flight, the water) rather than at a body part. SP5 graded it [contested] under rule 1, from a meta-analysis in mostly novice adults and a bias re-analysis read in abstract, and left this unit to regrade it from the two larger reviews (flag F68).

The two meta-analyses. A 2021 set of meta-analyses found an external focus better than an internal one for performance (73 studies, 1,824 participants, g = 0.264, 95% CI 0.217 to 0.310), retention (g = 0.583, 0.425 to 0.741) and transfer (g = 0.584, 0.325 to 0.842), with no moderation by age group, health status or skill level, and a distal focus slightly better than a proximal one (g = 0.224) (Chua et al., 2021, abstract read on Penn State University's research record). A 2024 re-analysis of the data of the earlier meta-analyses with robust Bayesian methods found moderate to strong evidence of publication bias in every analysis; corrected for it, the effects were negligible (performance g = 0.01, retention 0.15, transfer 0.09, distance −0.01), Bayes factors favoured no effect, and the effects varied across studies for reasons not yet identified. Its authors conclude that focus effects are on average "small to nil" (McKay et al., 2024, abstract read on McMaster University's record and on the preprint page). The full texts of both could not be opened in this session, and the 2013 review by Wulf could not be reached; the abstracts do not say how many participants were athletes or children. SP5, section 3.5, reports the samples it opened: a network meta-analysis in 566 mostly novice healthy adults that found an external focus better at retention and transfer, and a pooled analysis of four crossover studies in 49 athletes (baseball, table tennis, golf; mean ages about 19) that found no significant difference between external and internal focus, which its authors say may reflect low power.

Grade. A cue that points attention outward: [contested; rule 1: two meta-analyses reach opposite conclusions on whether the effect exists, both read in abstract; samples not described in the abstracts, so for athletes under 18 an inference]. The regrade leaves SP5's grade unchanged and rests it on the two larger reviews. Neither abstract reports an outward cue doing worse on average than an inward one; the corrected estimates sit at about zero. The question flag F74 raised (whether rule 1 counts reviews read in abstract only) stands for the syllabus review.

What follows for practice. An outward cue is cheap to try and, on the corrected estimates, should not be expected to do much on its own. Use it as the wording of a cue that already passes section 4's tests ("Far corner", "Up to the roof", "Through the wall"), and judge it by whether the athlete can use it under pressure [practice].

1111. Accepting the noise without arguing with it

For whom. Some athletes have been trying to control nerves for years: told to relax, told not to think about it, told to calm down. Milan has heard "just relax" for five years. For an athlete whose attempts at control make things worse, section 2 is part of the reason: trying not to do something under pressure can make it more likely, in the adults studied.

What it is. Noticing the thought or feeling, naming it in a word or two, letting it be there, and returning attention to the next action: "There's the worry. Next ball." SP5 graded mindfulness and acceptance approaches [moderate, positive] from meta-analyses of programmes in athletes over 15 that ran for weeks or longer, with fair-to-low quality evidence, and offered the approach to athletes whose control strategies had not worked (SP5, sections 3.6 and 5). No new study was opened for this unit.

Grade. The programmes as tested: [moderate, positive; SP5's grade; for 10 to 15 an inference]. A single notice-name-return step used in competition, as this unit uses it: [practice; the step is a piece of the programmes, and no study opened tests it alone]. The approach is offered, not imposed, and it is not therapy: an athlete for whom noticing brings up distress they cannot set aside stops, and the first conversation and the scope card follow [practice; SP5].

When it touches the body ("there's my stomach"), the same rule as section 6 holds: sensations only, nothing on weight, shape or food (DD-SP2's language card; flag F214).

1212. Music, headphones, phones and social media on the day

The coach's part of the noise. SP8, section 11 covers it: your voice, your changes of plan, your last-minute talk (flag F119). This section covers what the athlete brings.

Music before the task. A meta-analysis of 30 controlled studies (474 healthy participants, mean ages 16.4 to 26.04, a mix of active and trained participants, some of them athletes) found that music before an exercise test went with small improvements in completion time (SMD −0.24, 95% CI −0.46 to −0.01), relative mean power (0.38) and relative peak power (0.53) and fewer fatigue symptoms, and no change in perceived exertion. Self-selected music gave larger benefits than music chosen by others on two outcomes. The tests were laboratory exercise tasks, not competition; heterogeneity was moderate to considerable and some outcomes showed possible publication bias; anxiety was measured in one study of nine people (Delleli et al., 2023, full text read at the publisher's page, first part). Grade: music the athlete chooses, before the warm-up or in waiting, [weak, positive, small; laboratory exercise tasks, rule 5; no competition outcome; for 10 to 15 outside the sampled ages]. The broader music meta-analysis in the course list (Terry et al.) was reached only at a record without its abstract.

Headphones. Sara keeps headphones on in the call room (SP7). Whether headphones or devices are allowed in a call room, warm-up area or field of play is a rule of the competition: ask the organiser or your federation, and write the date and the source (SP8, section 5) [practice].

Phones and social media. Two controlled studies from one research group, in athletes:

  • 24 national-level male volleyball players aged about 15.7, pair-matched and randomised, used social media on a phone for 30 minutes before each of 20 training sessions over four weeks, or watched Olympic documentaries; decision-making in attack and passing improved only in the documentary group, and perceived mental fatigue rose only in the social media group (Fortes et al., 2021a, accepted manuscript read in full on the University of Bologna repository).
  • 25 international-level swimmers (mean 20.4), in a randomised crossover, swam slower 100 m and 200 m freestyle (about 2%, in the second 50 m) after 30 minutes of social media apps than after 30 minutes of Olympic video; 50 m times did not differ (Fortes et al., 2021b, author's proof read in full on the University of Bologna repository).

Both papers report statistics that do not fit together in places (degrees of freedom; a jump result the Discussion contradicts). Grade: avoiding a long stretch of social media use in the half hour before performing [weak, positive; two controlled studies in athletes aged about 16 and about 20 from one group, rule 5; for 10 to 14 outside the sampled ages]. Nothing opened tests messages from parents, reading about oneself, or a phone used for music only. So, as practice: phone in the bag from arrival to the end of the athlete's last performance, except for music the athlete has chosen and for travel and safety contacts; no reading of results, comments or messages about the competition until after it [practice, built on the weak result above]. SP8's note to parents already asks that messages wait.

1313. The opponent's talk and an official's decision

The opponent. In 60 sport and exercise science undergraduates (mean 19.45, mostly basketball novices), randomly assigned to hear insults, distracting remarks or nothing from a confederate posing as an opponent in a free-throw competition, insults raised anger, both kinds of remark raised reported distraction, and baskets did not differ between groups; within the competition, anger was related to poorer shooting through distraction (a statistical path within the data, which does not show cause) (Ring et al., 2019, accepted manuscript read in full on Leeds Beckett University's repository). Grade, for the finding: [weak; one experiment in students, rule 5; inference under 18]. No study opened tests a technique for responding to an opponent's talk.

So, as practice: a planned non-reply. An if-then plan for the remark ("If he talks at the set piece, then I find my marker and say my cue"), with no answer to the opponent, and a reset if anger rises [practice, for the non-reply; weak, positive, for if-then plans, section 7]. Abuse, threats or anything discriminatory from an opponent, spectator or official is a matter for the officials and for your club's or federation's route, which DD-SP1 sets out; it is never something an athlete is asked to tune out [practice; DD-SP1].

The official. No study opened tests what an athlete does after a decision against them. The reset in section 8 applies [practice]. Your own reaction to a call is part of the noise on the athlete's side of the line (SP8, section 11).

1414. Waiting

Competition days are mostly waiting: the swimmer in the call room, the judoka between pool contests, the gymnast between apparatus, the high jumper between attempts, the substitute on the bench. No study opened tests what an athlete should do while waiting [practice throughout this section]. The course's tools cover it:

  • The routine is timed back from the start, so waiting before the routine is free time and the routine itself is not stretched to fill it (SP6, SP8).
  • A long gap restarts the routine from its first stage (SP8, section 4).
  • Something to do that is not the competition: music the athlete chose (section 12), a book, a card game with a teammate. Not the phone's results pages (section 12).
  • If-then plans for the gap: "If the event runs late, then I stay seated and restart my routine at minus 20" (section 7).

Every technique follows the same path, taken from SP5's four stages and SP6's ladder [practice; no study opened tests the sequence]: introduced in practice, practised calm, tested under pressure within the athlete's cap, used first in a lower-stakes competition, and kept only if it survives. The deliverable's cards spread it over four sessions:

  1. Session 1, introduce and practise calm. Two minutes of explanation, with the grade said plainly; the athlete writes their version; first use in an ordinary drill.

1717. How to tell a technique is making an athlete worse

Most trials report averages, and SP5 noted that individual athletes vary. A technique can cost an athlete something. Signs, each against the athlete's baseline (SP7) [practice; no study opened tests these signs]:

  • The skill breaks down when they use it. By their own account, they could not use it in most attempts twice running at the same level: SP6's step-down rule 3.
  • Their attention goes onto it. They talk about the technique more than the task; they check whether they did the breathing right. SP4's self-focus account is the reason to watch for this [weak, for the account; SP4].
  • The routine grows. It gets longer each week, has to be done exactly, or causes delays that the sport's time rules will punish.
  • It needs you. They cannot start without your cue from the side. SP7 and SP8 put the cue in the athlete's hands.
  • More of the thing they were trying to stop. Trying to push a worry away and getting more of it is section 2's pattern; switch from control to notice-name-return (section 11).
  • Distress. Tears, shaking, intrusive images, or thoughts about more than sport during or after practising it.

What to do, in order [practice]: drop or simplify the technique, with no comment and no link to selection (SP6, section 4); step down on the ladder; offer notice-name-return in place of a control technique; and, at any sign on SP4's table, stop for that athlete, hold the first conversation and use the route on your SP1 scope card and SP9 referral plan. A. Twelve technique cards

Each card: what it is; when to use it; the script (an example); how to train it in four sessions; its grade; the sample it was tested on; when not to use it; and Milan's version. Where the sample is adults outside sport, or students or novices, the grade is weak and the card says so.

Card 1: What-to-do cue
What it is Every cue, the athlete's and yours, names the action and where it goes, and leaves out the thing to avoid and its word.
When to use it Every instruction before and during competition; every self-talk phrase.
Script (example) Rewrite drill: "Say it as what you'll do. 'Don't lose it' becomes 'Open body, play forward'."
Four sessions 1: list the avoid-phrases the squad hears and says (the coach's included); rewrite each. 2: rondo; anyone who hears an avoid-phrase calls "say it again". 3: the same in a scored rondo, level 1 to 3 by cap. 4: the coach's match-day words checked against the rule.
Grade [weak, positive; inference under 18]. The one study of 13- to 16-year-olds opened found no ironic effect.
Tested on 40 male university hockey players and 73 inexperienced adult dart throwers, within-subject (Woodman et al., 2015); a review of 24 studies, mean age 21.78 (Bartura et al., 2024); 43 female volleyball players aged 13 to 16, no effect (Gorgulu & Gokcek, 2021). No study compared the two kinds of instruction directly.
When not to use it Never as a correction of the athlete in front of others ("you said it wrong"). A safety instruction that must name a danger ("stop at the line") stays as it is.
Milan's version Illustration, not an event: touchline words of the kind a loud coach uses, rewritten: "Show for it" in place of "Stop hiding!"; "Scan, open, forward" in place of "Don't play it back".

Card 2: The cue word the athlete writes
What it is One to three words the athlete writes for one moment, practised for weeks, said once at that moment.
When to use it Before a set action (a serve, a penalty, a start) or at a fixed point (the first touch, the 250 m mark).
Script (example) "Which moment? What do you want to do there? Two or three words. Now say it before the next rep."
Four sessions 1: cue-word builder (B), steps 1 to 4. 2: said aloud, then silently, in a calm drill. 3: in a scored drill within the cap. 4: rehearsal of the moment; keep, change or drop.
Grade [weak, mixed] for the planned phrase (SP5); [weak, no difference shown] for written against assigned; instructional for fine tasks [weak, positive; abstract read; inference under 18].
Tested on 117 athletes aged 13 to 23 who wrote their own phrases, eight weeks, and 42 male university basketball players, null (SP5); 81 collegiate cross-country runners, assigned against freely chosen, no difference (2012, abstract read); a meta-analysis of 32 studies, participants not described (Hatzigeorgiadis et al., 2011, abstract read).
When not to use it A phrase about the athlete's worth ("I'm good enough") used to argue with a worry about selection: that is outside a coach's part (SP5, section 1). A phrase introduced on competition day.
Milan's version "Scan, open, forward", before his first touch in the first fifteen minutes (his SP5 phrase; kept at the week-8 review, added).

Card 3: Self-talk as "you" or by name
What it is The athlete's own cue or encouragement phrased to themselves as "you" or with their first name.
When to use it For motivational statements in effortful stretches; for a cue after an error.
Script (example) "Try your phrase as if a teammate were saying it to you: 'You: scan, open, forward.'"
Four sessions 1: write the "I" and the "you" version. 2: alternate them across reps in a calm drill; the athlete notes which feels usable. 3: the chosen version in a scored drill within the cap. 4: keep whichever the athlete prefers; dropping it costs nothing.
Grade [weak, positive; inference under 18]. Outside sport for the emotional effect.

When not to use it Under 13: not tested in any under-18s, and it asks for reflection on one's own state. Where saying their own name feels to the athlete like hearing a critical adult.

What it is Describing a fast heart and quick breathing before a start as the body getting ready.
When to use it In the warm-up and the last minutes before the start, when the athlete notices the body signals.
