011. The vocabulary, kept apart

Five pairs of words are used loosely on every touchline. The studies below use them in narrower senses, and the senses decide what a finding can tell you.

  • Arousal is physiological activation: heart rate, breathing, muscle tension, alertness. It carries no feeling of its own. In the laboratory study of the inverted-U in section 3, arousal was set by exercise intensity, as a percentage of heart rate reserve (Arent & Landers, 2003, abstract read). A rower at the start line and a gymnast mounting the beam can both be highly aroused; one may feel ready, the other afraid.
  • Stress, in the account used in section 4, is a dynamic interaction between a person and their environment: the person weighs the demands of the situation against the resources they believe they have (Vine et al., 2016). The same match can be stressful for one player and not another.
  • Anxiety is an unpleasant emotional state of apprehension about a possible bad outcome. This definition is the course's working description; no defining source was opened.
  • Trait and state. Trait anxiety is a standing tendency to become anxious in a kind of situation; state anxiety is how anxious a person is at a given moment. The Sport Anxiety Scale-2 (SAS-2) measures trait anxiety about sport performance (Smith et al., 2006, abstract read); the revised Competitive State Anxiety Inventory-2 (CSAI-2R) asks how an athlete feels just before a particular competition (Gómez-López et al., 2020). The IOC's consensus statement on mental health in elite athletes calls pre-competition anxiety state anxiety and says it "is to be expected" among elite athletes competing at major events (Reardon et al., 2019, consensus).
  • Cognitive and somatic. Cognitive anxiety is the worry: thoughts about failing, being judged, what the result will mean. Somatic anxiety is how anxiety is felt in the body: nausea, a racing heart, shaking, tight muscles. The SAS-2 splits sport anxiety into three parts, somatic anxiety, worry and concentration disruption, each scored from five items (Smith et al., 2006). The CSAI-2R measures cognitive anxiety, somatic anxiety and self-confidence (Gómez-López et al., 2020).

Kept apart, these words change the question a coach asks. "Is he too nervous?" mixes all five. "What is he thinking before the match, what does his body do, when does it start and stop, and what does he do because of it?" separates them, and those are the lines of the anxiety profile in the deliverable [practice; the profile's structure is the course brief's, and no study opened tests it].

022. Anxiety, confidence and performance: what the meta-analyses show

Three meta-analyses in athletes were opened. All three pool correlations: they show which measures go together, not what causes what.

  • The CSAI-2 and performance. A meta-analysis of studies that measured cognitive anxiety, somatic anxiety and self-confidence with the CSAI-2 concluded that the relationships among the three and performance "appeared weak", and that self-confidence had the strongest and most consistent relationship with performance (Craft et al., 2003, abstract read on an index page, not the publisher's). The abstract read gives no pooled values or sample sizes, so none is given here.
  • Cognitive anxiety against confidence. A meta-analysis of 48 studies in competitive sport found cognitive anxiety had a small negative correlation with performance (r = −0.10) and self-confidence a positive one (r = 0.24), significantly larger. The cognitive-anxiety correlation was stronger in men (r = −0.22) than in women (r = −0.03), and at a high standard of competition (r = −0.27) than at a low one (r = −0.06) (Woodman & Hardy, 2003, abstract read). The abstract does not give the athletes' ages.
  • Confidence revisited. A 2022 meta-analysis of 41 studies (49 samples, 3,711 athletes, 24 sports, 15 countries) found a pooled correlation between self-confidence and performance of r = 0.25 (95% CI 0.19 to 0.30), which the authors call small, with medium-to-high heterogeneity (I² = 64.49). The correlation was larger in individual sports (0.29) than in team sports (0.14), larger for objective than for subjective performance measures, and larger in all-male samples (0.35) than in all-female samples (0.07). Study quality did not moderate it. "Most participants were adolescents and young adults"; age was not tested as a moderator (Lochbaum et al., 2022). The authors suggest the common premise that higher confidence leads to better performance may be overstated.

What follows for a coach. Worry before competing goes with slightly worse performance on average, and confidence with slightly better [moderate, for a small negative association of cognitive anxiety with performance (one meta-analysis in athletes read in abstract, its intervals and study quality not seen, rule 4; a second meta-analysis, read in abstract, calls the relationships weak; a 2025 scoping review of sport psychology meta-analyses says that three of the anxiety meta-analyses it maps "suggest a negligible or unreliable relationship with performance" (Lochbaum & Lane, 2025), so the size of this association is uncertain); moderate, positive, for the association of confidence with performance (one meta-analysis with heterogeneity its authors flag, rule 4); samples mostly adolescents and young adults]. These are small correlations. They do not show that nerves cause poor play, and they do not tell you that an anxious athlete will perform badly today. Do not read an athlete's nerves as a forecast of their result, and do not tell them that their nerves will cost them [practice; the step from a small correlation to this rule is the unit's, and no study opened tests it].

033. The inverted-U as coaches are taught it, and its limits

The claim as it circulates. Every athlete has an optimal level of arousal in the middle: too little and they are flat, too much and they fall apart, so the coach's job is to psych an athlete up or calm them down towards the top of the curve.

What the study opened found. In a laboratory experiment, 104 college-age participants were randomly assigned to one of eight arousal levels, from 20% to 90% of heart rate reserve, produced by riding a cycle ergometer, and performed a simple reaction-time task while competing for a cash prize. Reaction time followed a curve: a significant quadratic trend, best at 60% to 70% of maximum arousal, accounting for 13.2% of the variance in performance beyond the linear trend. Of the anxiety measures taken beforehand, only somatic anxiety on the Sport Anxiety Scale added to the prediction (Arent & Landers, 2003, abstract read on an index record, not the publisher's page). A second experiment, in 18 physically active young adults (mean age 23.6) on a treadmill, found timing on an easier anticipation task better at 70% than at 90% of heart rate reserve, and on a harder version worse at 90% when the instructions made the trial a competition and cognitive anxiety was highest; the authors' discussion reads this as partial support for a catastrophe model (their conclusion calls the support strong), in which high arousal combined with high cognitive anxiety brings a sharp drop, and found no sign of the hysteresis that model predicts (Duncan et al., 2016, accepted manuscript read).

The limits, as the evidence opened shows them.

  • The curve explains little. In the one experiment that found it, arousal explained 13.2% of the variance in a simple laboratory reaction-time task. Sport skills are not simple reaction time.
  • Arousal is not anxiety. In both experiments arousal was set by exercise. In the second, what went with the drop in performance was cognitive anxiety on top of high arousal. The meta-analyses in section 2 treat worry, bodily anxiety and confidence as separate measures with separate relationships to performance; the inverted-U as taught has one dimension.
  • The samples are young adults in laboratories, not athletes in competition. The first was college-age; the second, physically active adults who regularly competed in sport. Applying either result to a 13-year-old in competition is the unit's inference.
  • Nobody can see the top of the curve. No source opened gives a way for a coach to measure where an athlete is on it on the day.

Grade: for the inverted-U as a coaching rule, [weak; two laboratory experiments in young adults, not athletes in competition, rule 5; inference under 18]. What to do instead: stop aiming at a middle level of arousal. Ask the athlete what they are thinking and what their body is doing, and what they usually need before they compete; the profile in the deliverable records it [practice; no study opened tests this].

044. Challenge and threat

The biopsychosocial model of challenge and threat, as a 2016 review lays it out, sets the athlete's demand evaluations against resource evaluations. When the athlete judges that their resources meet the demands, the state is challenge; when demands are judged to exceed resources, threat. The two are ends of one continuum. Challenge is associated with relatively higher cardiac output and lower total peripheral resistance than threat; some studies have found no link between athletes' self-reports and these cardiovascular markers (Vine et al., 2016; a narrative review, outside the source types the course lists, so no grade rests on it).

What the reviews found.

  • A systematic review of 38 studies that used the model found that "support emerged for the performance benefits of a challenge state", across cognitive and behavioural tasks and across experimental, quasi-experimental and correlational designs, with considerable variation in how the states were measured (Hase et al., 2019, abstract read on the University of Bath's research portal, not the publisher's page).
  • An updated meta-analysis of 62 studies published 2017 to 2024 (7,418 participants, mean ages 13.3 to 72, most samples young adults; domains including mathematics, esports, throwing, academic exams, darts, problem-solving, football and golf) found challenge associated with better performance than threat. Effects were correlations, and "All mean effect sizes for the relationships should be interpreted as small". The challenge-and-threat index taken during performance gave r = 0.16 (95% CI 0.03 to 0.29), taken before performance r = 0.08 (0.02 to 0.14); cognitive evaluations in low-risk-of-bias studies r = 0.18 (0.12 to 0.24). Age, gender and year of publication did not moderate the effects; 65% of studies were rated at low risk of bias, and the evidence for publication bias was limited (Hase et al., 2025, abstract and part of the full text read). The part read did not report results separately for sport and non-sport tasks.

So the athlete's own reading of the situation matters, alongside how aroused they are: two sprinters with the same heart rate in the call room can be in different states. What to do: before a competition, talk about what the task asks and what the athlete brings to it (what they have practised, what they control, what help they have), and avoid adding demands in the last hour, such as selection talk or what the result will mean [weak, positive, for the association of challenge with better performance (a meta-analysis that pools sport with non-sport tasks, so rule 5); practice, for the specific conversation, which no study opened tests; most samples young adults, so for athletes under 18 inference]. Techniques that aim to move an athlete towards a challenge state belong to SP5 and DD-SP3.

055. Attention under pressure: two accounts of choking, side by side

Choking is a colloquial term, which a 2010 review of the field calls pejorative, for a sub-optimal sporting performance under stressful conditions (Hill et al., 2010, abstract read). Two accounts of how it happens dominate the research.

Distraction account Self-focus account
What goes wrong Worry takes up attention. Under anxiety the stimulus-driven attentional system dominates the goal-directed one, so the athlete is more easily distracted, by threat cues among other things (attentional control theory, as summarised in Vine et al., 2016). The athlete starts to monitor or consciously control a skill that normally runs automatically, and the skill breaks down (the explicit monitoring hypothesis and the theory of reinvestment, which Vine et al., 2016 name but do not set out; this description is the unit's summary, from no source opened).
Where attention goes Away from the task: to the result, the consequences, the people watching. Inward, onto the movement itself.
What it predicts (the unit's reading of each account; no source opened states these predictions) Worse performance where the task needs attention and working memory; an athlete who can name worries when asked what they were thinking. Worse performance of well-learned, automatic skills when the athlete attends to their mechanics; less damage if attention is kept off the movement.
Evidence for it Retrospective reports: 70 expert athletes (ages and sports not given in the abstract) asked about their focus under high pressure in competition named worries often and movement execution hardly ever, and the authors conclude their results fit distraction better (Oudejans et al., 2011, abstract read). Gaze studies, by one review's account (Roberts et al., 2019, abstract read). Dual-task experiments, which that review says have often favoured self-focus (Roberts et al., 2019).

The two are not exclusive. The review just cited argues that distraction alone, or distraction together with self-focus, could derail an expert skill, and that self-paced and externally paced sports may differ (Roberts et al., 2019, abstract read). A systematic review of 47 choking-intervention studies across 15 sports (62% of them with experienced athletes; 12 studies with trained novices (the review gives this as 32%; 12 of 47 is 26%); participants recruited mostly from collegiate sports and non-professional clubs, few with elite athletes; mean age 20 to 40 in 68% of studies; golf the most common sport; only four analysed real competition) found that interventions built on both accounts generally helped performance under pressure, and rated most experiments of medium quality (Gröpel & Mesagno, 2019, abstract and text read at the publisher's page). The interventions themselves are SP6's subject.

Two further points bear on sports made of many actions. A narrative review argues that both accounts fit single-action sports, such as shooting, better than sports made of many actions, such as racket sports like tennis or choreography-based sports like figure skating (the review's examples in another section), where early errors may feed further negative thoughts in what it calls "continuous choking" (Wergin et al., 2022; narrative, outside the course's source types, no grade rests on it). And the 2016 review lists gaze changes under anxiety, such as shorter "quiet eye" before basketball free throws, in studies it cites and this unit did not open (Vine et al., 2016).

Grade, for the accounts as explanations: [weak; both supported in part, no meta-analysis of the mechanisms opened, the reviews read in abstract; rule 5; samples mostly adults, so for athletes under 18 inference]. Ask the athlete afterwards where their attention was: on the result and the people watching, or on how they were moving [practice; no study opened tests a coach asking this]. On competition day, give no new technical instruction about how to move; if you give a cue, give one the athlete has practised [practice; the self-focus account suggests it, and the intervention studies behind it were not opened; SP1 grades the same step for Milan as practice].

Why an athlete can train well and compete badly. The sources opened point to what competition adds: evaluation and consequences. In the handball study, the climate's emphasis on comparison went with fear of failure, and fear of failure with anxiety just before competing (Gómez-López et al., 2020). In the interview study, what young athletes feared was what failing would mean about them (Sagar et al., 2007). Section 4's account says the same situation becomes a threat when demands are judged to exceed resources, and section 5's accounts say what threat does to attention. Training usually removes the consequences: a lost ball in a drill decides nothing. That gap is the reason SP6 builds pressure into practice. 077. How competition anxiety is kept going over years, and what adults add to it

Anxiety that lasts five or ten years has usually been kept going by what the athlete, and the people around them, do about it. Four processes appear in the sources opened.

Avoidance. In clinical psychology, avoidance is described as a safety-seeking behaviour: it prevents the feared outcome, and because the outcome does not happen the person credits the avoidance and the threat belief survives untested (Rosebrock et al., 2022, whose introduction states the model; the study develops a questionnaire in patients with psychosis and non-clinical adults, outside sport). In sport the avoidance is often small and inside the game: the volleyball player who serves safe instead of jump-serving, the midfielder who passes backwards, the judoka who avoids taking a grip. The athlete feels relief, the mistake they feared does not happen, and the fear is never tested. Whether this cycle explains competition anxiety in athletes has not been tested in any study opened for this unit [practice; a model stated in the introduction of one clinical study outside sport; no study opened tests it in athletes].

Reassurance. An anxious athlete may ask, again and again, for someone to say it will be fine. No study opened for this unit tests whether a coach's reassurance keeps an athlete's anxiety going, so nothing is claimed about it. What can be said is narrower: a coach cannot know whether an athlete will play well, so an answer to "Do you think I'll play well?" is a prediction the coach cannot make. Answer what you do know: whether they are starting, by the criteria you gave the squad, and what you want them to try [practice; no study opened tests it].

Fear of evaluation and the meaning given to a result. What young elite athletes feared about failing was what it would say about them (Sagar et al., 2007). The IOC statement notes that fear of negative evaluation by others may separate aspects of social anxiety from competitive performance anxiety (Reardon et al., 2019): a sign to note, not to judge. A result carries the meaning the adults around the athlete give it as well as the athlete's own.

What coaches and parents do that feeds it, without meaning to.

  • A climate built on comparison. The handball study in section 6 [weak, association, ages 16 and 17]. SP3's self-audit counts public comparison and selection used as a threat.
  • Parental pressure in a low-mastery climate. In youth swimmers followed across a season, high parental pressure together with a low-mastery or high-ego climate created by the parent went with the highest trait anxiety at every point, and high pressure with low mastery early in the season with rising anxiety; high pressure within a high-mastery climate went with falling anxiety (O'Rourke et al., 2011, abstract read on the University of Bath's research portal) [weak, association; one study; ages not given on the page read].
  • Families accommodating anxiety. A meta-analysis of studies in children and adolescents under 19, outside sport, found family accommodation associated with more severe anxiety: "Positive moderate association was observed for measures administered to parents", and small associations when children reported (Iniesta-Sepúlveda et al., 2021, abstract read on a university repository) [weak, association; outside sport, rule 5; applied to athletes, the unit's inference]. The page read does not define accommodation; how it appears in sport (a parent who lets a child skip competitions that frighten them, for example) is the unit's illustration.
  • A controlling coaching style. In 93 athletes aged 15 to 17 at one Swedish sports high school, followed over 12 months, a controlling coach style went with more expressive suppression (hiding one's emotions) and, which the authors call paradoxical, also with more cognitive reappraisal; a need-supportive style went with more reappraisal; suppression went with worse mental health on a general questionnaire, reappraisal with better (Fröjdö Regborn et al., 2025) [weak, association; one observational study; the authors say causality cannot be inferred].
  • "Just relax." SP1 set instructions about what to avoid against the ironic-effects review, and graded the practised what-to-do cue that replaces them [practice, as SP1 grades it].

Emotion regulation from 16 to 25. SP2 found no source for this band. Two studies were opened for this unit. In the Swedish sports high school above (ages 15 to 17), both reappraisal and suppression rose over the year (the rise in suppression, the authors say, "should be interpreted with caution"), and the authors attribute the change to environmental factors, such as the demands of upper secondary sport education, "rather than age" (Fröjdö Regborn et al., 2025). In 424 competitive athletes in Italy aged 16 to 36 (mean 23.1; individual and team sports; 71% regional level), reappraisal was associated with more excitement and happiness, and satisfaction of the needs for autonomy and relatedness went with more reappraisal; age could not be analysed because of the unequal distribution of ages in the sample (Robazza et al., 2023). The authors state: "The cross-sectional nature of this investigation does not allow to establish causal relationships". So for 16 to 25: reappraisal goes with more pleasant emotion and better general mental health, suppression with worse, and both need-supportive and, paradoxically, controlling coach styles with more reappraisal in one study [weak, association; one longitudinal observational study at 15 to 17 and one cross-sectional study at 16 to 36; rule 5]. Nothing in either says a 17-year-old cannot regulate emotion; how to teach reappraisal is SP5's.

Coach and parent training. The one controlled study of coach and parent training opened for this unit is SP3's: in community basketball, players aged 9 to 15 whose coaches took a 75-minute Mastery Approach to Coaching workshop and whose parents took a 60-minute workshop reported falling sport anxiety over a season (SAS-2 total, 25.44 to 23.98) while a matched league's rose (24.12 to 27.01). The design was quasi-experimental, not randomised, and coach and parent effects cannot be separated (Smoll et al., 2007). Its authors report that "a more recent study" found similar reductions in anxiety; they cite it as Smith, Smoll & Cumming (2007), and give no design for it. It could not be opened for this unit (PubMed returned a browser check; no publisher page was returned by search), so nothing from it is used and it carries no grade. Set a mastery climate and brief parents as SP3 does [weak, positive; one non-randomised controlled study, ages 9 to 15, rule 5; for 16 to 25, inference outside the study's age range].

088. Ordinary nerves, or a problem for a clinician: recognition only

Your part here is to notice, record and refer. Deciding whether an athlete has an anxiety disorder is assessment and diagnosis, which belong to a doctor or a licensed or registered mental health professional.

How clinicians draw the line. The IOC consensus statement says that "patterns of symptom onset, duration and severity should be used to differentiate anxiety disorders" from performance anxiety, while pre-competition state anxiety is to be expected (Reardon et al., 2019, consensus). Its tables of diagnostic criteria rest on three questions:

  • How long. For generalised anxiety disorder, "Excessive anxiety and worry, occurring most days for at least 6 months"; for social anxiety disorder, "Symptoms must be persistent, typically lasting 6 months or more"; for panic disorder, at least one month of persistent concern after a panic attack (Reardon et al., 2019).
  • How wide. For generalised anxiety disorder, worry "about a number of events or activities", not one (Reardon et al., 2019).
  • How much it stops the person. The symptoms "cause significant distress or impairment in important areas of functioning" (Reardon et al., 2019).

These are a clinician's criteria. You do not apply them, and you do not wait six months to refer. They tell you why the deliverable's table asks three things of what you notice: how long it has lasted, whether it has spread beyond competition, and what it is costing the athlete [practice; IOC consensus; the studies behind it were not opened].

The recognition tool. In a paper published online in 2020 and in print in 2021, the IOC set out two tools: an assessment tool (SMHAT-1) for sports medicine physicians and other licensed or registered health professionals, for elite athletes aged 16 and over, and a recognition tool (SMHRT-1) "developed for athletes and their entourage" (Gouttebarge et al., 2021, consensus, abstract read). The International Shooting Sport Federation's page for SMHRT-1 (read 18 Nov 2026) says the tool is to help recognise possible mental health problems, not to diagnose them, and lists red flags that call for immediate help: comments about harming oneself or others; talking about feeling hopeless or so overwhelmed that one cannot function; dramatic weight changes; other highly uncharacteristic behaviour, emotion or appearance; and a sudden episode of overwhelming fear with marked physical symptoms such as sweating or shortness of breath that is new or different from earlier episodes, which could be a panic attack or another medical problem (ISSF, IOC Sport Mental Health Recognition Tool, read 18 Nov 2026). The page says the tool may be copied in its current form; this unit does not reproduce it.

Where your part ends, and who takes over. At the referral. For an athlete in an academy or a national programme, the medical staff, and through them a licensed or registered mental health professional; for a club athlete, the athlete's doctor, with the parents of a minor as the club's medical contact advises; for a university athlete, the student health or counselling service. A red flag goes the same day by the urgent route your club or federation names. A concern about how an adult is treating a minor goes to the welfare or safeguarding officer. SP9 builds the route that fills each "To whom" line [practice; the course's scope line].

Questions to answer before you need them, each with the body to ask:

  • Who must consent before a 17-year-old is referred, and who must be told? Ask the academy's or club's medical lead, or your national health regulator.
  • What may you keep confidential when an athlete tells you about anxiety, and what must you pass on? Ask your federation's safeguarding officer.
  • Do you have any duty to report, and to whom? Ask the national safeguarding body for sport or the child-protection authority.

099. Short check-ins, and what a coach must not conclude from a questionnaire

Check-ins you may use. Two questions, asked the same way, at the same points (before training, on the morning of competition, after it): "From 0 to 10, how nervous are you?" and "What's on your mind about today?". Write down the athlete's own words. Over a few weeks this gives you the athlete's baseline, which SP7's baseline sheet uses [practice; no study opened tests coach check-ins]. A check-in asks how the athlete is; it does not measure a disorder.

Questionnaires you may hear of. Researchers measure sport anxiety with published scales, among them the SAS-2 (15 items; trait sport anxiety; published in the Journal of Sport and Exercise Psychology, 2006, 28(4), 479 to 501: Smith et al., 2006) and the CSAI-2R (17 items; state anxiety before a competition; published in the same journal, 2003, 25, 519 to 533: Cox et al., 2003; the paper itself was not opened, and the item count is from a university summary page). This unit reproduces neither.

What not to conclude from one:

  • A high score is not a disorder. The pages opened describe each scale's development for measuring sport anxiety; none gives a score that marks a clinical problem. The IOC places screening for disorders inside SMHAT-1, which is for licensed or registered health professionals (Gouttebarge et al., 2021) [practice; IOC consensus].
  • A low score does not clear an athlete. An athlete who expects selection to depend on the answer has a reason to answer low. This is the unit's reasoning, not a finding [practice].
  • One score is a moment. A state measure describes one competition; compare an athlete with their own baseline, not with the squad [practice].
  • Do not run screening questionnaires on your squad. SP1's scope line keeps screening with licensed or registered professionals [practice; the course's scope line].

1010. Milan's first fifteen minutes

SP2 passed this pattern to SP4 and SP5. The case register gives it: since about 12, Milan (fictional), 17, plays below his training level in matches; in the first fifteen minutes he hides from the ball and passes backwards; he feels sick on match mornings and sleeps badly the night before; in training he is one of the two best passers in the squad; he has been told "just relax" and "play like you train" for five years; he asks before most matches whether he is starting and whether the coach thinks he will play well; the academy decides at the end of this season whether to keep him.

Read through this unit, without a diagnosis:

  • Vocabulary. Somatic: nausea, broken sleep. Cognitive: not in the case register; the profile below records what he said once his coach began the check-ins in section 9 (an added fact). Behaviour: hiding from the ball and passing backwards, which are avoidance inside the game (section 7).
  • Challenge and threat. The academy decision and his final-year exams in the same weeks (SP2's added fact) are demands; what he believes he brings to them is unknown (section 4).
  • Attention. Whether his attention in those minutes is on worries or on his own movement is a question to ask him (section 5).
  • What keeps it going. Five years of being told to relax; questions to his coach that seek a prediction; a touchline coach who is anxious and loud; a car journey home on which the game is talked through (section 7; SP3's parents' page).
  • Time, spread, cost. Five years: long. Spread beyond match days: not yet asked. Cost: he plays below his level in the matches on which his future is decided.

The writer's opening note

This unit is information, not legal, medical or psychological advice. Every technique and recommendation carries a grade in square brackets from SP1's grade key (section 9 of SP1: five rules applied in order; "several" means three or more; the labels direction and inference). Where the only source opened is a consensus statement whose studies were not opened, the grade is [practice] with the note "the studies behind it were not opened", as in SP2 and SP3. Age bands are SP2's: about 10 to 12, 13 to 15, 16 to 18 and 19 to 25.

This unit is about recognition. It teaches no assessment, no diagnosis and no treatment. Where it describes how clinicians tell an anxiety disorder from ordinary nerves, it does so to explain why the deliverable's table asks about time, spread and cost, not so that you can apply the clinicians' criteria.

The three case athletes return: Dani (fictional), 14, judo; Milan (fictional), 17, football; Sara (fictional), 20, 400 metres. They illustrate; they are never evidence. This unit works mostly on Milan, whose scope card in SP1 deferred the table that tells ordinary nerves from referral to this unit.

How this unit was made4 parts

Provenance

The course's fourth unit.
