011. The day in phases, and what you control in each

A competition day runs through eight phases: the evening before, travel and arrival, warm-up, waiting, performing, between rounds or periods, straight after, and the days after. In each, you control a few things and the athlete controls more. Four rules from earlier units already govern the days before.

  • Nothing new. On competition day an athlete uses only skills already practised calm and under pressure in training, and nothing new is introduced from the night before to the end of the competition [practice; SP5 section 6; no study opened tests it].
  • No new pressure level, and no pressure drill the day before. The last session before a competition runs one level below the athlete's current level on SP6's ladder, with nothing new; the day before carries no pressure drill [practice; SP6 section 8].
  • No new technical instruction about how to move [practice; SP4 section 5].
  • Try the timings in a rehearsal first, where the athlete's cap allows it. SP6's top ladder level is a rehearsal of the competition's timeline, run once or twice a season, well before an important competition and never as the last session before one; it is where this protocol's timings are tried. Dani's, Milan's and Sara's caps are all 3 while their referrals are under way, below that level, so none of them runs it until the cap is lifted (SP6) [practice; SP6 sections 7 and 8].

The evening before: sleep. An expert consensus on sleep in athletes, a narrative review by 19 authors whose text, in the part read, does not describe how the consensus was reached, states that "Sleep disturbance is a common occurrence prior to competition that may impact athletic performance", and that such difficulties "were more evident in individual compared with team sports", citing a study of German athletes. It states that "Athletic performance is reduced by a night or more without sleep" and that the effect of partial sleep restriction over one to three nights, "a more real-world scenario, remains unclear". One of its boxed points, under its tool for banking sleep, says athletes can be confident that "a poor night's sleep the night before competition should not affect their performance", a point given without a citation and hard to square with its own statement that the evidence is unclear (Walsh et al., 2021, consensus, read on a copy of the published article in Liverpool John Moores University's repository, not the publisher's page). A survey of 1,010 Brazilian athletes (mean age 20, SD 7; 511 young athletes and 499 adults; the abstract does not say what age counts as young) who rated their sleep before competition found young athletes 2.30 times as likely to report poor sleep as adults (95% CI 1.47 to 3.61), and athletes in individual sports 3.45 times as likely as athletes in team sports (2.18 to 5.48) (Andrade et al., 2021, abstract read on a sport-literature index record). One survey, measured once: an association.

What follows. Expect worse sleep the night before, especially in a young athlete in an individual sport such as Dani, and do not add to it by making sleep a demand ("you must sleep well tonight") [practice; consensus; the studies behind it were not opened; weak, for the association with youth and individual sport, one survey, rule 5]. Keep the evening ordinary: the usual meal time, the usual bedtime, kit packed earlier in the day. An athlete whose sleep is short on ordinary nights is a different matter: that goes by SP4's table, row 3, and the scope card. Sara (fictional) has slept about five hours a night for six weeks; that is already referred, and the night before a race changes nothing about it [practice; SP1, SP4].

The days before: anxiety rises again in the last hour. In 90 table tennis players aged 15 to 39 (mean 26.3) in the Ghanaian league, international and national level, who rated their competitive anxiety at 7 days, 2 days and 1 hour before a league fixture, anxiety intensity rose at 7 days, fell at 2 days and rose sharply again in the last hour; cognitive anxiety was read as more helpful in the last hour than two days before, self-confidence was highest in the last hour, and, in the authors' main reading, the change over time did not differ by gender or skill level (one sentence of their results says the opposite), although anxiety levels differed between men and women and between skill levels. Some effects were small (Hagan et al., 2017, full text read). One repeated-measures study in one sport [weak, for the description, rule 5; for athletes under 18, inference]. So in the one study opened, nerves rising in the last hour were the expected pattern; it did not test whether anything a coach says changes them. Plan the last hour as the time for the athlete's routine, and keep your own talk out of it (section 2).

022. The last minute: how much instruction an athlete can use, and the case for one cue

What anxiety does to attention. SP4 set out two accounts of what pressure does: worry takes up attention (the distraction account), or the athlete starts to steer a movement that normally runs on its own (the self-focus account). One laboratory study opened for this unit tested the first. In 36 table tennis players (18 skilled college players and 18 less-skilled physical education students), predicting where a serve would land from video clips, high cognitive anxiety slowed the skilled players' responses without lowering accuracy, while the less-skilled players' response times did not change significantly; processing efficiency fell under high anxiety, mainly when the clip was cut off early. The authors read this as support for attentional control theory: "worried thoughts induced by cognitive anxiety occupy some working memory resources" (Ren et al., 2022, full text read). Attention was not measured directly, and the authors note that anxiety in a laboratory differs from anxiety in a match. One laboratory study in college players and students [weak, for the effect of anxiety on processing speed, rule 5; for athletes under 18, inference].

What coaches do in a match. An observation study of six top-level professional youth football coaches, with interviews, found that "The use of silence was the largest single behaviour" during games, a pattern the authors describe as silent monitoring interspersed with short instruction, praise and encouragement (Smith & Cushion, 2006, abstract read on a sport-literature index record). The abstract gives no percentages and no player outcomes: it describes what these coaches did, not what worked. In interviews with 8 coaches and 8 players in elite rugby union, a "succinct" tactical and technical plan for the second half, devised with the players, and a calm coach were what participants described as making half-time work (Smith & Sherwin, 2025, abstract read on the University of Limerick's research record). One qualitative study in elite rugby union; the abstract gives no ages.

The popular claim: a rousing pre-match speech lifts a team. In two studies of youth football, self-efficacy was measured when players arrived at the venue and again straight after the coach's pre-match speech. In 61 players aged 10 to 16 from six teams, self-efficacy differed between the two points, but the difference disappeared once age was taken into account; in 10 male players aged 16 and 17 followed over eight matches, the authors attributed the variation to the particular match, and the speech did not account for it. The authors conclude that the speeches did not appear to influence players' perceived efficacy (Rubio et al., 2018, abstract read on the Complutense University of Madrid's research record). Two small repeated-measures studies with no control group [weak, no effect shown on self-efficacy; rule 5; ages 10 to 17, so for 19 to 25 outside the sampled ages]. Nothing opened tested a speech's effect on performance.

The case for one cue. Put together: in the last minute the athlete's attention is already loaded (Ren et al.; SP4); new information about how to move invites the self-focus that SP4's second account says can break an automatic skill; a speech did not change young players' confidence in the one study opened; and coaches at a high level were mostly silent during play. So the last words are one cue the athlete already uses, phrased as what to do, in their own words, and nothing else [practice, for the one-cue rule; no study opened tests it; weak, for the load anxiety puts on attention, one laboratory study, inference under 18]. The cue itself carries SP5's grade: a planned self-talk phrase practised for weeks [weak, mixed; SP5], phrased as what to do, since instructions about what to avoid ("don't miss", "don't freeze") are set against the ironic-effects review in SP1 [weak, positive, for avoiding them; samples mostly adults, inference under 18; SP1]. DD-SP3 (section 2) opened that review's one study with under-18s: in 43 female volleyball players aged 13 to 16, serving under low and then high pressure in a fixed order with no control group, serve performance did not change overall and no ironic effect was shown, so it grades the effect for 13 to 16 [weak, no effect shown in the one study opened].

What the last minute sounds like, in three sports:

  • A swimmer behind the blocks: "Long first ten." Then nothing.
  • A volleyball player going in as a substitute to serve: "Your toss." Then a nod.
  • A gymnast saluting the judges: "Tall through the turn." Then you step back.

What it does not sound like: a list of three things; a reminder of what the result means; "just relax"; a change to the plan; a prediction ("you'll walk this"). SP4 and SP7 set the rule for "Do you think I'll win?": answer what you know, make no prediction [practice; SP4, SP7].

033. Your behaviour and expressed emotion on the sideline, and what it has been found to do

What the studies show. Two field studies in competitive team sports, reported together (one cross-sectional, in baseball and softball; one in football with measures before, during and after a game), found that coaches' expressions of happiness and anger predicted players' own happiness and anger, players' inferences about how well they were playing, and team outcomes; happiness was linked to better team performance and anger was not (van Kleef et al., 2019, abstract read on the University of Amsterdam's repository). The abstract gives no sample sizes or ages. These are associations; they cannot show that a coach's anger lowered performance [weak, for the association; two field studies, abstract read, rule 5; ages not given, so for under-18s, inference].

In interviews with 13 professional and national-level athletes, athletes said they could tell when their coach was stressed, "typically via a variety of verbal and behavioural cues"; most of the effects they described were negative, and they judged coaches less effective when stressed (Thelwell et al., 2017, abstract read on Loughborough University's repository). In a survey of 264 collegiate athletes aged 18 to 23 from 36 teams in interactive sports, developing a questionnaire on their preferences for coaches' pre-competition speeches, about 71% said they always wanted the coach to appear confident and about 73% that they always wanted the coach to be genuine; whether the coach should be emotional drew the most varied answers (Zimmerman et al., 2025, full text read). Preferences, not outcomes. The rugby study in section 2 described a calm, emotionally controlled coach as the condition for a useful half-time (Smith & Sherwin, 2025, abstract read).

What is not known. No study opened for this unit, or for SP7, measures what a coach's attempts to regulate an athlete's emotion do to the athlete. So this unit claims nothing about the effect of calming an athlete down from the sideline. The guidance below concerns your own display.

What follows for you.

  • Your face and voice are read during competition. Keep anger out of what you show; show what you want the athlete to feel and do [weak, positive, for the association of displayed happiness with team performance, and none shown for anger; van Kleef et al., 2019, abstract read, as above; the step to your behaviour is the unit's; inference under 18].
  • Be calm and genuine; do not perform a mood you do not feel. "Good press, keep it" is honest; a fixed grin over a 0-3 deficit is not [practice; adult athletes' stated preferences in one survey (Zimmerman et al., 2025); no study opened tests faked against genuine displays].
  • Mostly watch. Speak when the rules allow it and you have one thing to say [practice; Smith & Cushion, 2006, abstract read, describe what top-level youth coaches did; no outcome was measured].
  • "Harsh coaching builds toughness." On a touchline it takes the form of shouting at a player after an error. SP1 and SP3 graded the claim [weak, against]; nothing opened here changes that.

Milan's coach (fictional) is autonomy-minded in training and anxious and loud on the touchline. SP3 planned a second audit filmed from the bench side; SP7's repair script names a touchline shout. Section 11 gives his coach the skills to work on it.

044. Resets between rounds, periods and attempts

Most sports give the athlete gaps: the minutes between judo contests in a pool, half-time in football, the hours between a 400 m heat and a semi-final, the break between gymnastics apparatus, the seconds between points in tennis or between ends in archery. A reset is what the athlete does in the gap to end the last action and start the next one.

What is known. SP6 graded a pre-performance routine practised under pressure [moderate, positive; SP6, from one meta-analysis of athletes aged 11 to 40; for 10-year-olds outside the sampled ages] and gave every routine a reset for when something interrupts it [practice; SP6]. No study opened for this unit tests a reset between rounds or periods as such. The half-time study described recovery first, then a short plan, with a calm coach (Smith & Sherwin, 2025, abstract read).

So a reset between rounds has three parts, in this order [practice; no study opened tests the order]:

  1. Recover. Water, warm clothing, sitting or walking as the athlete's plan says; the body first.
  2. Close the last round. One sentence from the athlete about it, if they want to say one. Not a review: the review comes after the competition (section 7).
  3. Open the next. The athlete's own routine (SP6), which ends in their cue. If you add anything, it is one cue they have practised, about the next action [practice; section 2].

What not to do in a gap: replay the last round's mistakes; give a new tactic that has not been practised; talk about the result so far or what is needed to qualify, unless the athlete asks [practice; SP4 section 4 asks you to keep added demands, such as what the result will mean, out of the last hour (weak, positive, for the association of a challenge state with performance; inference under 18; SP4's grade)].

For a long gap, such as hours between a heat and a final, the routine starts again from its first stage: a shorter warm-up, then the call-room elements, timed back from the next start [practice; SP6's builder].

055. What your sport's rules allow you to say, and when

Rules on coaching during competition differ between sports, between competitions and between age groups, and they change. This unit states no rule as yours. Before the competition, answer these questions for your sport and the event, and write the answers on the protocol (the deliverable has a line for them):

  • When may you speak to the athlete? At any time, only during set breaks (between points, at stoppages, at half-time, between contests), or not at all during the event?
  • From where? A marked coaching area, a seat at the edge of the field of play, the stands, or nowhere inside the competition area?
  • How? By voice only, by signs, by written notes, by electronic devices? Is any of these banned?
  • Who? How many coaches are accredited, and does the accreditation need a qualification or a safeguarding check?
  • What happens if you break it? A warning, a penalty against the athlete, removal of the coach?
  • For minors, are there extra rules on who may accompany them into warm-up and call areas?

Ask: the competition's technical rules as published by your national or international federation for that event and age group; the event's technical meeting or manager; the referee, judge or technical delegate on the day. Write down the date you read the rule and where. A rule you have not checked for this event goes on the protocol as "not checked", and you assume the stricter reading [practice; the course's rule that no law or rule is stated as the reader's].

066. Several athletes on one day, and who gets you when

A club competition can put ten of your athletes on the mat, the track or the pool deck on one day; a multi-event meeting can put two of yours on the start line at the same time in different places. No study opened tests how a coach should share themselves out. The steps below are practice [practice; no study opened tests them].

  • Plan from the timetable. The day before, list each athlete's start times and the phases around them (warm-up, waiting, between rounds). Mark the clashes.
  • Decide by need, and say so in advance. In a clash, the athlete whose baseline sheet shows they stay near you before competing, or who has asked for you (SP7's script 1), the youngest, and the one with a referral under way come first, in that order, unless an injury or a red flag takes you elsewhere. Tell athletes and parents before the day who will be with each athlete at each time.
  • Name a second adult. For each clash, another accredited coach, a team manager or an adult senior athlete who knows the athlete's routine, holds the cue, knows the urgent route and has whatever check your club's safeguarding policy requires of adults who look after minors. They do not coach technique.
  • Do not let the result decide. Being with the athlete who is winning, and away from the one who has just lost, is ego-involving attention, the climate SP3 linked to fear of failure in 479 handball players aged 16 and 17 [weak, association; SP3's grade; for 10 to 15 and 19 to 25 outside the sampled ages].
  • The athlete who loses early. Make time for them before the end of the day, even briefly; with the youngest, in person.

Supervision of minors over a long day, and especially overnight, is a safeguarding matter. What adult-to-athlete ratio and what rules on transport, rooms and changing areas apply to your squad? Ask: your club's welfare or safeguarding officer, and your federation's safeguarding policy for travel and events.

077. After a loss and after a win

When to talk. In interviews with 36 girls aged 12 to 15 in team sports (football, volleyball, basketball and others), athletes wanted their parents after a game to read their mood and not press for discussion straight away, to give them time with their team, and then to give positive, honest feedback: positive points, a couple of pointers, then more positive points (Knight et al., 2011, full text of the accepted manuscript read on Swansea University's repository). Qualitative, one sample, about parents rather than coaches; applying it to a coach is this unit's inference. A video study of 98 car rides to and from practices and competitions, filmed by 28 parent–athlete pairs (athletes aged 11 to 16, six sports), found that "sport-related conversations made up only 12.9%" of the interactions, that most of the time was silent, that parents mostly asked closed questions, and that after their own praise, statements of confidence or comments on what to improve, parents cut in or left the athlete no room to reply in about half the cases (Tamminen et al., 2022, full text of the author's manuscript read on the University of Toronto's repository). The study did not record whether rides followed wins or losses, and did not ask athletes what helped. Observational; it describes, it does not test.

So: the athlete's state first, against their baseline (SP7's state-response table); the talk when they are ready and want it, often at the next session; open questions, and room to answer [practice; weak, for athletes' stated wish not to be pressed straight after a game, one qualitative study of 12- to 15-year-old girls about parents, rule 5; for 16 to 25 outside the sampled ages; the application to coaches and to boys is the unit's inference]. In order: an injury, illness or red flag goes to medical help or the urgent route now; an athlete not yet back near their baseline gets you near or the space they asked for, and no review; an athlete who is back near baseline but does not want to talk agrees a time, usually the next session; one who wants to talk talks first, about one thing they controlled and one thing that went well [practice].

What to talk about: what the athlete controls, and what they do not. The course's working distinction, which this unit takes from coaching practice and from SP5's goal types: the athlete controls their preparation, their routine, their process goals and their effort; they do not control the opponent, the officials, the draw, the weather, the result or selection. A debrief stays mostly on the first list [practice; SP5 graded preferring process goals for competition (weak, positive; inference under 18)].

How the athlete explains a result has been studied, with mixed findings. In 153 athletes from 17 UK university teams (mean age 20.5 among the 163 first recruited) asked after a win or a loss to explain the result, how controllable they judged its cause was not significantly associated with their collective efficacy (their belief in the team's capability) for the next match, after either a win or a loss (Murray et al., 2020, full text of the accepted manuscript read on the University of Stirling's repository). In 111 elite athletes aged 13 to 36, measured once, explaining bad events in a more internal, stable, global, controllable and intentional way went with more self-handicapping (Török et al., 2022, full text read). Two correlational studies, neither in favour of pressing an athlete to blame themselves [weak, for these associations; rule 5; Murray et al. in university athletes, so for under-18s inference]. So the "control what you can" frame is offered as a way of choosing what to work on next, never as a judgement that the athlete caused the loss [practice].

After a loss. The athlete talks first ("What did you see?"). One thing they controlled that they will keep; one thing they will change; one thing that went well. Nothing on technique straight after, unless they ask [practice; SP7 section 8, with its package grade for encouragement after mistakes (weak, positive; ages 9 to 15, so for 16 to 25 outside the sampled ages)].

After a win. The same questions. A win does not mean every process was right, and a loss does not mean every process was wrong; the debrief asks about the process either way. Praise effort and the process the athlete chose, not the result alone [weak, positive, for recognition tied to effort and improvement as part of a coach programme; ages 9 to 15, so for 16 to 25 outside the sampled ages; SP3's grade].

088. A first title and an early title, and what the days after need

SP2 graded the evidence that early success forecasts later success: entering a talent programme younger went with higher junior and lower senior performance [strong, for the association; SP2], and successful juniors and successful seniors are largely different people [weak, for junior success as a poor forecast of senior success; SP2]. Dani (fictional) won a national title at 13 and is now growing fast.

What athletes report after a big competition. Four British female athletes interviewed after the 2016 Olympic Games described highs around the Games and lows after returning home, including the loss of the attention they had received; the authors read the low as a normal response to returning home (Howells & Lucassen, 2018, abstract read on the Open University's repository). In 49 Danish Olympic athletes surveyed one month after the Tokyo Games, 27% reported either below-average well-being or moderate to severe depression scores, and 16% both; there was a tendency, not statistically significant, for athletes who missed their performance expectations to report more negative experiences (Diment et al., 2023, abstract read at the journal's page). Adults at the Olympic Games: a long way from a 14-year-old's national title. Applied to younger athletes and smaller events, these are this unit's inference [weak, for the description; a qualitative study and a small survey, rule 5].

What the days after need [practice; no study opened tests these steps]:

  • Sleep and recovery. Evening matches are "commonly associated with later sleep onset time", the sleep consensus notes, and with shorter time in bed and less sleep (Walsh et al., 2021, consensus). Plan the next morning's session lighter or later after an evening final [practice; consensus; the studies behind it were not opened].
  • Back to ordinary. The next session is an ordinary session: the same climate, the same expectations, no new goals set in the glow of a win or the sting of a loss.
  • The debrief at an agreed time (section 7).
  • The title as one data point. Talk about what the athlete did to win it, not about what it says they will become; keep their goals about improvement and skill [practice; SP2].
  • A low after a high. Expect some flatness after a big event. Flatness that lasts weeks, spreads beyond sport, or comes with poor sleep or withdrawal goes by SP4's normal-or-refer table and the scope card; a red flag goes the same day by the urgent route. Deciding what it is belongs to a doctor or a licensed or registered mental health professional [practice; SP1 scope line; SP4].
  • The coach's own identity. Do not build your own reputation around an athlete's early title, in what you say to them or to others (SP2) [practice].

099. Parents on the day

What athletes want from parents at competitions. The 36 girls aged 12 to 15 in section 7 wanted parents, during games, to encourage the whole team and not only their own child, to focus on effort over the result, to stay positive when the team was losing, and to control their own emotions; and not to draw attention to themselves or their child, not to coach, and not to argue with officials (Knight et al., 2011). Before games, they wanted help getting ready on time and calm encouragement. A narrative review of parenting in sport (outside the course's source types, so no grade rests on it) notes that the line between parental pressure and support is "not necessarily clear-cut", that the timing of parents' behaviour before, during and after competitions changes how children perceive it, that watching a child compete when upset or losing can be hard for parents, and that few parent-education strategies have been evaluated (Knight et al., 2017, accepted manuscript read on Swansea University's repository). SP3 graded parent education as a short, specific briefing at the start of the season [weak, positive: programmes evaluated in a few small studies of mixed design; SP3] and parental support going with more enjoyment [weak, association; SP3].

What to ask of parents on the day [weak, for the behaviours young athletes said they wanted, one qualitative study of 12- to 15-year-old girls in team sports; for boys, other ages and individual sports, the unit's inference; practice, for the format]:

  • Get the athlete there on time, with kit and food; keep the morning calm and ordinary.
  • In the stands: cheer effort and the whole team; leave instructions to the coach; leave officials alone; keep your own emotion in check.
  • Where to stand or sit: where the event and the coach ask, out of the athlete's warm-up and waiting areas. Dani's father stands at the edge of the mat (case register); the note to her parents asks for a place in the stands, and the reason.
  • After: let the athlete come to you; let them have time with the squad; let them choose whether to talk, in the car and at home; if they do, ask what they enjoyed before asking about the result. The car-ride study found most rides silent, which is allowed (Tamminen et al., 2022).
  • Concerns: about the coach or selection, in private, at another time; about health, by the route on the page; about anyone's behaviour towards a child, to the welfare or safeguarding officer.

For an adult athlete, the page goes to the athlete, who decides what to share (SP3). Sara's parents follow results online; Sara decides what they hear from her.

1010. Competitions over several days

A two-day championship, a week-long tournament or a judo event with a team day after the individual one repeats the day's phases, with three things added [practice; no study opened tests a multi-day plan].

  • Sleep across nights. Expect poorer sleep before the first day and after evening sessions (section 1; Walsh et al., 2021). Keep evening routines the same each night; keep screens, meetings and reviews out of the last hour before bed where you can [practice; consensus; the studies behind it were not opened]. 1111. Your own nerves, and how to manage them with SP5's skills

Coaches are under pressure on competition day too. A narrative review of psychological stress in sports coaches (outside the course's source types, so no grade rests on it) concludes that its "potential health and performance costs" for coaches "are significant", and that coaches' stress needs to be monitored and addressed (Fletcher & Scott, 2010, abstract read on a sport-literature index record). Athletes said they could tell when their coach was stressed and judged them less effective when stressed (Thelwell et al., 2017, abstract read). In 13 elite coaches interviewed about their own use of psychological skills, self-talk and imagery were "cited more frequently than relaxation and goal setting", for purposes in training and in competition, before, during and after (Thelwell et al., 2008, abstract read on the University of Portsmouth's research portal). In a survey of 2,272 Italian coaches during the COVID-19 lockdown, reappraisal was associated with lower perceived stress and suppression with higher (Santi et al., 2021, abstract read at the publisher's page). One cross-sectional survey, outside competition.

None of SP5's skills has been tested on coaches' competition-day stress. The grades below are SP5's, from studies of athletes and students; applied to a coach, they are this unit's inference [practice, for using them on yourself; SP5's grades for each skill, with inference].

  • A process goal for yourself. One behaviour you will do on the day, such as "One cue per player at stoppages, then silence", checked afterwards by a colleague or on film [weak, positive, for process goals; SP5; inference for coaches].
  • A self-talk phrase for your moment. The moment your voice rises (a mistake in the first minutes, a bad call): "Watch, then one word" [weak, mixed; SP5; inference for coaches].
  • Breathing early, not as a performance fix. Two minutes of slow breathing, about six breaths a minute, before the warm-up or at half-time, if you have practised it. In the studies SP5 opened it changed heart rhythm; no study opened showed that it improves anyone's performance, yours or the athlete's [weak, positive, for heart-rate variability; weak, no performance benefit shown; SP5].
  • Imagery of your own behaviour. The evening before, rehearse the moment you find hardest (the early error, the referee's call) and see yourself doing your process goal in it [moderate, positive, for imagery in athletes; SP5; inference for coaches].
  • Notice and name. "There's my nerves; back to watching." The acceptance approach, for coaches whose attempts to calm down have not worked [moderate, positive, for athletes; SP5; inference for coaches].
  • A plan for the athlete when you are not at your best. If you are unwell or overwhelmed on the day, the second adult named in section 6 takes over the athlete's cue and routine [practice].

Your own nerves are also noise in the athlete's environment: a raised voice, a change of plan, a long talk in the last minute. DD-SP3 deals with the athlete shutting out noise; this section is the part of that noise that is yours.
