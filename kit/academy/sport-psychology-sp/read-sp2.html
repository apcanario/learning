011. Three clocks that run apart

Three different ages describe every athlete you coach.

  • Chronological age: years since birth. It decides the age group, the school year and most selection dates.
  • Biological age: how far the body has gone towards a mature state. The National Strength and Conditioning Association (NSCA), a US professional body, defines maturation in its position statement on long-term athletic development as progress towards a mature state that varies in timing, tempo and magnitude between bodily systems, and states that "individuals of the same chronological age can differ markedly with respect to biological maturity" (Lloyd et al., 2016, consensus).
  • Training age: in this course, the years of systematic training in the sport. The NSCA statement does not use the term; it asks that training be set by "the technical competency, training history, and stage of growth and development of the child", which is the same idea (Lloyd et al., 2016).

The International Olympic Committee's (IOC) consensus statement on youth athletic development, written after an expert meeting in November 2014, describes maturation as running through roughly the first two decades of life, and says that changes in a young athlete's performance may be "more related to biological clocks than to coaching and training" (Bergeron et al., 2015, consensus).

Two athletes of the same chronological age can therefore differ by years on the other two clocks. A swimmer of 12 who began at 6 and has not started her growth spurt, and a swimmer of 12 who began at 11 and is mid-spurt, will give you the same date of birth and different bodies and different skill histories.

What to do with this: record the three clocks separately for every athlete under about 16, and set technical and load demands by training history and growth stage as well as by age group [practice; consensus of the NSCA and IOC; no study opened for this unit tests recording the three clocks against not recording them].

022. The timing of puberty: performance, selection and self-image

Performance. A systematic review and meta-analysis of 13 studies of young athletes compared early and late maturers within the same age groups. In boys, the early-maturing groups had higher kinanthropometric values (standardised mean differences 0.37 to 2.31) and better physical-fitness results (0.17 to 2.31); in girls only height and body mass differed (0.96 to 1.19) (Albaladejo-Saura et al., 2021, abstract read). The abstract gives no sample sizes, sports, ages or test-by-test results, and the full text was not opened, so none is given here. The studies compare groups at one point in time; they show an association with maturity status, not a cause.

The IOC statement gives the growth in the numbers: from 8 to 16 years peak aerobic power rises by about 80% in girls and about 150% in boys, and girls often outscore boys on cycling peak power, an anaerobic test, until about 12 because they mature earlier (Bergeron et al., 2015). The NSCA statement adds that during the growth spurt a child may lose some motor control and whole-body coordination for a time, "commonly termed 'adolescent awkwardness'" (Lloyd et al., 2016).

Selection. The IOC statement describes youth sport as selective, with a "maturity-associated selection/exclusion process commonly occurring during the interval of puberty", mostly between about 9 and 15, and a "maturity-related gradient in selection favouring early maturing males in many sports" (Bergeron et al., 2015). It names no sport in which late-maturing girls are favoured, and this unit opened no study that does. The bibliography's review of maturation in youth athletes (Malina et al., 2015) could not be opened, so no figure from it is used.

So in a basketball trial for under-13s, or an under-14 football squad, the boy who is bigger, faster and stronger today may be the one who started puberty first. Do not select or drop a 10- to 15-year-old on size, speed or strength alone; judge those against how far through growth the athlete is and against their own rate of improvement [moderate, positive, for the maturity–performance association in boys (one meta-analysis in athletes, read in abstract, its intervals, sensitivity analyses and study quality not seen, rule 4); practice, for the selection rule (IOC consensus; no study opened tests it, rule 2)].

Self-image and mood. The brief asks what maturation does to self-image. No study of body image and pubertal timing could be opened for this unit; that part is unsourced here. On mood, a 2025 systematic review and meta-analysis of 13 studies (434,838 girls and women, not athletes; 5 cohort, 8 cross-sectional) found earlier menarche associated with more depression than menarche at the usual age (odds ratio 1.36, 95% CI 1.20 to 1.53; in adolescents 1.34, 1.15 to 1.56), and later menarche not associated with less depression. Heterogeneity was high, there was evidence of publication bias, most studies were cross-sectional, and confounders such as body mass and adversity were not consistently controlled, so the direction of the link is unknown (Ghadirzadeh et al., 2025). Boys were not studied.

For a coach this is a reason to notice and nothing more. A girl who matures early is not at risk because of it, and you cannot tell from her growth who is low in mood. If you notice persistent change against her own baseline, your part is the first conversation and the referral route in your SP1 scope card; assessing mood belongs to a doctor or a licensed or registered mental health professional, and SP9 builds the route [weak, for the association (studies outside sport, rule 5); inference for athletes; the referral rule is the course's scope line, practice].

033. Relative age effects

When children are grouped by year of birth, those born early in the selection year are up to almost twelve months older than those born late. The IOC statement puts it plainly: grouped by age, "older individuals generally outperform younger ones" (Bergeron et al., 2015).

The first meta-analysis of relative age in sport pooled 38 studies published from 1984 to 2007 (253 independent samples, 14 sports, 16 countries). It found the effect consistently, "but with small effect sizes"; the effect grew with the gap in relative age, and was largest for adolescent males aged 15 to 18 at regional and national level in highly popular sports. Its abstract gives no pooled odds ratio and does not say how many samples were female; the authors ask for research on whether the effect exists in female contexts (Cobley et al., 2009, abstract read at the DOI address on 17 Nov 2026 and re-read on a sport-literature database record, not the publisher's page). The second meta-analysis opened is on female sport: 57 studies, 308 independent samples, 25 sports, data from 1984 to 2016. Girls and women born in the first quarter of the selection year were over-represented against those born in the last quarter, odds ratio 1.25 (95% CI 1.21 to 1.30), which the authors call small. The effect was larger in pre-adolescent (11 and under) and adolescent (12 to 14) groups, at higher levels of competition, and in team sports and individual sports with high physical demands (Smith et al., 2018, abstract read). Birth quarter and selection are correlated here; the design cannot separate maturity, size, experience and selectors' expectations.

Relative age and maturation are two different clocks. A girl born in the last month of the selection year can still mature early; a boy born in the first month can mature late. In a handball club's under-12 squad the two can add up or cancel out.

What to do: list your squad's birth months beside each selection decision for under-15s, and ask, for every athlete you are about to leave out, whether you would make the same call if they were six months older [practice; no study opened tests this check; the existence of the effect is moderate in female sport (Smith et al., 2018: one meta-analysis in athletes, read in abstract, its sensitivity analyses and study quality not read, rule 4); in sport overall it is moderate, with small effects (Cobley et al., 2009: one meta-analysis in athletes, read in abstract on a database record, rule 4)].

044. The brain from adolescence into the twenties, and the claim that it "finishes at 25"

What is established, from the sources opened:

  • Brain change is protracted and differs by measure and region. The largest charting study pooled 123,984 MRI scans from 101,457 people aged from before birth to 100 years, from more than 100 earlier studies, about 10% of them longitudinal. Cortical grey matter volume peaked at 5.9 years (95% interval 5.8 to 6.1); regional grey matter peaks ranged from about 2 to 10 years across 34 cortical areas; subcortical grey matter peaked at 14.4 (14.0 to 14.7); white matter volume peaked at 28.7 (28.1 to 29.2). The data were "biased towards European and North American populations" (Bethlehem et al., 2022). This is a description of volumes by age in non-athletes; it measures no ability.
  • Individuals differ from the average. A review of adolescent brain development argues that the field's reliance on group averages "obscures meaningful individual variation in development", and names socioeconomic status, culture and peer environment as sources of that variation (Foulkes & Blakemore, 2018, abstract and figure captions read).
  • Behaviour reaches adult levels before the brain stops changing. A study of executive function (holding and updating information, inhibiting a response, switching between tasks) pooled four datasets, two longitudinal and two cross-sectional, 10,766 people aged 8 to 35 and 23 measures from 17 tasks. Performance changed fastest from about 10 to 15, less from 15 to 18, and was "stabilizing to adult-levels in late adolescence (18–20 years old)". More than 95% of the age-related change in accuracy came before 18. The authors say individual variation around this average is expected (Tervo-Clemmens et al., 2023). Non-athletes; laboratory tasks.

The claim examined. "The brain finishes developing at 25" was set against its evidence in SP1. The two papers in the course list that address the age of brain maturity directly, Somerville (2016) and Sawyer et al. (2018), could not be opened at a publisher's page, PubMed or their DOI addresses; only a library record and a repository record were seen, so no claim here rests on either (Somerville, 2016, not opened; Sawyer et al., 2018, not opened). For this unit, two steps were taken instead. First, a widely cited review of adolescent brain maturation was opened in abstract (Arain et al., 2013); the abstract names no age at which maturation ends. Second, the measures opened put no line at 25. White matter volume is still rising after 25. Executive function reaches adult levels at 18 to 20. Grey matter volumes peak in childhood and early adolescence. No source opened for this unit states 25 as an end point, and none shows a single end point. Whether some other measure ends near 25 cannot be settled from what was opened; the claim is neither confirmed nor refuted for every measure, and for the measures opened it does not hold.

For coaching: nothing in the brain-imaging data tells you what to ask of an athlete. The behavioural data do bear on one thing: in the study opened, the cognitive control measured in the laboratory was at adult levels by 18 to 20. Do not withhold decisions about training, selection or their own career from a 19- to 25-year-old on the ground that their brain is "not finished" [weak, against the claim; one large behavioural study outside sport, rule 5]. Sara (fictional), 20, has told her coach she "isn't finished yet" (SP1's claim-check sheet); this unit adds that, of the measures opened, the one closest to self-control in action, executive function, was at adult levels by 18 to 20.

055. Reward, peers and evaluation: what does not follow from a brain image

A review of developmental imaging studies argues that the popular account of adolescence as a time when a slow-maturing frontal brake lags a fast accelerator fits the data poorly. The authors report that evidence for slow maturation of cognitive-control regions is relatively inconsistent, while evidence is more consistent for increased limbic responses to rewards, emotional faces and social feedback, peaking in mid-adolescence. They propose, as a heuristic model, that adolescence is a period of social and emotional engagement in which goals and motives are reorganised, with adaptive value as well as risk (Crone & Dahl, 2012).

A heightened brain response is not a behaviour. Two behavioural experiments opened for this unit tested what the popular version predicts, that teenagers take more risks when peers watch:

  • A laboratory gambling task with 40 adolescents aged 11 to 18 and 24 adults (28 recruited, aged 20 to 38; 4 excluded) (the peers were photographs of fictitious same-age participants). Alone, adolescents and adults did not differ. Under observation, adolescents took more high-risk gambles than adults, d = 0.95; the authors read the gap as adults choosing more safely when observed while adolescents kept choosing risky options at the same rate; the change within each group was not tested (Haddad et al., 2014).
  • A balloon-pumping risk task with 184 participants aged about 9 to 19, alone and with a same-age, same-sex peer apparently watching by webcam: "peer observation reduced risk-taking but increased exploration tendencies", with the exploration effect clearest in female pre-adolescents, and male adolescents' risk-taking not changed by peer observation (Lorenz & Kray, 2022).

Neither sampled athletes, both used strangers as peers, and neither tested a teammate, a crowd or a coach.

What follows for a coach: do not assume that a 13- to 15-year-old will take more risks because teammates are watching, and do not assume the opposite; watch what your athlete does in front of others, in your sport [weak; two behavioural experiments outside sport that agree only in part, rule 5; inference]. What does not follow: any coaching rule from the imaging finding alone, such as motivating with prizes because the teenage brain responds to reward. SP3 and SP7 grade their own advice on behavioural evidence.

066. Self-regulation and emotion regulation across the four bands

Self-regulation (cognitive control). The executive-function study in section 4 gives the shape for non-athletes: steep change from 10 to 15, smaller from 15 to 18, adult levels by 18 to 20 (Tervo-Clemmens et al., 2023).

Emotion regulation. One study was opened: 254 school pupils aged 9 to 16 in eight Spanish schools, completing two self-report questionnaires once. Read in full, the age differences are small and do not all run one way. On one questionnaire's strategies the two age groups did not differ once corrected for multiple tests; on the other, the 13- to 16-year-olds reported more self-blame and more planning and less positive refocusing, small effects. A combined emotion-regulation score differed by age only slightly (partial η² = 0.02), and in the paper's table the younger group scored higher, although its abstract says the younger group scored lower on the strategies. Gender had no main effect on the combined score; girls scored higher than boys at 9 to 12 and lower at 13 to 16 (partial η² = 0.07) (Sanchis-Sanchis et al., 2020). A single cross-sectional, self-report study, from which, as the authors say, "no causal relationships can be established"; the bibliography's other studies were not opened. For 16 to 25, no source on emotion regulation was opened for this unit; SP4, section 7, grades two studies in athletes that overlap this band (one at 15 to 17, one at 16 to 36), both weak, and the reader should take that band from SP4.

By band, as inference from non-athletes: at 10 to 12 cognitive control is changing fastest, and the one emotion-regulation study opened found only small differences between 9 to 12 and 13 to 16, not all in one direction; prompt and model a routine and keep instructions short; at 13 to 15 cognitive control is still changing steeply; at 16 to 18 it approaches adult levels; from 18 to 20 it is at adult levels in the laboratory data [weak, for the description of change by band; inference under 18; one cross-sectional study and one behavioural study outside sport, rule 5; practice, for prompting, modelling a routine and keeping instructions short, which no study opened tests, rule 2]. The age-and-stage table carries these by band.

Milan (fictional), 17, plays below his training level in the first fifteen minutes of matches and has done since about 12. Nothing in these data says a 17-year-old cannot regulate; they say the capacity measured is close to adult levels by 17 (inference from non-athletes). SP4 and SP5 take up his pattern now.

077. Identity from 16 to 25: junior to senior, and the end of a career

The International Society of Sport Psychology (ISSP) position stand on career development and transitions treats an athlete as a whole person whose development has six interacting layers: athletic, psychological, psychosocial, academic-vocational, financial and legal (Stambulova et al., 2021, consensus; full text read at the publisher's page). From it:

  • Transitions come in kinds. Normative transitions are predictable (junior to senior, retirement); non-normative ones are not (injury, deselection); the stand adds quasi-normative transitions, predictable for a particular category of athletes, such as cultural transitions.
  • Junior to senior. The stand describes this transition as lasting months to years, citing research in Swedish ice hockey that divides it into preparation, orientation, adaptation and stabilisation phases; it gives no success rate.
  • Dual career. It defines a dual career as "a career with foci on sport and studies or work", and says that athletes who cannot cope with its demands face elevated stress, compromised mental health, burnout and dropout, and names personal resources and support among the factors that help.
  • The end of a career. It reports a review of 126 studies (1968 to 2010) that found adjustment to retirement depends on athletic identity, education and finances, and is helped by planning, a new career or interest, and support. That review itself was not opened for this unit.

Athletic identity is how far a person defines themselves by sport. The stand names it as a factor in how athletes adjust when a career ends; no study measuring it in 16- to 25-year-olds was opened for this unit, so no size of effect is given.

What to do with athletes from about 16: ask, once a season, what they want from sport this year and from life outside it; keep study or work in the plan; and treat deselection, a long injury and the first senior season as transitions to prepare for [practice; ISSP consensus; the studies behind it were not opened, so the grade rests on the consensus alone]. Sara (fictional) is in three at once: first senior season, back from a season lost to injury, second year at university.

088. Sleep need by age, and what the consensus statements say

The hours below are given only as each consensus statement gives them, for the ages it names.

  • 6 to 12 years: 9 to 12 hours per 24 hours, on a regular basis. 13 to 18 years: 8 to 10 hours. A panel of 13 voting members, including a moderator, used a modified RAND appropriateness method, after a literature search that left 864 articles for review. Regularly sleeping the recommended hours is associated with better attention, behaviour, learning, memory, emotional regulation, quality of life, and mental and physical health; regularly sleeping less is associated with attention, behaviour and learning problems and with more accidents, injuries and depression (Paruthi et al., 2016, American Academy of Sleep Medicine, consensus; hours, panel, search and associations as given in its methodology paper, Paruthi et al., 2016b). The evidence reviewed was in children and adolescents in general, not athletes.
  • Adolescents (IOC): a recommended optimal 8.5 to 9.5 hours, with later bedtimes and wake times in adolescence, widespread insufficient sleep made worse by training and competition schedules, and "preliminary evidence indicating an increased likelihood of injury with insufficient sleep" (Bergeron et al., 2015, consensus). The IOC range sits inside the AASM range for 13 to 18.
  • Adults 18 to 60: 7 or more hours per night on a regular basis; regularly sleeping less is associated with weight gain, diabetes, high blood pressure, heart disease, depression and other harms; more than 9 hours a night "may be appropriate for young adults". Fifteen experts, 5,314 articles, the same method (Watson et al., 2015, American Academy of Sleep Medicine and Sleep Research Society, consensus).

The athlete-specific expert consensus on sleep in the course's reading (Walsh et al., 2021) could not be opened, so this unit gives no recommendation from it.

What to do: ask each athlete how long they usually sleep on school or work nights, and set the time of early and late sessions so that the hours for their age are possible [weak, positive, for the hours by age (consensus built on studies outside sport, rule 5; inference for the athlete populations); practice, for asking and timing sessions, which no study opened tests, rule 2]. A sustained drop against an athlete's own usual sleep is something to notice and raise; treating it is outside your part: persistent sleep problems with low mood or withdrawal go to the route in your SP1 scope card, and SP9 builds it [practice; the course's scope line]. The IOC's suggestion of "tailored training and even perhaps schooling schedules" is its consensus, untested in any study opened here [practice].

099. Early specialisation, sampling and the 10,000 hours

The consensus statements. The IOC statement says that youth "should avoid early sport specialisation", that young people who specialise after puberty tend to have fewer injuries and stay in sport longer than those who specialise early, and that diversification between and within sports should be encouraged (Bergeron et al., 2015, consensus). The NSCA statement defines early specialisation as year-round intensive training in one sport to the exclusion of others, links it to overuse injury, possible overtraining or dropout and a narrower motor base, and says youth "should not train in excess of 8 months per year in a single sport" (Lloyd et al., 2016, consensus).

The studies of who becomes a champion. A meta-analysis of 51 study reports (477 effect sizes, 6,096 athletes, 772 of them among the world's best) found that adult world-class athletes, compared with national-class athletes, had done more practice in other sports in childhood and adolescence, started their main sport later, progressed more slowly at first and accumulated less main-sport practice. Among youth athletes the pattern ran the other way: higher performers started their main sport earlier and did more of it (Güllich et al., 2022, full text read on a copy of the published article, not the publisher's page). Correlational: it compares groups by their histories and cannot show that the multi-sport practice caused the later success.

A scoping review of 38 studies of sport participation and physical activity models, 33 of them based on the developmental model of sport participation, found the claim that sampling does not hinder elite participation supported by every study that tested it, and the claim that sampling goes with longer participation supported by six studies, five of them cross-sectional; most studies found that many successful athletes specialised only after 16, but "specific age ranges have yet to be consistently demonstrated". Most evidence came from elite athletes and 71% of studies were cross-sectional (Gallant & Bélanger, 2021).

The 10,000 hours, tested. The claim as it circulates: 10,000 hours of practice makes a champion. A meta-analysis of deliberate practice and sports performance found that deliberate practice accounted for 18% of the variance in performance overall and "accounted for only 1% of the variance in performance among elite-level performers" (an estimate the full text bases on six effect sizes from 228 athletes); athletes who reached a high level had not started their sport earlier in childhood than lower-level athletes (Macnamara et al., 2016). In its full text, read on a copy of the published PDF, not the publisher's page, the paper mentions 10,000 hours only in describing an earlier study of violinists. The world-class athletes in Güllich et al. (2022) had accumulated less main-sport practice than national-class athletes. Against the evidence opened, the number of hours in the main sport did not separate the best from the very good, and no peer-reviewed source opened for this course states 10,000 hours as a threshold. A popular book that spread the figure is not a source and is not cited.

What to do with athletes about 10 to 15: keep other sports and varied practice in their week and do not reward single-sport volume for its own sake [moderate, positive, for the association between early multi-sport practice and later world-class performance (one meta-analysis in athletes, abstract read at the journal's page and full text on a copy of the published article, not the publisher's page; its senior contrasts' intervals exclude zero and it reports no study-quality rating, rule 4); the age boundaries are practice, untested]. A 12-year-old tennis player who also plays football and swims has, on the evidence opened, the history more common among the world-class athletes in that meta-analysis. That is an association and promises nothing for this player.

1010. How weakly early success predicts adult success

Talent programmes. A meta-analysis of 51 effect sizes from 6,233 athletes (82% male; studies from 2009 to 2022, nine countries; regional to international level) found that higher-performing juniors had entered talent promotion programmes younger (d = −0.53, 95% CI −0.74 to −0.32; 16 effects) and higher-performing seniors had entered them older (d = 0.56, 0.40 to 0.72; 34 effects). The primary studies were rated high quality; heterogeneity was high (I² about 75% for each estimate, the paper's Table 2), which the authors do not discuss. Citing earlier studies, the authors call successful juniors and successful seniors largely two disparate populations, and report annual athlete turnover of 25% to 47% in youth sport academies and 28% to 55% in federations' junior squads. The studies are correlational: the pattern could come from who is selected, from what programmes do, or both (Güllich & Barth, 2024).

One sport, followed over time. One study, described on a repository copy as a retrospective study of Italian high and long jumpers from youth to senior level, is not opened (Boccia et al., 2017, not opened). Its DOI address redirected on 17, 18 and 19 Nov 2026 and was not followed, and its full text was read only on a repository copy, which the course does not count as opened; so none of its figures or conclusions is used in this unit, and no grade rests on it.

The IOC statement says, from its side, that for most young athletes "the probability of elite success is highly unlikely", and it describes a disconnect between advocated talent systems and their outcomes (Bergeron et al., 2015).

A champion of 13. A national title at 13 is a fact about 13. Treat it as one data point [strong, for the association: entry to a talent programme at a younger age went with higher junior and lower senior performance (one meta-analysis in athletes, effects excluding zero, high-quality studies, no disagreeing review found, rule 3; correlational, so no causal claim); weak, for junior success as a poor forecast of senior success: a systematic review of 110 prospective samples (38,383 junior athletes) and 79 retrospective samples (22,961 senior athletes) found successful juniors and successful seniors to be "largely two disparate populations"; for example, international-level under-17/18 juniors and international-level seniors were "7.2% identical and 92.8% disparate" (figures from its abstract; the figure behind them was not seen) (Güllich et al., 2023, a synthesis of percentages with no meta-analysis or controlled study, rule 5; descriptive, so no causal claim)]. Keep the athlete's goals about improvement and skill, keep other sports and other parts of life in the week, and do not build the athlete's identity, or your own, around the title [practice, for these steps; untested]. Dani (fictional) won a national title at 13 and is now growing fast; on the evidence opened, neither fact tells you what she will be at 20.

A late developer of 17. A 17-year-old who is behind today may be on the slower early trajectory that the world-class athletes in the meta-analyses showed. Keep the door open: where you control selection, record the athlete's rate of improvement over two or three seasons beside their current level, and say so to the athlete [practice, for the step; untested: no study opened for this unit tests recording rate of improvement as a selection step; Boccia et al. (2017), section 10, was not opened]. Milan (fictional) has played in the academy since 11 and faces a decision at the end of this season; his training performance and his match performance tell two different stories, and the decision-makers should have both.

The writer's opening note

This unit is information, not legal, medical or psychological advice. Every technique and recommendation carries a grade in square brackets from SP1's grade key (section 9 of SP1: five rules applied in order, "several" meaning three or more, and two labels, direction and inference). A grade from adults, students or non-athletes applied to an athlete under 18 carries the label inference.

The unit works in four age bands: about 10 to 12, 13 to 15, 16 to 18 and 19 to 25. The bands come from the course brief; no study opened for this unit draws its boundaries there, and section 4 says what the brain data do and do not show about them. Where a study sampled other ages, the unit says so.

The three case athletes return: Dani (fictional), 14, judo; Milan (fictional), 17, football; Sara (fictional), 20, 400 metres. They illustrate; they are never evidence.
