011. The cognitive-behavioural model in plain words, and where teaching ends

The model. Cognitive-behavioural approaches rest on one idea: what an athlete feels and does after an event depends on what they make of it. A narrative review of rational emotive behaviour therapy (REBT), which it describes as regarded by many scholars as the original cognitive-behavioural therapy, sets this out as an ABC framework: an activating event (A), the beliefs about it (B), and the emotional and behavioural consequences (C), with A leading to C only through B (Turner, 2016; open a standard textbook or handbook chapter on the cognitive-behavioural or ABC model, cite it by edition and chapter, and replace the Turner citation with it. Until then: "a narrative review, outside the source types the course lists; the model is unsourced by the course's standard, reported as this review states it, and no grade or recommendation rests on it)".

This unit uses a five-part plain-words version, which is its own and is not taken from any source:

  • Situation: what is happening. A tennis player serving at 4–5 in the third set; a swimmer in the call room; Milan (fictional) receiving the ball in the first fifteen minutes.
  • Thought: what goes through the athlete's mind. Milan's words at a check-in (SP4): "If I give the ball away early, they'll decide I'm not good enough."
  • Feeling: the emotion: fear, frustration, excitement.
  • Body: what the body does: a racing heart, nausea, tight shoulders.
  • Behaviour: what the athlete does: Milan passes backwards; the swimmer goes out too fast; the server pushes a safe second serve.

Each part feeds the others. The skills in this unit give the athlete a way in at a different point: goals and self-talk work on the thought; breathing and relaxation on the body; attentional focus and imagery on the action itself, where attention goes during it and how it is rehearsed; mindfulness and acceptance on the athlete's relationship with the thought and the feeling, without trying to change them.

Teaching a skill against treating a problem. The same model underlies cognitive-behavioural therapy, which clinicians use to treat anxiety disorders and other conditions. Teaching a skill and treating a problem differ in who, what and why:

You, teaching a skill A clinician, treating a problem
Who The whole squad, or any athlete who wants it One person referred with a problem
What A technique practised in training, for performing An assessment, a formulation, a treatment plan
Why To perform and to train better To relieve distress or impairment
Material The athlete's sport: the drill, the race, the match Whatever the person brings, including life outside sport

The REBT review states that treating athletes for mental illness is "ethically beyond many practitioners' professional competencies", meaning sport psychology practitioners, not coaches (Turner, 2016). A coach is further from that line. So, under SP1's scope line [practice; the course's scope line]: you teach skills inside normal training; you do not use a skill to work on an athlete's beliefs about their worth, their life or their past; and when skill practice brings up distress, thoughts that reach beyond sport, or a sign on SP4's normal-or-refer table, you stop the exercise for that athlete, hold the first conversation and refer by your scope card. Milan's profile in SP4 already offers referral; the skills block in the deliverable runs alongside that referral and never replaces it [practice; SP4].

022. What the meta-analyses of psychological skills training show overall, and on whom

Psychological interventions as a whole. A meta-analysis of randomised controlled trials of psychological and psychosocial interventions in athletes, against no treatment or placebo, found at post-test g = 0.57 (95% CI 0.22 to 0.92; 35 studies, 997 participants) and at follow-up one to four weeks later g = 1.16 (0.25 to 2.08; 8 studies, 189 participants), with "some" evidence that effects were greatest when coaches delivered the intervention (Brown & Fletcher, 2017, abstract read). The abstract gives no ages.

Three families of technique. A 2024 review of 111 studies pooled 25 of them in five meta-analyses. Against controls: multimodal psychological skills training g = 0.83 (95% CI 0.21 to 1.45; 7 studies), mindfulness- and acceptance-based approaches g = 0.67 (0.01 to 1.32; 6 studies), imagery g = 0.75 (0.14 to 1.36; 5 studies). Restricted to randomised trials, none stayed significant: training g = 0.51 (−1.58 to 2.59), mindfulness and acceptance g = 0.66 (−0.11 to 1.43), imagery g = 0.81 (−0.14 to 1.75). In the pooled analyses, self-talk, goal setting and relaxation appeared only inside multimodal packages, and breathing was not reported. On whom: 54 of the 111 studies sampled university athletes, the reported mean ages in the meta-analyses ran from 19.1 to 35, and younger athletes were excluded by design: "High school athletes were considered equal to junior level participants and were therefore excluded." (Reinebo et al., 2024, full text read). SP1 reports the same review; this unit adds the randomised-only values and the exclusion.

What psychological variables go with performance (flag F2). The 2022 review of 30 sport psychology meta-analyses could not be opened in this session (both its DOI address and its publisher's page were refused by the session's permission check), so nothing here rests on it; SP1, section 6, reports its variables half from a full-text read on 18 Nov 2026"" (delete). A 2025 scoping review by two of the same authors, of 73 meta-analyses with athlete samples and performance outcomes published from 1988 to 2025, reports confidence–performance effects ranging from 0.25 to 0.30, describes three anxiety meta-analyses as suggesting a negligible or unreliable relationship with performance, and reports that process-goal setting, mindfulness and psychological skills training often produced medium-to-large effects while short-term breathing manipulations were negligible or inconsistent. Its authors did no quality appraisal and pooled nothing; its effect-size tables are images and were not read (Lochbaum & Lane, 2025). Its ages: athletes from youth to elite level, with no breakdown.

Read together: positive pooled effects, wide intervals, effects that lose significance when only randomised trials are kept, and samples of adults, many of them university athletes. Teach psychological skills as part of training [moderate, positive (rule 4: meta-analyses in athletes with unstable estimates); samples mostly adult where ages are reported (the 2017 abstract gives none), the 2024 review excluding junior athletes, so for athletes under 18 an inference]. Where you can, teach them yourself, inside training [weak, positive: a moderator result in one meta-analysis read in abstract, rule 5].

Does any technique reach strong (flag F1)? No. Every technique graded below fails rule 3: its meta-analyses report low certainty, unclear or high risk of bias, heterogeneity, or effects that do not survive restriction to randomised trials, or there is no meta-analysis in athletes at all. The best grade any skill earns here is moderate.

033. The skills, one by one

Each skill: what it is, the evidence opened with its sample and design, its grade, and how to teach it in each age band. The teaching steps by band are this unit's practice, untested in any study opened, unless a study is named beside them.

3.1 Goal setting

What it is. A goal is a standard the athlete aims at. Three kinds are distinguished in coaching and in the research the unit could reach: an outcome goal is about a result against others (win, medal, selection); a performance goal is about the athlete's own measured standard (a time, a percentage of first serves in); a process goal is about how the athlete performs an action (drive the arms through the bend, scan before receiving). These working definitions are the unit's.

The evidence opened. The sport meta-analysis the brief points to, which compared the three kinds, was read in abstract only, on a university repository page: process goals had the largest effect on performance (d = 1.36), performance goals a smaller one (d = 0.44) and outcome goals a negligible one (d = 0.09); process goals also had a large effect on self-efficacy (d = 1.11), and specific and non-specific goals did not differ significantly (d = 0.37 against 0.72). The abstract does not say who took part, and gives no confidence intervals, no number of studies for each kind, no ages and no rating of study quality (Williamson et al., 2024, abstract read). A scoping review that reports it adds that "there are vastly different effect size values across process, performance, and outcome goals" (Lochbaum & Lane, 2025).

One randomised study in young athletes was opened. In 24 male competitive swimmers aged 15.2 on average (12 per group, all nationally ranked), an individual time goal for the 100 m freestyle, set from the swimmer's own times, against the instruction to do their best, over eight weeks, went with a 4.3% improvement against 2.5% (p = 0.004); total mood disturbance on a mood questionnaire rose 47.1% in the goal group against 19.1% in the control group (the paper's percentages; its table means give different changes). Process goals were not tested (Ferchichi et al., 2026). Its authors call it a time-based performance goal, which matches this unit's definition of a performance goal.

Grade. Setting a specific, individual performance goal: [weak, positive; one randomised study in 24 male swimmers aged 15.2 on average, range not reported, rule 5; for 19 to 25 outside the sampled ages]. Preferring process goals for competition: [weak, positive; a sport meta-analysis read in abstract only, whose participants are not described and whose study quality, samples and ages were not seen, rule 5; for athletes under 18 an inference until the samples are seen]. Watch mood when you add a demanding goal: [weak; one study, the same swimmers].

Teaching by band. About 10 to 12: one process goal per session, set by you, said in a few words, checked at the end ("Did you scan before you received?"). About 13 to 15: the athlete chooses one of two process goals you offer, and one performance goal per month from their own records, never from rankings (SP3's climate). About 16 to 18: the athlete writes a process goal for each competition and a performance goal for the block, and you agree them. About 19 to 25: the athlete owns the goals; you ask what they are and whether training serves them. Outcome goals stay out of training talk at every band [practice; SP3's task-involving climate].

3.2 Self-talk

What it is. Self-talk is what athletes say to themselves, aloud or silently. As a skill it is a short, planned phrase. Instructional self-talk says what to do ("Eyes up, scan"); motivational self-talk raises effort or confidence ("Go now", "I'm ready"). The usual advice is to match them to the task: instructional for skills that need precision, motivational for effort and endurance. These definitions and the matching advice are stated here as they circulate in coaching; the self-talk meta-analysis in the course's reading was read in abstract only: across 32 studies (62 effect sizes) self-talk gave a moderate positive effect on sport-task performance (ES = .48); it was more effective for fine than for gross tasks, and for fine tasks instructional self-talk was more effective than motivational. The abstract also reports larger effects for novel than for well-learned tasks, and for interventions that included self-talk training than for those that did not. The abstract does not say how many participants were athletes or report a matching result for motivational self-talk on effort tasks (Hatzigeorgiadis et al., 2011, abstract read). The systematic review in the reading list could not be opened.

The evidence opened. Two randomised studies in athletes.

  • In 117 athletes aged 13 to 23 (mean 16.0; canoe racing, gymnastics, rhythmic gymnastics, swimming, wrestling, ice hockey, handball, volleyball, and judo in the paper's table), 104 of whom remained after 13 withdrew, randomised to a one-week self-talk training (three 60-minute sessions), an eight-week training (three 20-minute sessions a week) or no training, athletes developed and practised their own self-talk. Coach-rated performance improved only in the eight-week group; somatic state anxiety fell in the one-week group by follow-up; the coaches knew who took part, which the authors list as a limitation (Walter et al., 2019).
  • In 42 male university basketball players (mean age 20.9), randomised to instructional, motivational or no self-talk, neither kind changed three-point shooting against no self-talk, in quiet or under noise (no significant intervention effect or interaction; the authors read a buffering of noise on static shots from simple effects), and the paper reports no direct comparison of the two kinds (Yang et al., 2024).

Grade. A planned self-talk phrase, practised for weeks: [weak, mixed; two controlled studies in athletes, one positive at eight weeks on coach ratings, one null on shooting, rule 5; the positive study sampled ages 13 to 23, so it covers 13 to 23; for 10 to 12 an inference from older athletes]. Instructional self-talk for fine, precise tasks: [weak, positive; a meta-analysis of sport-task studies read in abstract, participants not described as athletes, rule 5; inference for under-18s]. Motivational self-talk for effort and endurance: [practice; no result in the abstract read]. SP1 and SP4 graded Milan's practised what-to-do cue [practice]; with these two studies opened, a self-talk cue grades [weak, mixed].

Teaching by band. About 10 to 12: one word or two, chosen with the child, linked to a movement they can see ("Reach"); say it aloud together in drills. About 13 to 15: the athlete writes their own phrase, as in the eight-week study; practise it aloud, then silently. About 16 to 18 and 19 to 25: the athlete writes phrases for set moments (before a serve, after a mistake, at the bell) and tests them in training. At every band, phrase it as what to do; SP1 sets instructions about what to avoid against the ironic-effects review [weak, positive; as SP1 grades it].

3.3 Imagery

What it is. Imagery is rehearsing an action or a situation in the mind, with as many senses as possible: seeing the bar, feeling the take-off, hearing the gun. SP1 set the popular claim that the brain cannot tell imagined from real action against its evidence; imagining and doing were not the same brain activity in the study it opened.

The evidence opened.

  • Athletes, all ages. A 2025 multilevel meta-analysis of 86 randomised studies (3,593 athletes; ages not pooled, ten studies gave none) found SMD = 0.5 (95% CI 0.34 to 0.67). The authors rate the certainty of the evidence low; more than half of the studies were at unclear risk of bias, and the funnel plot was asymmetric, the pooled effect was no longer significant when only non-significant studies were kept, though their S-value analysis found no plausible level of bias that would remove the effect. Longer programmes went with larger effects (100 days 0.62, 50 days 0.47, 20 days 0.38), three sessions a week with larger effects than one (0.59 against 0.09) or seven (−0.13), and imagery combined with other skills with larger effects than imagery alone (Liu et al., 2025). On session length the paper's numbers do not settle it: ten-minute sessions 0.51, 20 minutes 0.22, 45 minutes 0.93 with an interval that crosses zero.
  • Players aged 9 to 18. A meta-analysis of nine trials of imagery for the tennis serve (548 participants aged 9 to 18, from beginners to national level) found serve accuracy improved, g = 0.99 (95% CI 0.68 to 1.30; 8 trials, 237 participants), and speed not significantly, with certainty low for both (very low for serve technique). Programmes ran 4 to 24 weeks, one to three sessions a week, 3 to 25 minutes a session (mean about 13) (Deng et al., 2024).
  • Children and adolescents learning motor skills. A review of 22 randomised trials in 934 participants aged 9 to 18 (pupils, young athletes in several sports, and children with motor or intellectual difficulties) could pool only two studies (SMDs 0.83 to 1.87), rated risk of bias from some concerns to high and certainty low, and concluded that imagery combined with physical practice may have high potential (Behrendt et al., 2021).
  • The 2024 review in section 2: g = 0.75 against controls, not significant in randomised trials alone (Reinebo et al., 2024).

Grade. [moderate, positive; meta-analyses in athletes that fail rule 3 on certainty and bias, rule 4; for ages 9 to 18 the evidence is the tennis serve, certainty low for serve accuracy and serve speed and very low for serve technique, and imagery paired with physical practice]. Practise it about three times a week over months, not daily for a week [weak, positive; moderator results in one meta-analysis, rule 5].

Teaching by band. About 10 to 12: one short scene of one skill, eyes open or closed, straight before doing it physically [weak, positive, for pairing with physical practice; low-certainty review, ages 9 to 18]. About 13 to 15: a 2- to 3-minute script of one action seen and felt, then the action. About 16 to 18 and 19 to 25: the athlete builds scenes of their own competitions, including recovering from a mistake; the athlete writes the script.

3.4 Breathing and relaxation

What it is. Slow breathing in one study opened meant about six breaths a minute, with a slightly longer out-breath (4.5 seconds in, 5.5 out) (Laborde et al., 2019). Progressive muscle relaxation means tensing and releasing muscle groups in turn.

What the breathing studies measured. Mostly the heart, and some laboratory tasks; none opened measured performance in competition.

  • Heart rate variability. A meta-analysis of 223 studies of voluntary slow breathing found increases in vagally mediated heart rate variability (a heart-rhythm measure linked to the parasympathetic nervous system) during the breathing, immediately after a single session, and after multi-session programmes (Laborde et al., 2022, abstract and parts of the text read). The parts read give no pooled values and do not say how many participants were athletes. This is a physiological result. It says nothing about whether an athlete then plays, runs or fights better.
  • A laboratory task after exercise. In students at a sport university (two experiments, 107 analysed, ages 18 to 41; their sport background was not assessed), one 17-minute session of breathing at about six breaths a minute, against watching a documentary, went with fewer errors on a colour-word inhibition task (0.36 against 0.69), no change in reaction time and no change in perceived stress; the heart-rhythm measure did not explain the effect (Laborde et al., 2019).
  • A laboratory motor task. In 35 healthy kinesiology students whose athletic status was not reported (mean age 21.7), slow breathing raised heart rate variability and slowed reaction time on a precise grip task; fast breathing raised errors and breathlessness (Buchanan & Janelle, 2022).
  • Across meta-analyses with athlete samples, short-term breathing manipulations were negligible or inconsistent for performance (Lochbaum & Lane, 2025; values not seen).

Relaxation. In 24 collegiate track-and-field athletes (about 21 years; 14 trained, 10 controls; randomised within gender, with participants replaced after randomisation), progressive relaxation twice a week for a month before a provincial games went with higher self-confidence in weeks 1 and 2 only (g = 0.8 and 1.0), no difference in somatic or cognitive anxiety, and personal bests in 7 of 14 against 2 of 10, a difference the authors' model did not establish (one-sided p = 0.074) (Liang et al., 2021). In 59 male basketball players aged 14 to 19 at one club, 12 sessions of progressive muscle relaxation lowered heart rate against controls; the groups differed on cognitive anxiety before the start, and the sample was chosen for convenience by team availability, no randomisation is reported, and the trained group was older (mean 15.97 against 14.33) (Battaglini et al., 2022, abstract read).

Grade. Slow breathing to raise heart rate variability: [weak, positive; a meta-analysis not limited to athletes, rule 5; inference for under-18s]. Slow breathing to improve performance: [weak, mixed in the laboratory, no competition outcome tested; laboratory studies in sport students and kinesiology students, one with fewer errors, one with slower reactions, rule 5; inference for under-18s]. Progressive muscle relaxation: [weak, mixed; two small controlled studies in athletes, ages 14 to 19 and about 21, rule 5]. So: teach breathing as a way to settle the body between efforts and early in a routine the athlete chooses, and say what it does in the studies: it changes heart rhythm. Do not tell an athlete it will make them play better [practice; the step from the studies to the instruction is the unit's]. Where a task needs a fast reaction (a sprint start, a goalkeeper facing a penalty), do not put slow breathing in the last seconds before it [weak; one laboratory study in students not described as athletes, rule 5; inference].

Teaching by band. About 10 to 12: count breaths with the child (in for four, out for six) for one minute at the end of a session. About 13 to 15 and older: the athlete chooses where breathing goes in their routine, practises it after hard efforts in training, and stops if they feel dizzy or short of breath [practice].

3.5 Attentional focus: external against internal

What it is. An internal focus is attention on the body's movements ("bend your knees"). An external focus is attention on the movement's effect on the world ("push the floor away", "send the ball to the far post"). A distal external focus is further from the body (the target) than a proximal one (the racket face). These definitions are the unit's working ones.

The evidence opened.

  • Adults learning sport skills. A network meta-analysis of 12 studies, one of them a counterbalanced crossover (566 healthy adults (eligible from 18 to 65; reported mean ages about 19 to 27), mostly novices; badminton, golf, basketball, volleyball, long jump, football, darts) found an external focus better than an internal one at retention, SMD = 0.56 (95% CI 0.20 to 0.92), and at transfer, 0.35 (0.02 to 0.68); subject blinding was at high risk of bias in every study, and allocation concealment and therapist blinding in all but one, and post-acquisition heterogeneity was substantial (Favre-Bulle et al., 2024).
  • Distance of the focus. A meta-analysis of 20 randomised trials (497 physically fit participants, one study of children, mean age 7) found a distal external focus better than an internal one, SMD = 0.59 (0.14 to 1.05), and a proximal external focus better than internal, 0.32 (0.13 to 0.51). The benefit of a further focus appeared in experienced performers; on novices the authors write that "there is no evidence that novices can benefit from external focus at any distance" (in novices no comparison was significant: distal against proximal, and each distance against internal focus) (Zang et al., 2025).
  • Athletes. The 2024 review pooled four crossover studies (49 athletes in baseball, table tennis and golf, mean ages about 19) and found no significant difference between external and internal focus, which its authors say may reflect low power (Reinebo et al., 2024).
  • The bias re-analysis. A 2024 robust Bayesian meta-analysis of the external-focus literature found moderate to strong evidence of publication bias in every analysis; after correction the estimated effects were negligible (performance g = 0.01, retention 0.15, transfer 0.09, distance effect −0.01), and its authors write that their results "contradict the existing consensus that an external focus is always more effective" than an internal focus (McKay et al., 2024, abstract read).
  • The reviews of Wulf (2013) and Chua and colleagues (2021) in the course's reading could not be opened in this session; nothing is reported from them.

Grade. Give instructions and cues that point attention at the movement's effect: [contested; two meta-analyses disagree on whether the effect exists, one finding it in mostly novice adults and a bias-corrected re-analysis, read in abstract, finding it small to nil, rule 1; in athlete samples one small meta-analysis found no significant difference; samples mostly adults, so for under-18s an inference]. This fits SP4's self-focus account of choking, which says attention on mechanics under pressure can break an automatic skill.

Teaching by band. At every band this is mainly a change in your words. About 10 to 12: give the target, not the joint ("Land like a cat on the mat"). About 13 to 15 and 16 to 18: before a drill, ask the athlete where they will put their attention, and offer an external option. About 19 to 25: the athlete chooses their cue; keep technical internal coaching for early learning in quiet blocks, away from competition [practice].

3.6 Mindfulness and acceptance, the alternative to control

What it is. The skills above mostly try to change something: a thought, a heart rate, a feeling. Mindfulness and acceptance approaches aim instead at noticing thoughts, feelings and sensations as they are, letting them be there, and keeping attention on the task and on what matters to the athlete. For an athlete who has been told "just relax" for years, as Milan has, the message changes from "get rid of the nerves" to "you can play with the nerves there". The description is the unit's; the reviews below define their interventions in their own terms.

The evidence and its quality.

  • A meta-analysis of nine controlled trials (290 healthy athletes over 15, 13 sports, programmes from 4 weeks to over 2 years) rated the trials of fair quality (mean quality score 5.4 on the Physiotherapy Evidence Database scale) and found large effects on mindfulness scores (SMD 1.03, 90% CI 0.67 to 1.40), moderate-to-large effects on psychological measures (0.72, 0.46 to 0.98), and, in shooting and darts, on performance (1.35, 0.61 to 2.09) with very high heterogeneity (I² = 82%) (Bühlmayer et al., 2017, abstract read).
  • A systematic review of 66 studies of any design (n = 3,908) reported large effects in randomised trials against no treatment on mindfulness, flow, performance and competitive anxiety, and graded the evidence low quality: "None of the included studies were rated as having a low risk of bias" (Noetel et al., 2019, abstract read).
  • The 2024 review: g = 0.67 (0.01 to 1.32), not significant in randomised trials alone or with an outlying study removed (Reinebo et al., 2024).

Grade. [moderate, positive; meta-analyses in athletes of fair-to-low quality whose estimates are unstable, rule 4; samples over 15, so for 10 to 15 an inference]. The reviews agree the evidence is weak in quality and that larger randomised trials are needed; the parts read report no harms and do not say whether harms were measured.

Teaching by band. About 10 to 12: a minute of noticing sounds or the breath, framed as a focus game, nothing about feelings [practice; inference]. About 13 to 15: notice-and-name ("there's the worry; back to the ball") as part of a routine. About 16 to 18 and 19 to 25: short guided practices of a few minutes, and the acceptance message for athletes whose control strategies have not worked. Programmes in the studies ran for weeks to years with practice between sessions; a single talk is not the intervention that was tested [moderate, for the programmes as tested].

044. How a skill is taught, and how long that takes

Four stages, in order [practice; no study opened tests the sequence]:

  1. Explain. In two or three minutes: what the skill is, when it is used, and what the evidence says it does, with its grade. Ask the athlete where it might fit in their sport.
  2. Practise calm. In ordinary drills, with nothing at stake, until the athlete can do it without prompting.
  3. Practise under pressure. In drills with a score, a consequence or an audience. SP6 builds the pressure ladder and its step-down rule; in this unit's block the pressure is light: a scored game, a timed rep, a teammate watching.
  4. Use in competition. Only after stages 2 and 3, and first in a competition that matters less.

How long. In the studies opened, interventions lasted weeks to months: skills-training packages from about 3.5 to 30 hours in total, mindfulness and acceptance programmes about 4 to 8 hours over several weeks (Reinebo et al., 2024); mindfulness programmes from 4 weeks to over 2 years (Bühlmayer et al., 2017, abstract read); imagery for the tennis serve 4 to 24 weeks, mean about 11 (Deng et al., 2024). Two findings speak to dose directly: imagery effects were larger after 100 days than after 20 (Liu et al., 2025), and in the self-talk trial the eight-week programme changed coach-rated performance where the one-week programme did not (Walter et al., 2019). So a fortnight introduces a skill; it does not finish it. Keep each skill going after its fortnight, a few minutes at a time, for the rest of the season [weak, positive, for longer practice; moderator results and one randomised comparison, rule 5].

Ten minutes inside the session. The imagery sessions in the tennis studies averaged about 13 minutes (Deng et al., 2024), and the eight-week self-talk sessions were 20 minutes (Walter et al., 2019). Ten minutes is shorter than most sessions tested; it is chosen so that skills fit inside training, and is the unit's practice, untested [practice].

055. When an athlete does not take to a skill

Most of the trials pooled averages; individual athletes varied, and the meta-analyses opened report heterogeneity or wide intervals for the skills they pooled. An athlete for whom a skill does nothing is common, not a failure of either of you. Steps, in order [practice; no study opened tests them]:

  • Check the basics. Did they practise it, and as many times as the plan said? Do they understand what it is for? Ask them to show you.
  • Check the fit. Is it the right skill for the moment? A swimmer who freezes on the blocks may need a breathing step before the call; one who fades in the last 25 metres may need a self-talk phrase there. Ask the athlete where the problem shows (SP4's profile).
  • Let them change it. Their own words, their own image, their own place in the routine. The self-talk trial had athletes develop their own phrases (Walter et al., 2019) [weak, positive]; choice fits SP3's autonomy support [moderate, for the association, as SP3 grades it].
  • Offer the alternative to control. If trying to calm down or stop a thought makes it worse, offer the acceptance approach in section 3.6 [practice, for offering it to this athlete; the approach itself moderate, positive; inference under 16].
  • Drop it. A skill is offered, not imposed. Keep the athlete's training and selection independent of whether they use it [practice; SP3's controlling behaviours].
  • Stop and refer. If practice brings distress, images or thoughts the athlete cannot set aside, or thoughts about more than sport, stop the exercise for that athlete, hold the first conversation, and use SP4's normal-or-refer table and your SP1 scope card [practice; the course's scope line]. A skill that does not help is not a reason to push harder.

066. Why a skill is never first handed over on competition day

A skill first met on competition day has had none of stages 2 and 3. Four reasons from this course's evidence, none of them a test of the rule itself:

  • It has not been practised. In the opened studies, effects came after weeks of practice; the one-week self-talk programme did not change performance (Walter et al., 2019).
  • It moves attention onto the athlete's own process. SP4's self-focus account says that attending to a skill that normally runs automatically can break it under pressure; a new technique asks for exactly that attention.
  • It can backfire in ways nobody has seen yet. Slow breathing slowed reactions in one laboratory study (Buchanan & Janelle, 2022); a breathing step tried for the first time before a race start might do the same, and you would find out in the race (the unit's inference from a laboratory study outside sport).
  • The quick-fix studies are laboratory studies. The single-session breathing study that its authors suggest as a quick fix tested a colour-word task in sport students after burpees, not a competition (Laborde et al., 2019).

The rule: on competition day an athlete uses only skills they have practised calm and under pressure in training; nothing new is introduced from the night before to the end of the competition [practice; no study opened tests it; consistent with SP4's rule of no new technical instruction on match day]. SP8 builds the day's protocol from the skills this block has taught.

A. Eight-week skills block

Built on the case: Milan's squad (fictional). Academy squad of 20 players aged 16 to 18; Milan, 17, central midfielder. The block uses the first ten minutes after the warm-up in two sessions a week (added: the squad's Tuesday and Thursday sessions), and the squad plays one match a week at the weekend (added). Milan's anxiety profile (SP4) and the referral route to the academy's medical staff stand; the block is for the whole squad and is not a treatment for Milan [practice; SP1 scope line].

Order of skills and why: process goals first (the squad's goals for the first fifteen minutes), self-talk second (one phrase for receiving the ball), external focus third (where the eyes and the pass go), imagery fourth (rehearsing the first fifteen minutes). Breathing and mindfulness are offered in week 8 as options, not taught to the squad.

1 Process goals: explain, practise calm Tue: 3 min explain; each player writes one process goal for the passing drill on a card (e.g. "scan twice before receiving"). Thu: the drill with the goal; players mark the card after. "Today's goal isn't winning the drill. It's one thing you do with the ball. Write it as an action: what your eyes or feet do. After the drill, tick it if you did it." [weak, positive; Williamson et al., abstract read; for 16 to 18 an inference]

I won't ask about the result." [weak, positive; Williamson et al., abstract read; for 16 to 18 an inference]
3 Self-talk: explain, practise calm Tue: 3 min explain; each player writes a short what-to-do phrase for receiving under pressure, said aloud in a rondo. Thu: same phrase, said silently. B. Skill cards

One card per skill. "Tested on" names the samples of the studies this unit opened. Scripts are the unit's examples, not tested wording.

Card 1: Goal setting
What One process goal (how to perform an action) per session or competition; one performance goal (a standard from the athlete's own record) per block; outcome goals (results against others) kept out of training talk.
When Before a drill, a session or a competition; reviewed straight after. A gymnast before a beam routine: "Eyes on the end of the beam on the turn." A rower before a piece: "Hold the catch together for 20 strokes."
Script "Write one thing you'll do, not a result. After, tick it if you did it."
Grade "Performance goal from own record: [weak, positive; one study of swimmers aged about 15]. Process goals for competition: [weak, positive; sport meta-analysis read in abstract, samples not seen; for under-18s an inference]." (use moderate if the §3.1 grade finding is applied)
Tested on One randomised study of 24 male nationally ranked swimmers aged about 15, over eight weeks (Ferchichi et al., 2026).
When not to use it When a demanding goal is lowering the athlete's mood: in the swimmers, mood disturbance rose more in the goal group [weak]. When the goal is selection or a ranking.

Card 2: Self-talk
What A short phrase the athlete chooses, said aloud then silently at set moments: instructional (what to do) or motivational (effort, readiness).
When Before a serve or a free throw, after a mistake, at a set point in a race. A judoka in the grip fight: "Inside first." A cyclist on the last climb: "Push the circle."
Script "What do you want to tell yourself to do there? Two or three words. Say them out loud on this rep."
Grade [weak, mixed]. Instructional for precise tasks: [weak, positive; meta-analysis read in abstract]. Motivational for effort: [practice].
Tested on 117 athletes aged 13 to 23 in individual and team sports, eight weeks of training, coach-rated performance (Walter et al., 2019); 42 male university basketball players, no effect on shooting (Yang et al., 2024).
When not to use it As a phrase about what to avoid ("don't miss"). As a way to argue the athlete out of a worry about their worth: that is beyond a coach's part (section 1).

Card 3: Imagery
What Rehearsing an action or a scene in the mind with as many senses as possible, paired with doing it.
When Before practising the skill physically; in the days before competition; about three times a week. A tennis player before serving practice; a volleyball setter before a rotation drill; a judoka rehearsing a grip sequence.
Script "See it from your own eyes. Feel your feet, hear the ball. Run it once in your head, then do it."
Grade [moderate, positive]. For ages 9 to 18: tennis serve accuracy, low certainty.
Tested on 86 randomised studies of athletes, ages not pooled (Liu et al., 2025); nine trials of tennis players aged 9 to 18 (Deng et al., 2024); 22 trials of children and adolescents aged 9 to 18, athletes and non-athletes (Behrendt et al., 2021).
When not to use it When the images are of failing and the athlete cannot change them, or are intrusive: stop, first conversation, scope card [practice]. In place of physical practice.

Card 4: Breathing and relaxation
What Slow breathing at about six breaths a minute with a longer out-breath; progressive muscle relaxation (tense and release muscle groups in turn).
When Between efforts, after training, early in a pre-competition routine. A swimmer between heats; a basketball player at a time-out; a 400 m runner in the call room.
Script "In for four, out for six, for two minutes. This changes your heart rhythm; it isn't a promise of a faster race. If you feel dizzy, stop."
Grade Breathing for heart-rate variability: [weak, positive; inference under 18]. Breathing for performance: [weak, no benefit shown in competition]. Relaxation: [weak, mixed].
Tested on Breathing: a meta-analysis of 223 studies, not limited to athletes, measuring heart-rate variability (Laborde et al., 2022, abstract and parts of the text read); 107 sport students in a laboratory task (Laborde et al., 2019); 35 kinesiology students, athletic status not reported (Buchanan & Janelle, 2022). Relaxation: 24 collegiate track-and-field athletes (Liang et al., 2021); 59 male basketball players aged 14 to 19, no randomisation reported, abstract read (Battaglini et al., 2022).
When not to use it In the last seconds before a reaction start or a fast decision [weak; one laboratory study in students not described as athletes; inference]. If the athlete feels dizzy or breathless. As a fix for poor sleep or low mood, which go to the scope card [practice].

Card 5: Attentional focus
What Cues that point attention at the movement's effect (target, ball flight, the floor) rather than at body parts.
When In instructions and cues, especially for skills already learned and in competition. A shot-putter: "Push the shot up to the roof." A rower: "Push the boat past the blade." A footballer: "Send it to his front foot."
Script "Pick the spot before it comes. Eyes and mind on the spot, not on your foot."
Grade [contested; rule 1: a bias-corrected re-analysis, read in abstract, finds the effect small to nil; inference under 18]. In athlete samples, one small meta-analysis found no significant difference.
Tested on 566 adults aged 18 to 65 learning sport skills, mostly novices (Favre-Bulle et al., 2024); 497 physically fit participants, one study of children (Zang et al., 2025); 49 athletes in four crossover studies (Reinebo et al., 2024).
When not to use it The parts read report no harms. Early learning of a new technique may still need some body cues in quiet practice, away from competition [practice].

Card 6: Mindfulness and acceptance
What Noticing thoughts, feelings and sensations, letting them be there, and returning attention to the task: an alternative to trying to control them.
When For athletes whose attempts to calm down or stop thoughts have not worked; as a short daily practice over weeks. A golfer between shots; a sprinter in the call room; a judoka between contests.
Script "Notice the nerves. Name them: 'there's the worry'. You don't have to make them go. Now, back to the next action."
Grade [moderate, positive; fair-to-low-quality evidence; samples over 15, so for 10 to 15 inference].
Tested on Nine controlled trials, 290 athletes over 15 in 13 sports (Bühlmayer et al., 2017, abstract read); 66 studies, 3,908 athletes, none at low risk of bias (Noetel et al., 2019, abstract read).
When not to use it When noticing brings up distress the athlete cannot set aside, or memories of harm: stop, first conversation, scope card; this is not therapy [practice; the course's scope line]. As a single talk in place of weeks of practice.

Aim: teach a small set of psychological skills inside normal training, knowing how strong the evidence is for each
Takes: SP1, SP2, SP4.
Gives: the skills SP6 puts under pressure, SP8 uses on the day and SP10 places in the season; the skill cards DD-SP3 extends.

Unit SP5, The cognitive-behavioural toolkit. Cover: the cognitive-behavioural model in plain words (situation, thought, feeling, body, behaviour) and the line between a coach teaching a skill and a clinician treating a problem; what the meta-analyses of psychological skills training show overall, and on whom; then each skill with what it is, its evidence, its grade and how it is taught to each age band: goal setting (outcome, performance and process goals, and what the sport meta-analysis found about each kind), self-talk (instructional and motivational, matched to the task), imagery, breathing and relaxation (saying what the breathing studies measured), attentional focus (external against internal), and mindfulness and acceptance approaches as the alternative to control, with what the reviews say about the quality of their evidence; how a skill is taught (explain, practise calm, practise under pressure, use in competition) and how long that takes; what to do when an athlete does not take to a skill; why a skill is never first handed over on competition day. Deliverable: an eight-week skills block that fits inside normal sessions (ten minutes, one new skill a fortnight, the words the coach says), built in full for Milan's squad and for Sara as an individual athlete; and one card per skill (what, when, script, grade, sample it was tested on, when not to use it), then the templates.
