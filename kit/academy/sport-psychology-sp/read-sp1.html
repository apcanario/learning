011. What sport psychology is, and who may call themselves what

Sport psychology studies how mental processes and behaviour relate to taking part and performing in sport, and applies what it finds. A guidance document hosted by the Association for Applied Sport Psychology (AASP), the US-based professional body, describes two: a sport-science route, whose graduates teach mental skills but usually do not become licensed psychologists, and a psychology route, whose graduates most often become licensed psychologists and can work with clinical problems such as substance misuse and eating disorders (AASP, How to Choose a Sport Psychology Consultant, undated, read 17 Nov 2026). The same document tells readers to check that a psychologist is licensed to practise where they work, and notes that licensing rules differ from one US state to the next.

Which titles are protected, and who may assess or treat a mental health problem, are set by each country's law and regulators; this course states none of them as yours. Put them as questions:

  • In your country, is "psychologist", "sport psychologist" or "sport and exercise psychologist" a protected title, and who keeps the register? Ask your national psychology regulator or the professional association of psychologists.
  • Is there a recognised certification for mental-skills practitioners who are not psychologists (for example, the national or international sport psychology society), and what does it allow them to do? Ask that society.
  • Who may assess and treat a mental health problem in a minor, and with whose consent? Ask your national health regulator, and your federation's medical or safeguarding officer.

022. Your part as a coach, and where it ends

This course gives the coach five jobs: shape the training climate, teach psychological skills, notice, respond, and refer. It gives the coach no part in assessment, diagnosis or treatment. That is the course's scope line, built from what the sources opened for this unit assign to coaches. (The Delphi study in the course's seed list on the coach's role in athlete mental health could not be opened, so it is not used.)

What the opened sources say:

  • Recognise and refer belong to the people around the athlete; assessment belongs to licensed professionals. In 2021 the International Olympic Committee (IOC) published two tools, built from a literature review, a survey of 360 athletes and a modified Delphi process (consensus). The assessment tool, SMHAT-1, is for sports medicine physicians and other licensed or registered health professionals, for elite athletes aged 16 and over. The recognition tool, SMHRT-1, was "developed for athletes and their entourage", coaches among them. The stated aim is earlier recognition and timely referral (Gouttebarge et al., 2021, abstract read). Applying this division to a club squad of 11- to 17-year-olds is this unit's inference.

  • Teaching skills is within your part. A meta-analysis of randomised controlled trials in athletes found some evidence that the effects of psychological interventions on performance were greatest when coaches delivered them (Brown & Fletcher, 2017, abstract read). The abstract does not give the athletes' ages.

  • The climate you create is associated with how athletes feel. A cross-sectional survey of 252 athletes in six Chinese provinces, aged 10 to over 25, found a controlling coaching style negatively correlated with athletes' basic psychological need satisfaction, and through that with constrained commitment (taking part because one feels one has to) and fear of failure (Hu et al., 2023). One survey, a correlation: it cannot show cause.

So the scope line, as the course uses it [practice]: you shape the climate, teach skills inside training, notice change against an athlete's own baseline, hold a first conversation, and refer. You do not run screening questionnaires, name a condition, or start a treatment. When what you notice touches mental health, eating, injury or abuse, your part ends at the referral, and the person your route names takes over: a doctor, a licensed or registered mental health professional, or, for a safeguarding concern, the person your club or federation names. SP9 builds the route; DD-SP1 and DD-SP2 deal with abuse and with weight.

033. The kinds of evidence, and what each can show

Five kinds of evidence turn up in this field. Each answers a different question.

  • Consensus and position statements. Experts convened by a body such as the IOC agree on what evidence and practice support. A consensus shows agreement; it does not test anything.
  • Meta-analyses and systematic reviews. A systematic review finds every study on a question by a stated method; a meta-analysis pools their results into one estimate and shows how much they disagree. It is only as good as the studies it pools: Brown & Fletcher pooled only randomised controlled trials, while another review of psychological interventions in athletes found that its pooled effects were no longer significant once it removed non-randomised trials and self-reported outcomes (Reinebo et al., 2024).
  • Controlled studies. One group gets the technique, a comparison group does not, and, in a randomised controlled trial, chance decides who is in which group. This is the design that can show a technique caused a change.
  • Correlational studies. These measure two things in the same people and see whether they go together. They can show an association. They cannot show which causes which, or rule out a third factor. The authors of a systematic review of grit and physical activity, most of whose studies were cross-sectional, say exactly this about their own evidence (Martin et al., 2025).
  • Case reports. One athlete or one team, described. They show that something is possible; they cannot show how often it happens or whether a technique works for anyone else.

044. Reading a study in plain words

Three things decide most of what a study can tell you: its effect size, its sample and its control group.

Effect size. A standardised effect size such as Cohen's d (Hedges' g is a close variant) expresses the difference between two groups in units of how much people vary within a group. Lakens' primer describes d as the standardised mean difference and reports the common labels, small (d = 0.2), medium (0.5) and large (0.8), with the warning that "these values are arbitrary and should not be interpreted rigidly" (Lakens, 2013). The same primer offers a plainer translation, the common-language effect size: the probability that a person picked at random from one group scores higher than a person picked at random from the other. In its worked example, d = 1.13 corresponds to a 79% chance. Ask also how wide the uncertainty is: a 95% confidence interval that runs from close to zero to very large, as several in section 6 do, means the study cannot tell a trivial effect from a big one.

Sample. Who was studied, how many, and how they were chosen. A finding in 20 university athletes describes university athletes; whether it holds for a 13-year-old is a separate question.

Control group. Without one, improvement could come from practice, from growing up, from the season, or from the athlete expecting to improve. A pre-to-post study with no comparison group shows that people changed; it does not show that the technique changed them. The meta-analysis of pre-performance routines in section 6 reports its pre-to-post results and its controlled results separately, and they differ (Rupprecht et al., 2024).

055. Why most studies are small and on students or adults, and what that means for your 13-year-old

The studies are small. An editorial in the Journal of Sports Sciences reported that in a random sample of 120 papers submitted to the journal, the median sample size was 19, and only 12 of the 120 reported a power calculation made before the study (Abt et al., 2020, editorial). The sample covers sport and exercise science as a whole. The editorial explains that small samples miss real effects more often, overestimate the effects they do find, and replicate less often. Inside sport psychology, the pre-performance routine meta-analysis shows the pattern: of its 61 intervention samples, 16 had fewer than 10 participants, 32 had 10 to 19, and 13 had more than 20 (Rupprecht et al., 2024).

The samples are mostly students and adults. In a 2024 systematic review of psychological interventions to improve athletic performance, university athletes made up 54 of the 111 included studies (48.65%), and the mean ages reported in the meta-analysed studies ran from about 19 to 35 (Reinebo et al., 2024). Some lines of evidence the course relies on come from outside sport altogether: from school pupils, from undergraduates in laboratories, from animals.

So when a study of 20-year-old university athletes is the best evidence for a technique, applying it to Dani, 14, is an inference, and this course labels it so. Where a study did sample younger athletes (the tennis imagery meta-analysis in section 8 sampled players aged 9 to 18), the course says so.

066. What the reviews say about psychology and performance

The 2022 systematic review of meta-analyses on sport psychology and performance in the course's seed list could not be opened, so this section reports none of its figures and says nothing about how far psychological variables (traits and states) relate to performance. It covers interventions, from three meta-analyses that were opened.

  • Psychological interventions in general. Brown & Fletcher pooled randomised controlled trials in athletes against no treatment or placebo. At post-test: 35 studies, 997 participants, Hedges' g = 0.57 (95% CI 0.22 to 0.92). At follow-up one to four weeks later: 8 studies, 189 participants, g = 1.16 (95% CI 0.25 to 2.08). The authors call the effect moderate and say it may last at least a month (abstract read; ages not given) (Brown & Fletcher, 2017).
  • Three families of technique. Reinebo et al. reviewed 111 studies and pooled 25 of them (37 effects) into five meta-analyses. In parallel-group studies, psychological skills training gave g = 0.83 (95% CI 0.21 to 1.45), mindfulness- and acceptance-based approaches g = 0.67 (0.01 to 1.32), and imagery g = 0.75 (0.14 to 1.36). The authors call these effects not stable: when they removed non-randomised trials and subjective outcomes, none of the pooled estimates stayed significant. They asked for more randomised trials of every type (Reinebo et al., 2024). Samples: mostly university athletes, adults.
  • Pre-performance routines. Rupprecht et al. pooled 112 effect sizes from 61 intervention samples aged 11 to 40. Pre-to-post: standardised mean change 0.31 (95% CI 0.18 to 0.44). Controlled comparisons under low pressure: g = 0.64 (0.45 to 0.83); under pressure: g = 0.70 (0.24 to 1.16), from few effect sizes. Age, gender and skill level did not moderate the effect. The pre-to-post data showed a small-study effect; a p-uniform analysis found no evidence that publication bias explained the results (Rupprecht et al., 2024).

Read together: positive pooled estimates, wide intervals, small studies, mostly adult samples, and effects that fell below significance when one review kept only the strongest designs. Mental-skills interventions improve performance [moderate, positive]; later units grade each skill on its own.

077. The replication record: four cautionary cases

Four well-known findings that are often told to coaches. Three are stated here from a study that tested them, with what that study could and could not show; for the fourth, ego depletion, no such study could be opened.

Power posing. The claim: holding an expansive power posture for two minutes changes hormones and makes you more confident and bolder. The original replication in the course list could not be opened for this unit. A later randomised test assigned 82 men aged 17 to 32 (81 analysed, recruited through a participant pool and student job sites) to hold an expansive or a constrictive posture for two minutes, three times in one session; testosterone, cortisol and progesterone changed over the session, but not differently by posture. The study measured no behaviour and no feelings of power (Metzler & Grèzes, 2019). Sample: young adult men; applying this to younger athletes is the unit's inference. No sport performance outcome was tested.

Ego depletion. The claim: self-control draws on a limited resource that runs down with use. The two large multilab replications (Hagger et al., 2016; Vohs et al., 2021) could not be opened for this unit, and no other source of an accepted type on their result was opened, so this unit reports neither their effect sizes nor their conclusions. For the claim that they failed to find the effect: no peer-reviewed source found that could be opened. The case is not graded here until a replication is opened.

Grit. The claim: grit, passion and perseverance for long-term goals, is what separates achievers. The meta-analysis in the course list that tested grit's distinctness could not be opened for this unit. A 2025 systematic review of grit, resilience and physical activity (33 studies, 37,370 adults aged 18 and over, 5,113 of them athletes, among them elite wrestlers, ultra-runners and wheelchair rugby players) found grit associated with taking part in physical activity, and in some studies with better competitive performance, while others found no association or mixed results; most of its studies were cross-sectional, so the authors make no causal claim. It also states that grit's perseverance-of-effort component is highly correlated with conscientiousness, citing earlier work (Martin et al., 2025). The review admitted adults only, though one included study's sample may have contained 17-year-olds.

The size of growth-mindset effects. The claim: teaching a growth mindset, the belief that ability can be developed, transforms achievement. The two meta-analyses in the course list could not be opened. A national randomised experiment with a pre-registered analysis plan gave 12,490 ninth-grade students (aged about 14 to 15) in 65 US public schools two online sessions of about 25 minutes. Among lower-achieving students (n = 6,320), core grade point average rose by 0.10 grade points (95% CI 0.04 to 0.16), a standardised effect of 0.11, and the effect was larger in schools whose peer norms supported taking on challenges (Yeager et al., 2019). The effect was real, small and conditional, on school grades, in adolescents. Nothing in it measured sport.

A famous finding from a small sample is a hypothesis until someone runs it again, larger, with the analysis fixed in advance.

088. The popular claims, set against their evidence

Each claim is stated as it circulates, then set against a source opened for this unit or against "no peer-reviewed source found".

"The brain finishes developing at 25." The largest study opened for this unit pooled 123,984 MRI scans from 101,457 people aged from before birth to 100 years, drawn from more than 100 earlier studies, to chart brain measures by age; it describes and does not test a cause. Different measures peak at different ages: cortical thickness at 1.7 years, total grey matter volume at 5.9, subcortical grey matter at 14.4, white matter volume at 28.7 (Bethlehem et al., 2022). There is no single finishing age in these data, and the brain keeps changing across adult life. The study measures volumes, not abilities or judgement. For coaching: do not treat 25, or any age, as the point at which an athlete becomes able to decide [practice]; SP2 deals with age and maturation.

"A coach can read what an athlete feels from the face." A review in Psychological Science in the Public Interest, a journal of the Association for Psychological Science, examined the published studies on whether facial movements reliably and specifically signal particular emotions across people, situations and cultures, and concluded that they do not (a review of research outside sport); the same publisher's summary notes an accompanying article that argues for a richer model of expression across face, voice and body (Barrett et al., 2019, publisher's summary page read). The face carries information; it is not a reliable readout of what an athlete feels. Ask, and compare with the athlete's own baseline [practice]. The evidence against reading faces grades as weak under this course's rules, because it comes from outside sport; its direction is against the claim. SP7 builds this.

"10,000 hours, or early specialisation, makes a champion." A meta-analysis of deliberate practice and sport performance found that deliberate practice accounted for 18% of the variance in sports performance overall and 1% among elite-level performers, and that athletes who reached a high level of skill had not begun their sport earlier in childhood than lower-skill athletes; these are correlations (Macnamara et al., 2016, abstract read). It does not test a 10,000-hour threshold; for that figure as a rule: no peer-reviewed source found. On early specialisation, a 2024 meta-analysis (51 effect sizes, 6,233 athletes, 82% male, studies from 2009 to 2022) found that entering a talent promotion programme earlier was associated with higher junior performance and with lower senior performance: "effects on short-term junior performance versus long-term senior performance are opposite" (Güllich & Barth, 2024, abstract and parts of the full text read). The pattern held across individual and team sports and across performance levels. It is a correlation: it cannot show that early entry caused the later shortfall. Do not read early entry to a talent programme as a forecast of senior success [strong, for the association].

"A power pose before competing raises confidence and performance"; "Willpower is a tank that runs dry"; "Grit is what separates winners"; "A growth mindset transforms performance." Set against their evidence in section 7. Power pose: posture did not change hormones in the one randomised test opened, which was not described as a replication (Metzler & Grèzes, 2019); for sport performance, no peer-reviewed source found [weak, no effect shown]. Willpower: the two multilab replications could not be opened for this unit; for their result, no peer-reviewed source found that could be opened, and the claim is not graded here. Grit: associated with physical activity in adults, mostly cross-sectionally (Martin et al., 2025); the associations with competitive performance in that review are mixed and mostly cross-sectional [weak, positive, for an association]. Growth mindset: a small, conditional effect on school grades (Yeager et al., 2019); for sport performance, no peer-reviewed source found [weak, positive and small, outside sport].

"Learning styles; left-brained and right-brained athletes; we use 10% of the brain." A survey of 242 primary and secondary school teachers in the UK and the Netherlands found that 93% (UK) and 96% (Netherlands) agreed that people learn better when taught in their preferred learning style, 91% and 86% that left-brain or right-brain dominance explains differences between learners, and 48% and 46% that we use only 10% of the brain; the authors class all three as neuromyths. Teachers with more general knowledge about the brain believed more of the myths (Dekker et al., 2012). The sample was teachers; the study measures belief and does not test learning styles. Do not sort athletes by learning style or by brain side [practice; no supporting evidence opened].

"Telling yourself not to think about it works." A systematic review of ironic effects in motor tasks under pressure found 24 studies published before January 2022 (1,152 participants across 17 studies), and most of their findings supported Wegner's account: trying not to do something under pressure can make that error more likely (Bartura et al., 2024). Half the studies used novices, a third used highly trained athletes competing nationally and one used elite athletes; mean age was about 22, and one study included under-18s. The review pools nothing, and whether three or more of the athlete studies were controlled, which rule 4 needs for moderate, was not checked here, so this unit grades it by rule 5. Wegner's own papers could not be opened for this unit. Phrase instructions as what to do, not what to avoid [weak, positive; DD-SP3 regrades it from the primary studies].

"Harsh coaching builds toughness." No peer-reviewed source found that shows it does. In the one study opened (section 2), athletes who reported a more controlling coach reported lower need satisfaction, associated in turn with fear of failure (cross-sectional; Hu et al., 2023) [weak, against the claim]. The reviews on controlling coaching in the course list could not be opened; SP3 and DD-SP1 must open them.

"The amygdala hijack; the lizard brain." For the "lizard brain", the idea that a reptilian core, an emotional limbic layer and a rational cortex work fairly independently: a 2022 review argues that this triune model does not explain how the brain works in everyday life, that emotion and cognition are interdependent, and that purely emotional or purely cognitive circuits do not exist (Steffen et al., 2022, abstract read). For the "amygdala hijack" as a mechanism: no peer-reviewed source found.

"Dopamine explains motivation, as told in videos." The videos usually present dopamine as the pleasure chemical. A review by Berridge reports that, in animal studies, dopamine mediated wanting, the pull towards a reward, and not liking, the pleasure of it, and that raising dopamine does not reliably raise people's ratings of pleasure: "Dopamine then failed to live up to its hedonic reputation" (Berridge, 2018). Most of that evidence comes from rats, some from people; none from sport.

"The brain cannot tell a vividly imagined action from a real one." No peer-reviewed source found for the claim as stated. An EEG study of 21 adults found brain activity that overlapped when walking and when imagining walking, and also differed: imagery needed additional cortical resources (Mustile et al., 2024, abstract and methods read). On performance, imagery interventions gave g = 0.75 (95% CI 0.14 to 1.36) in parallel-group studies of mostly adult athletes, an effect that did not stay significant when restricted to randomised trials and objective outcomes (Reinebo et al., 2024); in tennis players aged 9 to 18, imagery training improved serve accuracy, g = 0.99 (95% CI 0.68 to 1.30), with low certainty (Deng et al., 2024). Imagery has evidence behind it as a skill [moderate, positive]; in the study opened, imagining and doing were not the same brain activity.

"Mental toughness is a trait you install." A systematic review of mental toughness and individual differences (the abstract does not give the number of studies or their populations) reports that about 50% of the variation in mental toughness can be accounted for by genetic factors, and associations with positive traits, coping and outcomes in education and mental health; it mentions possible interventions and reports no tests of them in its abstract (Lin et al., 2017, abstract read). For a programme that installs it: no peer-reviewed source found.

"Neuro-linguistic programming improves performance." For sport performance: no peer-reviewed source found. The systematic review found for neuro-linguistic programming in health (10 experimental studies, five of them randomised trials with samples of 22 to 106) concluded: "There is little evidence that NLP interventions improve health-related outcomes", adding that this reflects few and weak studies (Sturt et al., 2012, abstract read) [weak, no benefit shown].

"Brain-training apps transfer to sport." A systematic review of 43 studies of seven commercial cognitive training devices found one study in athletes (soccer players: passing accuracy improved, dribbling and shooting did not); it found small samples and little independent replication, and concluded there is "little direct evidence that the use of CCT devices can transfer" to sport performance (Harris et al., 2018) [weak, no benefit shown].

099. The five evidence grades

Every later unit grades every technique and recommendation with one of these five. The definitions are the course's own:

  • strong (consistent meta-analytic evidence in athletes)
  • moderate (a meta-analysis of small or mixed studies, or several controlled studies in athletes)
  • weak (single studies, or studies outside sport)
  • contested (failed replications, or reviews that disagree)
  • practice (professional consensus or coaching practice with no controlled test)

So that two readers give the same grade, the course applies them by these rules, in this order. Stop at the first rule that fits.

  1. Contested if a pre-registered or multi-site replication failed to find an effect the original reported, or if two reviews or meta-analyses reach opposite conclusions on whether the effect exists.
  2. Practice if no study of any design (controlled, correlational or meta-analytic) tests the claim, and it rests on professional consensus or coaching practice. A claim tested only by correlational studies has no controlled test but is not practice: it goes on to rules 3 to 5.
  3. Strong if at least one meta-analysis pooling studies of athletes finds an effect whose confidence interval excludes zero, the effect holds in the meta-analysis's own sensitivity or subgroup analyses, its authors rate the primary studies as adequate or better, and no meta-analysis or review in athletes found for the unit disagrees.
  4. Moderate if there is a meta-analysis in athletes that fails a condition of rule 3 (small studies, wide or unstable estimates, heterogeneity its authors flag, low certainty), or three or more controlled studies in athletes that agree. "Several" in the definition means three or more in this course.
  5. Weak in every other case: one or two controlled studies in athletes, or any amount of evidence from outside sport (non-athletes, school pupils, laboratories, animals).

Three further rules. The direction goes beside the grade (weak, no benefit shown; moderate, positive). A grade from adults, students or non-athletes applied to an athlete under 18 carries the label inference. A correlational meta-analysis can earn strong for an association, never for a cause.

1010. Five questions to ask of any claim

  1. Where is the study? The paper behind the claim, not the person repeating it. With no paper, the best grade is practice.
  2. Who was studied, and how many? Athletes or not; children, adolescents, students or adults; tens or thousands. If your athletes are younger than the sample, using it is an inference.
  3. Compared with what? A control group, assigned by chance? Without one, the study shows change, not cause; two things measured together are a correlation.
  4. How big, and how sure? What effect size, in the study's own terms, and how wide is its confidence interval? A large effect from a small sample is a hypothesis.
  5. Has it held up? Has anyone run it again, larger, with the analysis fixed in advance, or pooled it with other studies?

The answers feed the rules of section 9 and give a grade with its direction.

B. Claim-check sheet

Worked on three claims from the course's list.

Claim 1: "A power pose before competing raises confidence and performance." (Milan's coach has been told this at a coaching clinic.)

Question Answer
1. Where is the study? The test opened: a randomised study (Metzler & Grèzes, 2019). The original and the first replication were not opened.
2. Who was studied, and how many? 82 men aged 17 to 32 (81 analysed), recruited through a participant pool and student job sites; not athletes. Milan is 17: inference.
3. Compared with what? Expansive against constrictive posture, assigned at random. Controlled.
4. How big, and how sure? No difference by posture in testosterone, cortisol or progesterone. Confidence and performance were not measured.
5. Has it held up? The hormone part did not hold in this test. No sport performance study found.
Grade Weak, no effect shown (rule 5: one controlled study, outside sport, not a replication). For sport performance: no peer-reviewed source found.
What I do I do not teach it as a technique [weak, no effect shown].

Claim 2: "The brain finishes developing at 25." (Sara has read this and told her coach she "isn't finished yet".)

Question Answer
1. Where is the study? None found that states it. The lifespan study opened: Bethlehem et al., 2022.

strong consistent meta-analytic evidence in athletes Rule 3: a meta-analysis of athletes whose effect excludes zero, holds in its sensitivity or subgroup analyses, with primary studies rated adequate or better, and no disagreeing review found. "Early entry to a talent programme does not forecast senior success." A meta-analysis of 51 effects in 6,233 athletes found earlier entry associated with higher junior and lower senior performance, holding across individual and team sports and levels, with high-quality primary studies (Güllich & Barth, 2024). Strong for the association; it says nothing about cause.
moderate a meta-analysis of small or mixed studies, or several controlled studies in athletes Rule 4: a meta-analysis in athletes that fails a condition of rule 3, or three or more agreeing controlled studies in athletes. "A pre-performance routine improves performance." Pooled controlled effects of g = 0.64 and, under pressure, 0.70 (95% CI 0.24 to 1.16), but most samples had fewer than 20 participants and the pre-to-post data showed a small-study effect (Rupprecht et al., 2024). Moderate, positive; ages 11 to 40.
weak single studies, or studies outside sport Rule 5: every other case, such as one or two studies in athletes, or any evidence from outside sport. "A growth-mindset lesson improves performance." Unit SP1, What a coach can do, and how to tell evidence from noise. Cover: what sport psychology is and the professions in it (who may call themselves what, and who may treat, differs by country: put it as a question for the reader, with the kind of body to consult); the coach's part as the literature defines it (shaping the climate, teaching psychological skills, noticing, responding, referring) and where it ends (assessment, diagnosis, treatment); the kinds of evidence in this field (consensus statements, meta-analyses, controlled studies, correlational studies, case reports) and what each can show; how to read an effect size, a sample and a control group, in plain words; why most studies are small and on students or adults, and what that means for a coach of a 13-year-old; what the reviews of meta-analyses say about how far psychological variables and interventions relate to performance, with their limits; the replication record as cautionary cases, each from its replication study or meta-analysis (power posing, ego depletion, grit, the size of growth-mindset effects); the popular claims on the course's list, each stated as it circulates and then set against its evidence, saying 'no peer-reviewed source found' where none was; the course's five evidence grades, defined so that two readers would give the same grade; five questions to ask of any claim met in a video, a clinic or a book. Deliverable: a scope-of-practice card (what I do, what I refer, to whom; blanks for the reader's country, federation and club); a claim-check sheet of five questions, worked in full on three claims from the list; and the grade key every later unit uses, with one worked example per grade.
