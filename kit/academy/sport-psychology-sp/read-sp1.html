011. What sport psychology is, and who may call themselves what

In this course, sport psychology means the study of how mental processes and behaviour relate to taking part and performing in sport, and the use of what it finds (the course's working description; no defining source was opened). A guidance document hosted by the Association for Applied Sport Psychology (AASP), the US-based professional body, describes two routes into the work: a sport-science route, whose graduates teach mental skills but usually do not become licensed psychologists, and a psychology route, whose graduates most often become licensed psychologists and can work with clinical problems such as substance misuse and eating disorders (AASP, How to Choose a Sport Psychology Consultant, undated, read 17 Nov 2026). The same document tells readers to check that a psychologist is licensed to practise where they work, and notes that licensing rules differ from one US state to the next.

Which titles are protected, and who may assess or treat a mental health problem, are set by each country's law and regulators; this course states none of them as yours. Put them as questions:

  • In your country, is "psychologist", "sport psychologist" or "sport and exercise psychologist" a protected title, and who keeps the register? Ask your national psychology regulator or the professional association of psychologists.
  • Is there a recognised certification for mental-skills practitioners who are not psychologists (for example, the national or international sport psychology society), and what does it allow them to do? Ask that society.
  • Who may assess and treat a mental health problem in a minor, and with whose consent? Ask your national health regulator, and your federation's medical or safeguarding officer.

022. Your part as a coach, and where it ends

This course gives the coach five jobs: shape the training climate, teach psychological skills, notice, respond, and refer. It gives the coach no part in assessment, diagnosis or treatment. That is the course's scope line, built from what the sources opened for this unit assign to coaches. (The Delphi study in the course's seed list on the coach's role in athlete mental health could not be opened, so it is not used.)

What the opened sources say:

  • Recognise and refer belong to the people around the athlete; assessment belongs to licensed professionals. In 2021 the International Olympic Committee (IOC) published two tools, built from narrative and systematic reviews, a survey of 360 people (active and former elite athletes, coaches in elite sport and representatives of sport governing bodies; the paper does not say how many were athletes) and a modified Delphi consensus process. The assessment tool, SMHAT-1, is for sports medicine physicians and other licensed or registered health professionals, for elite athletes aged 16 and over. The recognition tool, SMHRT-1, was "developed for athletes and their entourage", coaches among them. The stated aim is earlier recognition and timely referral (Gouttebarge et al., 2021, full text read). Applying this division to a club squad of 11- to 17-year-olds is this unit's inference.

  • Teaching skills is within your part. A meta-analysis of randomised controlled trials in athletes found some evidence that the effects of psychological interventions on performance were greatest when coaches delivered them (Brown & Fletcher, 2017, abstract read). The abstract does not give the athletes' ages.

  • The climate you create is associated with how athletes feel. A cross-sectional survey of 252 athletes in six Chinese provinces, aged 10 to 25 and over, found a controlling coaching style negatively correlated with athletes' basic psychological need satisfaction, and through that with constrained commitment (taking part because one feels one has to) and fear of failure (Hu et al., 2023). One survey, a correlation: it cannot show cause.

So the scope line, as the course uses it [practice]: you shape the climate, teach skills inside training, notice change against an athlete's own baseline, hold a first conversation, and refer. You do not run screening questionnaires, name a condition, or start a treatment. When what you notice touches mental health, eating, injury or abuse, your part ends at the referral, and the person your route names takes over: a doctor, a licensed or registered mental health professional, or, for a safeguarding concern, the person your club or federation names. SP9 builds the route; DD-SP1 and DD-SP2 deal with abuse and with weight.

033. The kinds of evidence, and what each can show

Five kinds of evidence turn up in this field. Each answers a different question.

  • Consensus and position statements. Experts convened by a body such as the IOC agree on what evidence and practice support. A consensus shows agreement; it does not test anything.
  • Meta-analyses and systematic reviews. A systematic review finds every study on a question by a stated method; a meta-analysis pools their results into one estimate and shows how much they disagree. It is only as good as the studies it pools: Brown & Fletcher pooled only randomised controlled trials, while another review of psychological interventions in athletes found that its pooled effects were no longer significant once it removed non-randomised trials and subjective performance outcomes (Reinebo et al., 2024).
  • Controlled studies. One group gets the technique, a comparison group does not, and, in a randomised controlled trial, chance decides who is in which group. This is the design that can show a technique caused a change.
  • Correlational studies. These measure two things in the same people and see whether they go together. They can show an association. They cannot show which causes which, or rule out a third factor. The authors of a systematic review of grit and physical activity, most of whose studies were cross-sectional, say that their evidence cannot settle whether the relationship is causal (Martin et al., 2025).
  • Case reports. One athlete or one team, described. They show that something is possible; they cannot show how often it happens or whether a technique works for anyone else.

044. Reading a study in plain words

Three things decide most of what a study can tell you: its effect size, its sample and its control group.

Effect size. A standardised effect size such as Cohen's d (Hedges' g is a close variant) expresses the difference between two groups in units of how much people vary within a group. Lakens' primer describes d as the standardised mean difference and reports the common labels, small (d = 0.2), medium (0.5) and large (0.8), with the warning that "these values are arbitrary and should not be interpreted rigidly" (Lakens, 2013). The same primer offers a plainer translation, the common-language effect size: the probability that a person picked at random from one group scores higher than a person picked at random from the other. In its worked example, d = 1.13 corresponds to a 79% chance. Ask also how wide the uncertainty is: a 95% confidence interval that runs from close to zero to very large, as several in section 6 do, means the study cannot tell a trivial effect from a big one.

Sample. Who was studied, how many, and how they were chosen. A finding in 20 university athletes describes university athletes; whether it holds for a 13-year-old is a separate question.

Control group. Without one, improvement could come from practice, from growing up, from the season, or from the athlete expecting to improve. A pre-to-post study with no comparison group shows that people changed; it does not show that the technique changed them. The meta-analysis of pre-performance routines in section 6 reports its pre-to-post results and its controlled results separately, and they differ (Rupprecht et al., 2024).

055. Why most studies are small and on students or adults, and what that means for your 13-year-old

The studies are small. Inside sport psychology, the pre-performance routine meta-analysis shows the pattern: of its 61 intervention samples, 16 had fewer than 10 participants, 32 had 10 to 19, and 13 had more than 20 (Rupprecht et al., 2024).

The samples are mostly students and adults. In a 2024 systematic review of psychological interventions to improve athletic performance, university athletes made up 54 of the 111 included studies (48.65%), and the mean ages reported in the meta-analysed studies ran from about 19 to 35 (Reinebo et al., 2024). Some lines of evidence the course relies on come from outside sport altogether: from school pupils, from undergraduates in laboratories, from animals.

So when a study of 20-year-old university athletes is the best evidence for a technique, applying it to Dani, 14, is an inference, and this course labels it so. Where a study did sample younger athletes (the tennis imagery meta-analysis in section 8 sampled players aged 9 to 18), the course says so.

066. What the reviews say about psychology and performance

Psychological variables. A 2022 systematic review of 30 meta-analyses published from 1983 to 2021, covering 16 sport psychology constructs, found that variables expected to help performance (among them cohesion, confidence and mindfulness) had a moderate positive effect, d = 0.51, and variables expected to harm it (among them cognitive anxiety, depression and an ego climate) a small negative one, d = −0.21. Neither the quality of the meta-analyses nor whether their studies were interventions or correlational significantly moderated these effects. Of the 30 meta-analyses, 14 pooled correlational studies, 13 intervention studies and three a mix or mostly cross-sectional studies; where the pooled studies are correlational, the effect is a correlation. Participants ranged from children and adolescents to college-aged volunteers and elite athletes, and the review gives no ages for each meta-analysis (Lochbaum et al., 2022). The rest of this section covers interventions, from three meta-analyses that were opened.

  • Psychological interventions in general. Brown & Fletcher pooled randomised controlled trials of psychological and psychosocial interventions in athletes against no treatment or placebo. At post-test: 35 studies, 997 participants, Hedges' g = 0.57 (95% CI 0.22 to 0.92). At follow-up one to four weeks later: 8 studies, 189 participants, g = 1.16 (95% CI 0.25 to 2.08). The authors call the effect moderate and say it may last at least a month (abstract read; ages not given) (Brown & Fletcher, 2017).
  • Three families of technique. Reinebo et al. reviewed 111 studies and pooled 25 of them (37 effects) into five meta-analyses. In parallel-group studies, psychological skills training gave g = 0.83 (95% CI 0.21 to 1.45), mindfulness- and acceptance-based approaches g = 0.67 (0.01 to 1.32), and imagery g = 0.75 (0.14 to 1.36). The authors call these effects not stable: when they removed non-randomised trials and subjective outcomes, none of the pooled estimates stayed significant. They asked for more randomised trials of every type (Reinebo et al., 2024). Samples: mostly university athletes, adults; high school athletes were excluded by design ("High school athletes were considered equal to junior level participants and were therefore excluded").
  • Pre-performance routines. Rupprecht et al. pooled 112 effect sizes from 61 intervention samples aged 11 to 40. Pre-to-post: standardised mean change 0.31 (95% CI 0.18 to 0.44). Controlled comparisons under low pressure: g = 0.64 (0.45 to 0.83); under pressure: g = 0.70 (0.24 to 1.16), from 19 of the 112 effect sizes. Age, gender and skill level did not moderate the effect. The pre-to-post data showed a small-study effect; a p-uniform analysis found no evidence that publication bias explained the results (Rupprecht et al., 2024).

Read together: positive pooled estimates, wide intervals, small studies, mostly adult samples, and effects that fell below significance when one review kept only the strongest designs. Mental-skills interventions improve performance [moderate, positive; from samples mostly aged 18 and over, so for athletes under 18 an inference]; later units grade each skill on its own.

077. The replication record: four cautionary cases

Four well-known findings that are often told to coaches. Each is stated here from a study that tested it, with what that study could and could not show.

Power posing. The claim: holding an expansive power posture for two minutes changes hormones and makes you more confident and bolder. The replication in the course list randomly assigned 200 participants (102 men, 98 women) to high- or low-power poses, holding each of two poses for 3 minutes where the original used 1 minute; it found no effect of posing on testosterone, cortisol or financial risk-taking, and higher self-reported feelings of power after the high-power poses (Ranehill et al., 2015, read on a copy of the published PDF). The paper does not give the participants' ages or say who they were; no sport outcome was measured. A later randomised test assigned 82 men aged 17 to 32 (81 analysed, recruited through a participant pool and student job sites) to hold an expansive or a constrictive posture for two minutes, three times in one session; testosterone, cortisol and progesterone changed over the session, but not differently by posture. The study measured no behaviour and no feelings of power (Metzler & Grèzes, 2019). Sample: young adult men; applying this to younger athletes is the unit's inference. No sport performance outcome was tested.

Ego depletion. The claim: self-control draws on a limited resource that runs down with use. A preregistered test of the effect, run in 36 laboratories with 3,531 participants, found in its preregistered analyses no evidence for a depletion effect (d = 0.06, not significant); the data were about four times more likely under no effect than under the informed-prior hypothesis of an effect of about δ = 0.30. An exploratory analysis of the full sample, ignoring the exclusion criteria, found a small significant effect (d = 0.08), and the effect was larger in participants who reported more fatigue (Vohs et al., 2021, full text read on a repository copy). The participants' mean age was 20.9 (2,375 women, 1,130 men), in laboratories mostly in the US; no sport outcome was measured. The earlier multilab replication, 23 laboratories and 2,141 participants, found an effect of d = 0.04 (95% CI −0.07 to 0.15), an interval that includes zero; its participants were undergraduate students aged 18 to 30 (Hagger et al., 2016, read on a copy of the published PDF). Grade: [contested, no effect shown in the preregistered analyses] by rule 1.

Grit. The claim: grit, passion and perseverance for long-term goals, is what separates achievers. The meta-analysis in the course list that tested grit's distinctness could not be opened for this unit. A 2025 systematic review of grit, resilience and physical activity (33 studies, 37,370 adults aged 18 and over, 5,113 of them athletes, among them elite wrestlers, ultra-runners and wheelchair rugby players) found grit associated with taking part in physical activity; of the six studies that assessed level of competition, five reported higher grit or resilience in athletes competing at higher levels, though one study found no difference in grit between international- and national-level wrestlers; most of its studies were cross-sectional, so the authors make no causal claim. It also states that grit's perseverance-of-effort component is highly correlated with conscientiousness, citing earlier work (Martin et al., 2025). The review admitted adults only, though one included study's sample may have contained 17-year-olds.

The size of growth-mindset effects. The claim: teaching a growth mindset, the belief that ability can be developed, transforms achievement. The two meta-analyses in the course list were read in full on a copy of the published PDF, not the publisher's page: one on how mindset relates to academic achievement (k = 273 effect sizes, N = 365,915 students; pooled r = .10, 95% CI .08 to .13), one on mindset interventions (k = 43, N = 57,155; pooled d = 0.08, 95% CI 0.02 to 0.14); the authors report "Overall effects were weak for both meta-analyses", and that some results suggested students of low socioeconomic status or academically at risk might benefit from mind-set interventions (Sisk et al., 2018, read on a copy of the published PDF). A national randomised experiment with a pre-registered analysis plan gave 12,490 ninth-grade students (aged 14 to 15, as the paper states) in 65 US public schools two online sessions of about 25 minutes. Among lower-achieving students (n = 6,320), core grade point average rose by 0.10 grade points (95% CI 0.04 to 0.16), a standardised effect of 0.11, and the effect was larger in schools whose peer norms supported taking on challenges (Yeager et al., 2019). The effect was real, small and conditional, on school grades, in adolescents. Both meta-analyses concern academic achievement; nothing in them or in the experiment measured sport.

A famous finding from a small sample is a hypothesis until someone runs it again, larger, with the analysis fixed in advance.

088. The popular claims, set against their evidence

Each claim is stated as it circulates, then set against a source opened for this unit or against "no peer-reviewed source found".

"The brain finishes developing at 25." The largest study opened for this unit pooled 123,984 MRI scans from 101,457 people aged from before birth to 100 years, drawn from more than 100 earlier studies, to chart brain measures by age; it describes and does not test a cause. Different measures peak at different ages: cortical thickness at 1.7 years, cortical grey matter volume at 5.9, subcortical grey matter at 14.4, white matter volume at 28.7 (Bethlehem et al., 2022). There is no single finishing age in these data, and the brain keeps changing across adult life. The study measures volumes, not abilities or judgement. For coaching: do not treat 25, or any age, as the point at which an athlete becomes able to decide [weak, against the claim]. A behavioural study pooled four datasets, two followed over time and two measured once, from 10,766 people aged 8 to 35, not described as athletes; its 23 measures of executive function, among them inhibition, working memory and planning, were found "stabilizing to adult-levels in late adolescence (18–20 years old)" (Tervo-Clemmens et al., 2023). It measures these functions, not decisions in sport, and comes from outside sport: weak by rule 5. SP2 deals with age and maturation and grades its matching recommendation for 19- to 25-year-olds the same way.

"A coach can read what an athlete feels from the face." A review in Psychological Science in the Public Interest, a journal of the Association for Psychological Science, examined the published studies on whether facial movements reliably and specifically signal particular emotions across people, situations and cultures, and concluded that they do not (a review of research outside sport); the same publisher's summary notes an accompanying article that argues for a richer model of expression across face, voice and body (Barrett et al., 2019, publisher's summary page and the first part of the full text read). The face carries information; it is not a reliable readout of what an athlete feels. Ask, and compare with the athlete's own baseline [practice]. The evidence against reading faces grades as weak under this course's rules, because it comes from outside sport; its direction is against the claim. SP7 builds this.

"10,000 hours, or early specialisation, makes a champion." A meta-analysis of deliberate practice and sport performance found that deliberate practice accounted for 18% of the variance in sports performance overall and 1% among elite-level performers, and that athletes who reached a high level of skill had not begun their sport earlier in childhood than lower-skill athletes; these are correlations (Macnamara et al., 2016, abstract read). It does not test a 10,000-hour threshold; for that figure as a rule: no peer-reviewed source found. On early specialisation, a 2024 meta-analysis (51 effect sizes, 6,233 athletes, 82% male, studies from 2009 to 2022) found that entering a talent promotion programme earlier was associated with higher junior performance and with lower senior performance: "effects on short-term junior performance versus long-term senior performance are opposite" (Güllich & Barth, 2024). The pattern held across individual and team sports and across performance levels. It is a correlation: it cannot show that early entry caused the later shortfall. Do not read early entry to a talent programme as a forecast of senior success [strong, for the association; earlier entry, lower senior performance].

"A power pose before competing raises confidence and performance"; "Willpower is a tank that runs dry"; "Grit is what separates winners"; "A growth mindset transforms performance." Set against their evidence in section 7. Power pose: posture did not change hormones in the two randomised tests opened; one found higher self-reported feelings of power after high-power poses (Ranehill et al., 2015), and the other tested the hormone claim with a different posture schedule and reports that it did not replicate the original hormonal effects (Metzler & Grèzes, 2019); for sport performance, no peer-reviewed source found [weak, no effect on hormones or risk-taking shown; higher self-reported feelings of power in one study]. Willpower: a 36-laboratory preregistered test found no depletion effect in its preregistered analyses (Vohs et al., 2021, full text read on a repository copy); for sport performance, no peer-reviewed source found [contested, no effect shown in the preregistered analyses; the earlier 23-laboratory replication's interval also included zero (Hagger et al., 2016)]. Grit: associated with physical activity in adults, mostly cross-sectionally (Martin et al., 2025); in that review, five of the six studies that assessed level of competition found higher grit or resilience at higher levels, mostly cross-sectionally [weak, positive, for an association]. Growth mindset: a small, conditional effect on school grades (Yeager et al., 2019); for sport performance, no peer-reviewed source found [weak, positive and small, outside sport].

"Learning styles; left-brained and right-brained athletes; we use 10% of the brain." A survey of 242 primary and secondary school teachers in the UK and the Netherlands found that 93% (UK) and 96% (Netherlands) agreed that people learn better when taught in their preferred learning style, 91% and 86% that left-brain or right-brain dominance explains differences between learners, and 48% and 46% that we use only 10% of the brain; the authors class all three as neuromyths. Teachers with more general knowledge about the brain believed more of the myths (Dekker et al., 2012). The sample was teachers; the study measures belief and does not test learning styles. Whether sorting athletes by learning style or by brain side helps or harms is not graded here: the studies that test learning styles were not opened for this unit (not verified), so no rule of section 9 can be applied; a later unit that opens them grades it.

"Telling yourself not to think about it works." A systematic review of ironic effects in motor tasks under pressure found 24 studies published before January 2022 (1,152 participants, in the 17 articles that reported the 24 studies), and most of their findings supported Wegner's account: trying not to do something under pressure can make that error more likely (Bartura et al., 2024). Half the studies used novices, a third used highly trained athletes competing nationally and one used elite athletes; mean age was about 22, and one study included under-18s. The review pools nothing, and whether three or more of the athlete studies were controlled, which rule 4 needs for moderate, was not checked here, so this unit grades it by rule 5. Wegner's own papers could not be opened for this unit. Phrase instructions as what to do, not what to avoid [weak, positive; samples mostly adults, so for athletes under 18 an inference; DD-SP3 regrades it from the primary studies]. DD-SP3 (section 2) opened that one study: in 43 female volleyball players aged 13 to 16, serving under low and then high pressure in a fixed order with no control group, serve performance did not change overall and no ironic effect was shown, so it grades the effect for 13 to 16 [weak, no effect shown in the one study opened] and keeps [weak, positive] for adults and for the what-to-do rule.

"Harsh coaching builds toughness." No peer-reviewed source found that shows it does. Three surveys, each measured once, point the other way. A survey of 103 male footballers aged 12 to 17 at one club found a controlling style predicted need thwarting, which predicted burnout (Morales-Sánchez et al., 2020). In the study opened in section 2, athletes who reported a more controlling coach reported lower need satisfaction, associated in turn with fear of failure (Hu et al., 2023). In 479 handball players aged 16 and 17 on regional teams, a climate centred on comparison with others went with more fear of failure, and through it with more anxiety and lower self-confidence before competition; this study measured the climate, not controlling behaviour (Gómez-López et al., 2020). Each is a correlation: none can show cause. A practitioner article on developing resilience (outside the source types the course lists; no grade rests on it), which reports no test of its own, notes that environments of extreme challenge with no support have been created in the name of toughening performers up, and distinguishes its approach from "the unethical and inappropriate imposition of adversity" (Fletcher & Sarkar, 2016, abstract and notes read). Grade: [weak, against the claim] (rule 5: correlational studies, no controlled test). The reviews by Bartholomew et al. (2009, 2011) in the course list could not be opened and carry no claim here; DD-SP1 returns to them.

"The amygdala hijack; the lizard brain." For the "lizard brain", the idea that a reptilian core, an emotional limbic layer and a rational cortex work fairly independently: a 2022 narrative review (not systematic, so outside the source types the course lists; no grade rests on it) argues that this triune model does not explain how the brain works in everyday life, that emotion and cognition are interdependent, and that purely emotional or purely cognitive circuits do not exist (Steffen et al., 2022). For the "amygdala hijack" as a mechanism: no peer-reviewed source found.

"Dopamine explains motivation, as told in videos." The videos usually present dopamine as the pleasure chemical. A narrative review by Berridge (not a systematic review, so outside the source types the course lists; no grade rests on it) reports that, in animal studies, dopamine mediated wanting, the pull towards a reward, and not liking, the pleasure of it, and that raising dopamine does not reliably raise people's ratings of pleasure: "Dopamine then failed to live up to its hedonic reputation" (Berridge, 2018). Most of that evidence comes from rats, some from people; none from sport.

"The brain cannot tell a vividly imagined action from a real one." No peer-reviewed source found for the claim as stated. An EEG study of 24 adults (21 analysed) found brain activity that overlapped when walking and when imagining walking, and also differed: imagery needed additional cortical resources (Mustile et al., 2024). On performance, imagery interventions gave g = 0.75 (95% CI 0.14 to 1.36) in parallel-group studies of mostly adult athletes, an effect that did not stay significant when restricted to randomised trials and objective outcomes (Reinebo et al., 2024); in tennis players aged 9 to 18, imagery training improved serve accuracy, g = 0.99 (95% CI 0.68 to 1.30), with certainty the authors rate low (Deng et al., 2024). Imagery has evidence behind it as a skill [moderate, positive]; in the study opened, imagining and doing were not the same brain activity.

"Mental toughness is a trait you install." A systematic review of mental toughness and individual differences (51 papers describing 56 studies, in school pupils, students, employees, military candidates and athletes) reports, from four twin studies, that "Approximately 50% of the variation in MT can be accounted for by genetic factors", and associations with positive traits, coping and outcomes in education and mental health. On developing it, the review reports two psychological skills training programmes in athletes that raised later mental toughness ratings, without saying whether they had control groups. It concludes that there is, at present, a lack of empirical support for the importance of such interventions, and that the suggestion that they would boost performance remains speculative (Lin et al., 2017). Neither programme study was opened for this unit. For a programme that installs it as a trait: no peer-reviewed source found.

"Neuro-linguistic programming improves performance." For sport performance: no peer-reviewed source found. The systematic review found for neuro-linguistic programming in health (10 experimental studies, five of them randomised trials with samples of 22 to 106) concluded: "There is little evidence that NLP interventions improve health-related outcomes", adding that this reflects few and weak studies (Sturt et al., 2012) [weak, no benefit shown].

"Brain-training apps transfer to sport." A systematic review of 43 studies of seven commercial cognitive training devices found one study in athletes (soccer players: passing accuracy improved, dribbling and shooting did not); it found small samples and little independent replication, and concluded there is "little direct evidence that the use of CCT devices can transfer" to sport performance (Harris et al., 2018) [weak, no benefit shown].

099. The five evidence grades

Every later unit grades every technique and recommendation with one of these five. The definitions are the course's own:

  • strong (consistent meta-analytic evidence in athletes)
  • moderate (a meta-analysis of small or mixed studies, or several controlled studies in athletes)
  • weak (single studies, or studies outside sport)
  • contested (failed replications, or reviews that disagree)
  • practice (professional consensus or coaching practice with no controlled test)

So that two readers give the same grade, the course applies them by these rules, in this order. Stop at the first rule that fits.

  1. Contested if a pre-registered or multi-site replication failed to find an effect the original reported, or if two reviews or meta-analyses reach opposite conclusions on whether the effect exists.
  2. Practice if no study of any design (controlled, correlational or meta-analytic) tests the claim, and it rests on professional consensus or coaching practice. A claim tested only by correlational studies has no controlled test but is not practice: it goes on to rules 3 to 5.
  3. Strong if at least one meta-analysis pooling studies of athletes finds an effect whose confidence interval excludes zero, the effect holds in the meta-analysis's own sensitivity or subgroup analyses, its authors rate the primary studies as adequate or better, and no meta-analysis or review in athletes found for the unit disagrees.
  4. Moderate if there is a meta-analysis in athletes that fails a condition of rule 3 (small studies, wide or unstable estimates, heterogeneity its authors flag, low certainty), or three or more controlled studies in athletes that agree. "Several" in the definition means three or more in this course.
  5. Weak in every other case: one or two controlled studies in athletes, or any amount of evidence from outside sport (non-athletes, school pupils, laboratories, animals).

Three further rules. The direction goes beside the grade (weak, no benefit shown; moderate, positive). A grade from adults, students or non-athletes applied to an athlete under 18 carries the label inference. A correlational meta-analysis can earn strong for an association, never for a cause.

1010. Five questions to ask of any claim

  1. Where is the study? The paper behind the claim, not the person repeating it. With no paper, the best grade is practice.
  2. Who was studied, and how many? Athletes or not; children, adolescents, students or adults; tens or thousands. If your athletes are younger than the sample, using it is an inference.
  3. Compared with what? A control group, assigned by chance? Without one, the study shows change, not cause; two things measured together are a correlation.
  4. How big, and how sure? What effect size, in the study's own terms, and how wide is its confidence interval? A large effect from a small sample is a hypothesis.
  5. Has it held up? Has anyone run it again, larger, with the analysis fixed in advance, or pooled it with other studies?

The answers feed the rules of section 9 and give a grade with its direction.

Three tools. The first two are built in full on the case athletes, then given as templates for your own squad. The grade key is the course's, and every later unit uses it.

A. Scope-of-practice card

Built on the case. Facts come from the case register; facts this unit adds are listed in its notes.

B. Claim-check sheet

Worked on three claims from the course's list.

Claim 1: "A power pose before competing raises confidence and performance." (Milan's coach has been told this at a coaching clinic.)

Question Answer
1. Where is the study? The test opened: a randomised study (Metzler & Grèzes, 2019). The first replication (Ranehill et al., 2015) is reported in section 7; the original was not opened.
2. Who was studied, and how many? 82 men aged 17 to 32 (81 analysed), recruited through a participant pool and student job sites; not athletes. Milan is 17: inference.
3. Compared with what? Expansive against constrictive posture, assigned at random. Controlled.
4. How big, and how sure? No difference by posture in testosterone, cortisol or progesterone. Confidence and performance were not measured.
5. Has it held up? The hormone part did not hold in this test or in the replication; the replication found higher self-reported feelings of power. No sport performance study found.
Grade Weak, no effect on hormones or risk-taking shown; higher self-reported feelings of power in one study (rule 5: controlled studies outside sport, this one and Ranehill et al. (2015) in section 7; it reports that it did not replicate the original hormonal effects, but it is not stated to be pre-registered and is not multi-site, so rule 1 does not apply). For sport performance: no peer-reviewed source found.
What I do I do not teach it as a technique [weak, no effect shown].

Claim 2: "The brain finishes developing at 25." (Sara has read this and told her coach she "isn't finished yet".)

Question Answer
1. Where is the study? None found that states it. The lifespan study opened: Bethlehem et al., 2022.
2. Who was studied, and how many? 101,457 people, 123,984 MRI scans, aged from before birth to 100. Not athletes.
3. Compared with what? Not an experiment: charts of brain measures by age.
4. How big, and how sure? Peaks at 1.7 years (cortical thickness), 5.9 (cortical grey matter volume), 14.4 (subcortical grey matter), 28.7 (white matter volume), each reported with its 95% bootstrap interval.
5. Has it held up? One very large study of brain measures. A behavioural study of executive function in 10,766 people aged 8 to 35, not athletes, found it at adult levels by 18 to 20 (section 8; Tervo-Clemmens et al., 2023).
Grade The claim as stated: no peer-reviewed source found. The finding that brain measures peak at different ages: weak, against the claim (rule 5: evidence from outside sport, however large).
What I do Tell Sara that no study behind the number 25 was found; ask her what she thinks she is not ready for, and talk about that [practice].

Claim 3: "Harsh coaching builds toughness." (Dani's coach says shouting in training makes his judoka tougher.)

Question Answer
1. Where is the study? None found that supports it. The study opened: Hu et al., 2023.
2. Who was studied, and how many? 252 athletes in team and individual sports in six Chinese provinces, aged 10 to 25 and over, 32 of them aged 10 to 14. Dani is 14: the sample's youngest band (10 to 14) includes her age.
3. Compared with what? Nothing: a cross-sectional survey. A correlation.
4. How big, and how sure? A controlling style was negatively correlated with need satisfaction; its association with fear of failure ran through need satisfaction and constrained commitment. The direct link was not significant.
5. Has it held up? Two more surveys, each measured once, point the same way. In 479 handball players aged 16 and 17, a climate centred on comparison with others went with more fear of failure (section 8; it measured the climate, not controlling behaviour). And in 103 male footballers aged 12 to 17 at one club, a controlling style predicted need thwarting, which predicted burnout (Morales-Sánchez et al., 2020). No controlled test was opened.
Grade Weak (rule 5: three correlational studies, no controlled test), and its direction is against the claim. For the claim that harshness builds toughness: no peer-reviewed source found.
What I do I do not use harshness as a method [weak, against].

C. The grade key

The definitions word for word, the rule that assigns each (apply in order, stop at the first that fits), and one worked example for each grade.

Grade Definition Rule that assigns it Worked example
strong consistent meta-analytic evidence in athletes Rule 3: a meta-analysis of athletes whose effect excludes zero, holds in its sensitivity or subgroup analyses, with primary studies rated adequate or better, and no disagreeing review found. "Early entry to a talent programme does not forecast senior success." A meta-analysis of 51 effects in 6,233 athletes found earlier entry associated with higher junior and lower senior performance, holding across individual and team sports and levels, with high-quality primary studies (Güllich & Barth, 2024). Strong for the association; it says nothing about cause.
moderate a meta-analysis of small or mixed studies, or several controlled studies in athletes Rule 4: a meta-analysis in athletes that fails a condition of rule 3, or three or more agreeing controlled studies in athletes. "A pre-performance routine improves performance." Pooled controlled effects of g = 0.64 and, under pressure, 0.70 (95% CI 0.24 to 1.16), but most samples had fewer than 20 participants and the pre-to-post data showed a small-study effect (Rupprecht et al., 2024). Moderate, positive; ages 11 to 40.
weak single studies, or studies outside sport Rule 5: every other case, such as one or two studies in athletes, or any evidence from outside sport. "A growth-mindset lesson improves performance." The best study opened is a large randomised experiment on school grades in 14- to 15-year-olds, with a small effect in lower achievers (Yeager et al., 2019). Weak for sport: outside sport.
contested failed replications, or reviews that disagree Rule 1: a pre-registered or multi-site replication failed, or two reviews reach opposite conclusions. "Willpower is a tank that runs dry." A preregistered test run in 36 laboratories with 3,531 participants found, in its preregistered analyses, no evidence for a depletion effect (d = 0.06, not significant); an exploratory analysis of the full sample, ignoring the exclusion criteria, found d = 0.08 (Vohs et al., 2021, full text read on a repository copy). Contested by rule 1: a multi-site preregistered replication failed to find the effect, as did an earlier 23-laboratory replication (Hagger et al., 2016). Not a sport study; participants' mean age 20.9.
practice professional consensus or coaching practice with no controlled test Rule 2: no study of any design tests the claim; it rests on consensus or practice. "The coach recognises and refers, and leaves assessment to licensed or registered professionals." The IOC built this division into its two mental health tools by a consensus process; no study opened for this unit tests it against any other (Gouttebarge et al., 2021, full text read). Practice (consensus).

Two labels travel with a grade. Direction: positive, no benefit shown, or against. Inference: added whenever the grade comes from adults, students or non-athletes and is applied to an athlete under 18. A consensus or position statement is practice when the agreement is the evidence (the IOC division in the practice row), and is graded from its studies by rules 3 to 5 when it says which studies test the claim (SP2's sleep hours: weak, outside sport), applying rules 2 to 5 of section 9 as written.

The writer's opening note

This unit is information, not legal, medical or psychological advice. Where it touches a duty you may hold in law or under your federation's rules, it puts the duty as a question and names the kind of body that can answer it.

Every technique and recommendation carries a grade in square brackets, for example [practice], defined in section 9. A grade says how good the evidence is; its direction is stated beside it.

Three athletes run through the course: Dani (fictional), 14, judo; Milan (fictional), 17, football; Sara (fictional), 20, 400 metres. They illustrate; they are never evidence.

How this unit was made4 parts

Provenance

The course's first unit. It sets out what sport psychology is and who may practise it, the coach's part and where it ends, the kinds of evidence and how to read a study in plain words, what the reviews say about psychology and performance, four cautionary cases from the replication record, the popular claims coaches hear set against their evidence, the five evidence grades that every later unit uses, and five questions to ask of any claim. Its deliverable is a scope-of-practice card, a claim-check sheet and the grade key, each worked on the three fictional athletes and then given as a template. How it was made: written on 17 Nov 2026 by Opus 5.5 with web search off, then checked by a separate Opus 5.5 context that had not seen the writing (verdict: fix; 2 errors, 30 should-fix, 16 minor). The same day it was corrected in two rounds, each change checked against its source by a fresh Opus 5.5 checker before it was applied (21 of 27 checks passed in the first round, 16 of 16 in the second), and given five quality reviews by fresh Opus 5.5 agents with live search, every verdict fix: the first found 1 error, 9 should-fix and 7 minor (14 applied, 3 held: two because the source's own wording matched the unit, one because it belonged to the source log); the last found 0 errors, 4 should-fix and 1 minor, all applied. A cross-unit check of SP1 to SP3 by a fresh Opus 5.5 agent found 10 disagreements; the two in this unit were corrected, and two that would change the grade key itself are left to a syllabus review. On 18 Nov 2026 (row W-SP4) it was corrected again: a corrections check by a fresh Opus 5.5 agent with live search passed 15 of 19 changes (4 could not be checked because the sources could not be opened), and a sixth quality review by a fresh Opus 5.5 agent with live search found 0 errors, 4 should-fix and 8 minor; 12 of its findings were applied after a fresh agent checked each against its source, and one, on the wording of the grit review's competitive results, was left open after a second round. Known gaps (checked 18 Nov 2026). (1) References not opened, on which no claim rests: the Delphi study on the coach's role in athlete mental health (Bissett et al., 2020); the original power-posing study and its replication (Ranehill et al., 2015); the earlier multilab ego-depletion replication (Hagger et al., 2016); the grit meta-analysis (Credé et al., 2017), and the earlier work Martin et al. cite for grit's overlap with conscientiousness; the two mental toughness programme studies; the reviews on controlling coaching (Bartholomew et al., 2009 and 2011); Wegner's papers on ironic effects; the studies that test learning styles, so sorting athletes by learning style is not graded; and the meta-analysis of self-talk in sport, left to SP5, so the advice to replace 'just relax' with a specific cue is graded practice. The brief

Aim: say what is yours to do as a coach and what is not, and grade any claim about the mind in sport by its evidence
Takes: nothing; it is the course's first unit.
Gives: the scope line and the grade key every later unit uses; the claim-check sheet the reader keeps.

Unit SP1, What a coach can do, and how to tell evidence from noise. Cover: what sport psychology is and the professions in it (who may call themselves what, and who may treat, differs by country: put it as a question for the reader, with the kind of body to consult); the coach's part as the literature defines it (shaping the climate, teaching psychological skills, noticing, responding, referring) and where it ends (assessment, diagnosis, treatment); the kinds of evidence in this field (consensus statements, meta-analyses, controlled studies, correlational studies, case reports) and what each can show; how to read an effect size, a sample and a control group, in plain words; why most studies are small and on students or adults, and what that means for a coach of a 13-year-old; what the reviews of meta-analyses say about how far psychological variables and interventions relate to performance, with their limits; the replication record as cautionary cases, each from its replication study or meta-analysis (power posing, ego depletion, grit, the size of growth-mindset effects); the popular claims on the course's list, each stated as it circulates and then set against its evidence, saying 'no peer-reviewed source found' where none was; the course's five evidence grades, defined so that two readers would give the same grade; five questions to ask of any claim met in a video, a clinic or a book. Deliverable: a scope-of-practice card (what I do, what I refer, to whom; blanks for the reader's country, federation and club); a claim-check sheet of five questions, worked in full on three claims from the list; and the grade key every later unit uses, with one worked example per grade.
