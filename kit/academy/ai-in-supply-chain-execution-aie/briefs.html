AI IN EXECUTION · PUBLIC · BRIEFS

AI in Supply Chain Execution: the briefs

One brief per unit, self-contained: the shared preamble (the reader, the frame, the four families and the ladder, the vendor and model-provider rules, the evidence grades and setting tags, how a study is reported, the common claims, the decision pattern, people and the law, the case operations, the deliverables, the safety rules, the tone, the course goal, the sourcing standard, figures) is baked into every open brief, followed by the unit's Aim, Takes and Gives lines. Written units show their note instead. The course itself is read in Course AIE.

AIE1

What counts as AI here

not written

Four families of technique under one label, a ladder of automation, and the keys every later unit grades by.

Aim place a feature in a family and on a rung, and grade a claim about it

Takes none. Gives the family key, the ladder, the grade and setting key and the decision map that every later unit uses.

Shipsfamily key + five-rung ladder + grade and setting key + decision map

Opus brief
Write one unit of a self-study course in the long-form report format of the Learning Project's markdown courses: a long-form report in Markdown. Length is whatever the topic needs (2,000–6,000 words for the body through the Self-check; the deliverable is outside that count); do not pad and do not cut to a target. Quality over quantity: a shorter unit whose every claim was opened and checked is better than a longer one. Reader: someone who builds, sells or implements order, warehouse or transport software, or is moving into that work: a product manager, a designer, an engineer, an implementer or a solutions consultant; experienced in their own craft; knows the ground of the track's first course, Supply Chain Execution for Product Teams (the operation, the three systems, the roles, the buyers), or has worked near it; has no background in machine learning or operations research; reads in order (each unit assumes the ones before it), reads on a phone, checks the citations, and wants genuine depth rather than a primer. Address the reader as 'you'. This course is written to be shared with anyone; it is an exception to the Learning Project's single-reader rule. Nothing about the Project's owner, his employer, his role or his work appears in it. The course stands on its own files: it may name the track's first course by its title, and never points to one of its units by code. Frame: every AI feature is treated as a decision. Say what is decided, how often, how long there is to decide, who or what decided it before, on what data, and what it costs when it is wrong. Place each feature in one of the four families set in AIE1 (rules and heuristics; mathematical optimisation; prediction by machine learning; generative models and agents) and on one rung of AIE1's ladder of automation, and say what a person still decides. Assume no machine-learning or optimisation vocabulary: define each term on first use and add it to the unit's glossary lines. No mathematics beyond arithmetic and no code. Teach no model-building, prompt-writing, design, research, engineering or sales method; the reader has their craft. The course is for learning the domain. It is not interview preparation and no unit is framed as one. Boundaries: demand forecasting and inventory planning belong to the track's planning course, and robots and their control software to its automation course; where a source raises them, the unit says so in one sentence and moves on. Vendors: no real vendor is assessed, ranked, compared or recommended in this course, and no real vendor's product line or AI feature is mapped. A real vendor, and a company that provides AI models, is named only in two ways: to attribute a public statement or a piece of its own documentation to it (dated, graded vendor-reported), or as an entry in a dated list taken from a named analyst report or an audited filing, every vendor on that list given alike. No vendor receives more space, a warmer word or a harder one than another; nothing from inside any vendor appears. A company that a peer-reviewed study names as its setting is named as that study's setting, with nothing said of it beyond what the study states. Worked product examples use the fictional vendors of the case register (Halvern Systems, Varnby Software, Kestrow) and their fictional AI features, labelled '(fictional)'. A vendor's description of an AI feature is evidence of what it claims, never of what the feature does. Evidence grades: every claim that carries a number, a mechanism or a recommendation carries one grade from the key set in AIE1, which restates the track's four grades: independent (peer-reviewed research; an industry association's survey with its sample stated; an audited company filing, for that company's own figures; the text of a standard, a law or a regulator's own statement, for what it says), analyst (a named analyst firm's report or release, with its date; a prediction is called a prediction), vendor-reported (a vendor's or model provider's page, release, case study or documentation; a consultancy's survey, since it sells the work it surveys), practice (a textbook's or practitioner's account with no measurement behind it). Every claim about how well something performs also carries one setting tag from AIE1's key: field trial (a controlled comparison in a working operation), field observation (data from a working operation with no control), simulation (a model run on synthetic or historical data), benchmark (a test set built for comparison), demonstration (shown working once), or none. A simulated or benchmarked result is never written as if it had been measured in an operation. A preprint is called a preprint and never carries a recommendation by itself. Reporting a study: state its setting, sample, design, what was compared with what, the period and who measured, and give the effect in the source's own terms; a correlation is called a correlation. A finding from another kind of work (a call centre, a consultancy, a hospital, a cockpit) applied to execution work is labelled as the unit's inference. A result obtained with one model or version says which, and its transfer to a current model is the unit's inference. Common claims: where a claim is common and its support is thin, name the claim and say what the evidence shows; the course's list is in the seed bibliography; say 'no independent source found' where none was. Decision pattern (AIE6, AIE7, AIE8 and DD-AIE1): treat each decision as the decision itself and its cadence, who or what made it before, the family doing the work, the data it needs, the rung, how it commonly fails, who carries the cost when it is wrong, what a person still decides, and the measure that would show it improved. People: a claim about jobs, pace, injury or surveillance is sourced to peer-reviewed work, a regulator or a named research report with its method, or it is not made. No unit gives a method for covertly monitoring an identified worker or for avoiding a duty to inform or consult workers. Law and regions: say which jurisdiction a rule belongs to; cite the text or the authority's own page, never a law firm's or consultancy's summary; no legal point is stated as the reader's: it is put as a question with the body to consult; the unit says it is information, not legal advice. Running case: three fictional operations: Corvane, a fashion retailer; Ostrel Foods, a food distributor; Tandric Logistics, a logistics provider. Their facts come only from courses/ai-in-supply-chain-execution/notes/case-facts.md; label each '(fictional)' on first use in the unit; a fact the unit invents about them is listed in the notes so that it can be added to the register; the case is never evidence for a claim about the world; no case operation is ever said to use a real vendor's product or a real company's model. Deliverables are built twice: in full on the case the brief names, then as a template the reader fills for a real feature or operation, each blank saying what goes in it. Every unit ends its body, before the deliverable, with two short sections: 'In a demo or a pilot review' (what to look for and what to ask the vendor, the site or a colleague) and 'Self-check' (five to eight questions an operations lead or a data scientist would expect the reader to answer, each with a short answer drawn from the unit's sourced text). Safety rules that hold in every unit: no price, discount, revenue or win rate of a private company is stated unless that company published it; no real customer of any vendor is described beyond what a dated public source states. Tone: direct, no flattery, no filler, no motivational language, no 'not X but Y' constructions; no forecast of what AI will do that the unit cannot attribute. Course goal: By the end, the reader can take any claimed AI feature in order, warehouse or transport software and say which decision it makes or supports, which kind of technique is doing the work, what a person still decides, who carries the cost when it is wrong, and how strong the evidence is that it works. Sourcing standard: every claim that carries a number, a mechanism or a recommendation is sourced where it is made; acceptable sources are peer-reviewed research, including peer-reviewed conference proceedings named as such; preprints, labelled; standard textbooks cited by edition and chapter; standards bodies' own pages; the text of a law or regulation at its issuing body's page, and a regulator's own release or order; industry association reports and surveys with their sample and year; research reports of a university centre or an independent research organisation, labelled as reports, with their method; analyst reports and releases, by firm, title and date, read in full, in a public reprint or in abstract, the unit saying which; audited company filings; and vendors', model providers' and consultancies' own pages, documentation and surveys, labelled vendor-reported and dated; videos, podcasts, social-media posts, unsigned blogs, comparison and lead-generation sites, law-firm and consultancy summaries of a law, and press reports of a study or a release are not sources, and may be named only as the claim under examination or used to find the original; every reference is opened before it is cited: found by a search, read at its publisher's page, its DOI address or the issuing body's own page, and recorded in the unit's source log with the address, what was read (abstract, reprint or full text) and the date; a claim that rests on an abstract alone says so; a reference that could not be opened carries no claim; the seed bibliography (courses/ai-in-supply-chain-execution/notes/bibliography.md) is a starting list whose entries were checked for existence only, never an authority on what they say; a figure is cited to the work that reports it, never to one that repeats it; no uncited figures; no invented names, companies, studies, quotations or numbers; if a claim cannot be sourced, say so and leave it out of the deliverable. A unit that reads as advice without evidence is a failed unit. Links are given as links (the DOI address for a paper), so the course bibliography can collect them. Figures: where a section's content has a shape, declare it as a fenced viz block of a kind kit/viz.py draws (one to four a unit is a guide; none where nothing has a shape); a figure adds no fact, and its caption says what it shows and where its data comes from. Where the unit depends on current facts (laws and their dates, standards, analyst reports, vendors' and model providers' documentation, surveys), search before writing and date the facts. End with the unit's deliverable, built out in full, not described.

Unit AIE1, What counts as AI here. Search before writing. Cover: why one label over four different kinds of technique is a problem for product work; how standards bodies and the law define an AI system (the ISO/IEC terminology standard, the OECD definition, the definition article of the EU regulation on AI, the NIST framework), each read at its issuing body's page, and where the definitions differ; the four families this course uses (rules and heuristics; mathematical optimisation; prediction by machine learning; generative models and agents), each defined so that two readers would place the same feature in the same family, with the test for each and the features that sit across two; what each family needs in order to work (a model of the problem; past cases with their outcomes; text and tools) and the way each is wrong when it is wrong; execution as a stream of decisions: the decisions on one order's path from promise to delivery, at the level of a map only, each with how often it is made and how long there is to make it; supporting a decision as against making it, and the types and levels of automation from the human-factors literature, with the date and setting of that work, reduced to a ladder of five rungs this course uses, each rung saying what the person still decides; the kinds of evidence in this field and what each can show (a field experiment, field data without a control, a simulation, a benchmark, a vendor case study, an analyst report, a survey of practitioners, a preprint); the grade key and the setting-tag key, defined so that two readers would give the same grade and tag, including how a preprint, a consultancy survey, an analyst prediction and a regulator's statement are handled; the course's list of common claims, each stated as it circulates, set against the evidence this unit opens, and marked with the unit that tests it. Deliverable: the family key, with one worked example per family and two borderline cases decided; the five-rung ladder; the grade and setting key, with one worked example per grade and per tag; a decision map for Corvane (decision, how often, time to decide, who or what decides today, family, rung); a working glossary that later units extend; then the decision map as a template.
