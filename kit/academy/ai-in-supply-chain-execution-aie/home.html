AI in Supply Chain Execution

Learning to read AI features in order, warehouse and transport software: which decision each makes or supports, which of four families of technique does the work, what a person still decides, who carries the cost when it is wrong, and how strong the evidence is. The decision first, the technique second, the claim third; demand forecasting, inventory planning and robots are out of scope, no real vendor or model provider is assessed, and the worked examples use the track's three fictional operations and three fictional vendors, with fictional AI features and six fictional claims. Thirteen core units in an opening and three parts, and one deep dive.

What you will be able to do

By the end, the reader can take any claimed AI feature in order, warehouse or transport software and say which decision it makes or supports, which kind of technique is doing the work, what a person still decides, who carries the cost when it is wrong, and how strong the evidence is that it works.

  • Outcome 1. Given a feature description, names the decision, places it in one of four families and on one of five rungs, and says what the person still decides, so that a second reader using the keys agrees (AIE1 to AIE4, AIE6 to AIE8).
  • Outcome 2. For a decision automated at a given rung, writes the override sheet and the risk register: who carries the cost, how the error is noticed, which text governs it where (AIE9, AIE10, DD-AIE1).
  • Outcome 3. Grades a vendor's AI claim on the claim sheet, designs a pilot that could show it false, and writes a feature brief that rests on both (AIE5, AIE11, AIE12, AIE13).

Every unit is sourced to peer-reviewed research, standards bodies' and regulators' own pages, the text of laws at their issuing bodies' pages, analyst reports, surveys, audited filings and vendors' and model providers' own dated pages, grades every claim (independent, analyst, vendor-reported, practice), tags every claim about performance by its setting, assesses no real vendor or model provider, and ends with a deliverable built in full on the fictional case and then as a template.

Frame

Pick

The decision first, the technique second, the claim third.

  • Every AI feature in this software makes or supports one operational decision.
  • Four technique families fail in four different ways; the label hides which.
  • Most evidence is simulation or vendor-told; the course grades and tags it.

Override if: you want a course about generative AI and agents only; then AIE2 and AIE3 shrink to one unit and AIE4 grows to three.

  • What the course is for. Being able to look at a claimed AI feature and say what it decides, what kind of technique does the work, what a person still decides, who pays when it is wrong, and how good the evidence is. It is the third course of the Supply Chain track. No unit is interview preparation.
  • Stands on its own files. It reuses the track's fictional case and assumes the ground of Supply Chain Execution for Product Teams, and it never points to that course's units by code, so it does not wait for that course to be finished.
  • Out of scope. Demand forecasting and inventory planning (the planning course). Robots and their control software (the automation course). How to build a model, write a prompt or design an interface.
  • No real vendor is assessed. The first course's rule, unchanged, extended to companies that provide AI models. The fictional vendors gain fictional AI features and six fictional claims.
  • Where the evidence pushes back. The solid work is decades of optimisation research; it was not called AI when it was written. Machine learning for batching, slotting and routing is published mostly as simulation on synthetic or historical data; field experiments in execution work are few. The human-factors research comes from cockpits, control rooms and clinics, and its transfer to a dispatch desk is an inference, which the units say. For generative features and agents in execution software no independent field study was found when the course was designed; expect "vendor-reported" and "no independent source found" often. The statistics that circulate lead back to interviews, polls and predictions. The law is moving, so units read the texts and date them.
