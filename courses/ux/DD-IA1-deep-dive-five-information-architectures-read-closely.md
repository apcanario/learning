# DD-IA1 · Deep dive: five information architectures read closely

IA1 to IA12 built the IA of one fictional product, Perqo (Perqo, fictional), from its website to the map that joins its three surfaces. This unit turns the same tools on five real products and reads each from outside: from the pages anyone can open, and from what each product's makers have published about its structure. The five are chosen to span the course's four levels: GOV.UK, a content-rich public site, for IA5's website; the Monzo app, a consumer app, for IA6's client app; the Stripe Dashboard, a business backoffice, for IA7; Airbnb, a marketplace with a purchase lifecycle, for the lifecycle layer of IA8's system map; and Shopify, one system across several surfaces, for the surface layer of IA8's map. For each the unit reads the organisation scheme and structure, the labelling, the navigation model, the search, the objects, the lifecycle it serves, the one decision that most shapes it, what it got right on the evidence and where its public structure shows strain. Then it compares the five. The deliverable is the five readings, recorded in the form of IA12's assumption register, the comparison table, and the lessons for Perqo, with one change proposed as a decision log entry.

Every observation in this unit was made on 10 November 2026, the day each page was read, and is dated in the text. Nothing is said about any product beyond what its public pages show or its makers have published. Every figure uses IA2's notation (IA2, section 5.2 and the notation sheet). This unit draws three of the notation's six tones. Tone 2, "the merchant side and anything outside Perqo", carries every box of the five real products that tone 6 does not mark, because all five are outside Perqo. Tone 6, a stop, "a path that ends without success, or a fault found in a review", marks the paths that end without success in a lifecycle, the strains this reading finds and, in section 7's second figure, a layer in which the pages read publish nothing, the gap this review finds in a published map. Tone 5, shared, carries one row: the Perqo interaction map (IA8), set beside the five in section 7's second figure.

## 1 · How a real product's IA is read from outside

**What can be read.** Two kinds of evidence are open to someone outside a company. The first is its public screens: the pages a visitor sees without signing in. The second is what its makers publish about the structure: design-system documentation, help-centre articles, developer documentation, and posts on the company's own blogs. The course's sourcing rule sets the limit: nothing is asserted about a real company's IA beyond what is visible on its public screens, dated, or documented by the company itself, cited. Two further limits come from how this unit read the pages. Every page was fetched as text on 10 November 2026, so a layout that cannot be read from a page's text is not asserted; and nothing behind a sign-in was seen. Three of the five products (the Monzo app, the Stripe Dashboard and Shopify's admin) sit almost wholly behind a sign-in, so their readings rest on their makers' documentation, and each reading names the screens it could not see.

**What it is worth to Perqo.** IA12's proxy table gives the answer before any product is read. Competitors' public screens show "what others chose to show, and their words for shared things"; they cannot show "whether it works for anyone; anything behind a sign-in, which is where a backoffice sits"; alone they reach C0 on IA11's ladder (IA12, section 2; IA11, section 6.4). The five products here are not Perqo's competitors, and some of their makers publish tests, but the same ceiling holds for anything this unit carries to Perqo: a test GOV.UK ran on GOV.UK's users is evidence about GOV.UK. For Perqo it is a reasoned default, C0, until Perqo's own test raises it. Every lesson in Deliverable C is held there.

**The lens.** Each reading applies three filed tools. IA2's five faults (IA2, section 3) are asked of every published artefact: does it state its decision, reader, scope and date; is its notation declared; are its levels mixed; does it show conditions and unhappy paths; do its labels drift from the vocabulary. IA12's triage tree (IA12, Deliverable A) is turned outward: for each product, the published account shows which inputs the makers had (research, stakeholders who answered, trusted content, requirements that held still) and which shortage it shows. And IA12's register fields (IA12, section 3.2) are the form in which Deliverable A records what each published artefact claims, what evidence the makers published, and what it leaves untested.

**Why these five.** Each was chosen because its makers document its structure in public, so that the reading rests on evidence, and because together they stand for the four levels:

| Product | Maker | Stands for | Why it was chosen |
|---|---|---|---|
| GOV.UK | Government Digital Service | IA5, the website | a public site with more than half a million pages (GDS's figure, 2021; section 2), whose makers publish their taxonomy principles and their navigation and search work on their own blogs |
| Monzo app | Monzo | IA6, the client app | a money app whose first screen shows balances, as the Perqo app's does, with its redesign and its help pages published by Monzo |
| Stripe Dashboard | Stripe | IA7, the backoffice | a backoffice of many object types, documented page by page, including how its lifecycle statuses are named on the Dashboard |
| Airbnb | Airbnb | IA8, the actor and lifecycle layer | a two-sided marketplace whose purchase lifecycle waits on a second actor, the host, with its structural releases published in its newsroom |
| Shopify | Shopify | IA8, the surface layer | one admin behind several sales surfaces, with shared products and orders, documented in its Help Center and its app design guidelines |

## 2 · GOV.UK: a content-rich public site (IA5)

**Scheme and structure.** The home page at gov.uk, read on 10 November 2026, is split in two. Under "Services and information" it lists 16 topics, from "Benefits" and "Births, deaths, marriages and care" to "Visas and immigration" and "Working, jobs and pensions"; under "Government activity" it lists links by department and document type: "Departments", "News", "Guidance and regulation", "Research and statistics", "Policy papers and consultations", "Transparency", "How government works" and "Get involved" (GOV.UK, home page, [gov.uk](https://gov.uk), and "Services and information", [gov.uk, browse](https://gov.uk/browse), both read 10 November 2026; public screens). The services half is a topic scheme, IA4's ambiguous scheme by subject (IA4, section 1.1); the government half is organised by who publishes and what kind of document it is. Each topic opens a browse page, and GOV.UK's public content API shows how those are built: the Benefits page is a record of type `mainstream_browse_page` whose eight children are curated, `"second_level_ordering": "curated"` (GOV.UK content API, Benefits browse page record, [gov.uk, content API](https://www.gov.uk/api/content/browse/benefits), read 10 November 2026; publisher's public data). That is IA4's hierarchy, designed top-down (IA4, section 2.1). Beside it runs a second structure, a topic taxonomy. Its principles, published by GDS, say "The taxonomy is hierarchical", "A topic should have between 2 and 12 child topics (or none)", "The maximum number of topics at any level should ideally be around 12", "Content should be tagged to the lowest possible level of the taxonomy" and "Content should not be tagged to both a topic and its parent" (GDS, "GOV.UK Taxonomy principles", published 13 June 2019, [gov.uk, taxonomy principles](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/govuk-topic-taxonomy-principles/govuk-taxonomy-principles), read 10 November 2026; publisher's documentation). The taxonomy's topic pages are reached from content pages, through sidebar links and breadcrumbs (same page).

**Labelling.** The labels are plain and specific, and mostly front-loaded with the word that tells them apart, as IA3's A2 asks (IA3, checklist). Two observations, both of 10 November 2026, fall short of the checklist. Seven of the eight children of Benefits begin with the same four words, "Benefits and financial support", followed by "for families", "if you're caring for someone", "if you're disabled or have a health condition", "if you're looking for work", "if you're on a low income", "if you're temporarily unable to work" and "when someone dies"; the eighth is "Manage an existing benefit, payment or claim" (content API, browse/benefits). Read by their first words, the seven are identical, which fails A2. And two labels drift within a page: the topic list reads "Births, deaths, marriages and care" where the navigation menu reads "Births, death, marriages and care", and the home page's first "Government activity" list says "Transparency documents" where its second says "Transparency" (gov.uk and gov.uk/browse, read 10 November 2026). That is IA2's fifth fault and IA3's D2 (one thing, one label) on the one site of the five whose makers publish rules for its vocabulary.

**Navigation model.** The global routes are the header's "Menu" and "Search GOV.UK", present on both pages read; the home page and gov.uk/browse carry the 16 topics as local routes into the services half; contextual routes are the taxonomy's sidebar links and breadcrumbs on content pages (taxonomy principles, 2019); and the supplemental route is the step by step, a pattern the GOV.UK Design System documents: it "presents an end to end journey in logical steps", is for journeys that "have a specific start and end point", and content pages that are part of one "always have a link at the top that goes to the standalone step by step page" (GOV.UK Design System, "Step by step navigation", [design-system.service.gov.uk, step by step](https://design-system.service.gov.uk/patterns/step-by-step-navigation), read 10 November 2026; design-system guidance). In 2021 GDS described what the model had become: about 200 mainstream topics, 400 specialist topics and 4,000 taxonomy topics, three different menu bars depending on page type, and "This triple-system, which has substantial duplication, causes confusion for users" (Sam Dub, "Our plans to improve navigation on GOV.UK", GDS blog, 17 May 2021, [gds.blog.gov.uk, navigation plans](https://gds.blog.gov.uk/2021/05/17/our-plans-to-improve-navigation-on-gov-uk); practice, a maker's account). The same post gives the scale, more than half a million pages and an average of 17.8 million users a week in 2021, and the route most people take: "while most users start from a search engine, around 15% use topic pages to navigate" (Dub 2021).

**Search.** GOV.UK's site search is "one of the main ways that people find information on GOV.UK"; in 2024 it moved to Google's Vertex AI Search, live from the end of February, and the makers report from an A/B test "a 14% increase in overall clickthrough from the search results page", "an 11% decrease in users having to refine their search" and "an 8% reduction in users leaving site from the search page" (Catriona Fraser, Christian Sutter and Tara Stockford, "A new search engine for GOV.UK", Inside GOV.UK, 23 October 2024, [insidegovuk.blog.gov.uk, new search engine](https://insidegovuk.blog.gov.uk/2024/10/23/a-new-search-engine-for-gov-uk/); a maker's report of its own test, no intervals given). A later post gives its use, "more than 4 million times a month"; it moved filters and sorting into "a single panel above the search results", because before the change "only a small minority of people selected filters", and it reports autocomplete suggestions used in 55% of searches where they appeared (Catriona Fraser and Monica Crusellas, "Making it quicker and easier to search on GOV.UK", Inside GOV.UK, 12 December 2024, [insidegovuk.blog.gov.uk, quicker search](https://insidegovuk.blog.gov.uk/2024/12/12/making-it-quicker-and-easier-to-search-on-gov-uk); same status). Read against IA3: the site has the supplemental search the Polar Bear book asks about first, and its makers measure it by refinement, IA3's reformulated query (IA3, section 5.2; checklist E2), and by clickthrough and exit from the results page.

**Objects.** The objects a visitor meets are pages of a few types, and the content API names them: a browse page is a `mainstream_browse_page`, with links to its `children`, its `top_level_browse_pages`, its `primary_publishing_organisation` and its `available_translations` (content API, browse/benefits). On this unit's reading of the record, every page is a content item with a document type, an organisation that publishes it, and tags into one or more of the topic systems; the taxonomy's topics are a second kind of object, and the step by step a third. That is IA4's database model underneath a hierarchy: items described by fields, with the groups made from the tags (IA4, section 2.3).

**Lifecycle.** GOV.UK serves a citizen's task with a start and an end. The step by step is the only pattern read that draws such a lifecycle across pages, and the Design System is explicit that it is not for everything: not when "the user only needs to read guidance and not take an action", and not "inside a transactional service" (Step by step navigation, read 10 November 2026). The services themselves, where a claim is made or a licence applied for, sit behind their own start pages and were not read.

**The shaping decision.** The decision that most shapes GOV.UK, on its makers' own account, is to drive navigation from tagging. In 2019 a GDS product manager wrote that the goal is "a single sitewide taxonomy that powers front end navigation", that taxonomy tagging was rolled out to all Whitehall publisher app users in July 2018, and that "all content from the point of the rollout has been tagged to the taxonomy"; the older policies and policy areas "have been removed and redirected to equivalent topics" (Si Stephens-Manassiev, "An update on the GOV.UK taxonomy", Inside GOV.UK, 12 February 2019, [insidegovuk.blog.gov.uk, taxonomy update](https://insidegovuk.blog.gov.uk/2019/02/12/an-update-on-the-gov-uk-taxonomy/); practice, a maker's account). A site of half a million pages from many departments cannot be curated by hand page by page; the tag decides where a page appears.

**What it got right, and where it strains.** On the evidence its makers published, GOV.UK tests its structural changes before rolling them out: the 2025 change from a grid to a list on the 16 topic pages followed an A/B test in which "the list design helped people find content faster, with less time spent on the page", and a "Most viewed" section, named after user research, was rolled out to all 16 (Jen Richardson, "Rolling out simpler ways to navigate GOV.UK's topic pages", Inside GOV.UK, 31 January 2025, [insidegovuk.blog.gov.uk, topic pages](https://insidegovuk.blog.gov.uk/2025/01/31/rolling-out-simpler-ways-to-navigate-gov-uks-topic-pages); the post gives no figures). Its taxonomy principles are the kind of written rule IA4's R1 to R10 are for Perqo, and on one point they meet: GOV.UK tags content to the lowest possible level and never to both a topic and its parent, and IA4 gives every catalogue item exactly one narrower term, and so one category (taxonomy principles, 2019; IA4, Deliverable B, B.1 and B.4, R2). The strain is the one its makers named: the single taxonomy did not replace the curated browse, so in 2021 there were three topic systems, publishers "need to tag their content to multiple topics, and to multiple topic systems", and in 2019 "We know that we need to do more to make it easier for publishers to tag their content accurately" (Dub 2021; Stephens-Manassiev 2019). In a reply on the topic-pages post, dated 4 February 2025, its author wrote that "the topic taxonomy pages don't form part of our current phase of work" (Richardson 2025). Run through IA12's triage tree, the published account shows research in hand (A/B tests, usability testing) and the first shortage the tree asks about: requirements that did not hold still, a merger of the three systems that GDS wrote in 2021 it was in "the early stages of thinking about", and that no post read reports finished. The screens this reading could not see: the services behind their start pages, the publishing tools, and any page drawn only by script.

The structure, as figure 1 draws it. GOV.UK (`home`), with "Popular on GOV.UK; Featured" in its note, two headings of the home page as read on 10 November 2026, holds six parts. Services and information (`section`, 16 topics) holds Benefits (`section`), which holds the Benefits and financial support pages (`stack`, "7 of 8 labels open with the same four words", tone 6) and Manage an existing benefit, payment or claim (`section`); and 15 other topics (`stack`, "Births, deaths, marriages and care to Working, jobs and pensions"). Government activity (`section`, 8 links) holds Departments (`index`), News (`index`) and 6 more links (`stack`, "Guidance and regulation to Get involved"). Search GOV.UK (`index`) carries "more than 4 million searches a month". Topic taxonomy pages (`stack`, tone 6) carry "the taxonomy, one of three topic systems in 2021; reached from content pages' sidebar and breadcrumbs". Step by step pages (`stack`) carry "end to end journeys", and Content pages (`stack`) "more than half a million pages (2021)". Every other box is tone 2.

## 3 · The Monzo app: a consumer app (IA6)

**Scheme and structure.** The Monzo app's screens sit behind a sign-in, and none was seen; this reading rests on Monzo's own help pages and blog, read on 10 November 2026. One of them, "Your guide to the home screen", is Monzo's English-language page for Spain (monzo.com/en-es), and the "Save tab" is named only there; the other two help pages are at the UK address, so the structure below joins two markets' pages and may match neither app exactly. On those pages the first screen, Home, is a summary: "Your accounts, including joint, are at the top of the screen"; "Right below your accounts, the Activity section shows your latest and upcoming payments"; "Your Pots have their own dedicated area, which you can view as a list or with images"; Savings & Investments is a separate section below Pots; and a "Save tab" is where to "Find your Savings Pots" (Monzo, "Your guide to the home screen", [monzo.com, home screen guide](https://monzo.com/en-es/help/using-monzo/help-home-screen); Monzo, "Finding important features in the new Home screen", [monzo.com, finding features](https://monzo.com/help/app-help/finding-features-new-homescreen); both undated, read 10 November 2026; vendor documentation). The scheme, on this unit's reading, is exact at both levels: by account first, then by time inside Activity. The structure is IA6's partial hub (IA6, section 3.2): Home holds routes into each account and each Pot, and the actions live on the account they act on, a "Card" button on each account and three dots for actions such as adding money, statements and scheduled payments (finding features).

**Labelling.** The labels the pages name are the customer's words for money: accounts, Activity, Pots, Card, Savings & Investments. "Pots" is a product name, and on this unit's reading the one label a newcomer has to learn, the branded-term risk IA3 names after Farrell (IA3, section 4.2); its own area on Home and its own word in the "Save tab" keep it in one place. The "+" at the top right is an iconic label, IA3's fifth form (IA3, section 4.1), and the pages tell people what it opens: "open a new account, create a new Pot, apply for a loan, or more" (home screen guide). Whether the icon carries text on the screen, IA3's A4, could not be seen.

**Navigation model.** The pages name a Home, a Save tab and a Help page, and say settings are reached by tapping "your profile photo in the top left corner", where a new option lets the customer edit the Home screen's layout (home screen guide; finding features). The full tab bar is not named on any page read, and this reading does not assert it. What is documented is the model's centre of gravity: almost every route starts on Home, and the customer sets its order. Monzo's post on the redesign says the Home screen summarises "your latest activity across all your Monzo accounts", and that the order and level of detail can be changed, so that people can make the screen look much like the previous version (Max White, "The new and improved Home screen", Monzo blog, 1 November 2023, [monzo.com, new Home screen](https://monzo.com/blog/the-new-and-improved-home-screen); vendor's account).

**Search.** One search covers the app: "You can search for everything in one place, with search available on both the Home and Help pages of the Monzo app"; it finds transactions with filters, "individual payees", and lets people "take certain actions from search results, including applying for other Monzo products"; it tolerates typos, so "Searching for amazn will still pull up all your Amazon transactions" (Monzo, "We're changing how search works in the Monzo app", [monzo.com, search changes](https://monzo.com/help/app-help/unified-search-changes-web), undated, read 10 November 2026; vendor documentation). The same page lists what was removed: exporting search results as a file, and filtering by location. Search here is a route to objects and to actions, which IA3's E1 would ask about: whether browsing still reaches every task without it (IA3, checklist E1). The pages read do not say.

**Objects.** The objects the pages name are the account, the card (reached from a Card button on each account, with its number, CVC and a freeze), the payment, the Pot and the payee. Each has its home: payments in Activity, Pots in their area, the card on its account. On this unit's reading the account is the parent object, and the card and the payments nest inside it, Prater's nesting from IA4 (IA4, section 4.1).

**Lifecycle.** The app serves a customer's month: see what is there, pay, look back. No payment lifecycle is published on the pages read; Activity shows "latest and upcoming payments", so the pages draw two states, upcoming and done, and no more.

**The shaping decision.** The decision that most shapes the app is to let the customer set the first screen. The makers' account of why: "Our app had outgrown its own design and architecture", and "It was way too hard for customers to get the most out of Monzo because they couldn't discover things or easily see their full financial picture"; early ideas were "overwhelming and confusing", which led to customers controlling "what they see on their Home screen" (Max White, "How we built the new Home screen", Monzo blog, 6 November 2023, [monzo.com, how we built it](https://www.monzo.com/blog/how-we-built-the-new-home-screen); vendor's account).

**What it got right, and where it strains.** The post names the core jobs the redesign had to keep in reach: "viewing balances, copying card details and searching for transactions" (White, 6 November 2023). Each of the three sits on Home in the help pages read: the balances at the top, the Card button on each account, the search on Home. That is IA3's B1 met as the makers describe it: the units the system acts on are shown where the person makes the decision they govern (IA3, checklist B1). The evidence the makers report is that they "heard from more than 1,000 Monzo customers" and "run 4 major and 2 minor experiments over the last 7 months", reaching "more than 8 million people" (White, 6 November 2023); no method or result is published, so for an outside reader the claim is a vendor's account of research, and no finding can be checked. The strain shows in the help centre itself: a page exists to tell customers where things are, "Finding important features in the new Home screen", and among them it says Pots moved to their own area under Activity (finding features). A redesign of the first screen moved routes people had learnt, and the makers wrote a page to find them again. The screens this reading could not see: all of them, including the tab bar.

The app map, as figure 2 draws it. Monzo app, the surface, drawn without a type tag as IA6 draws the Perqo app (IA6, B.3), its note "tab bar not fully named in the pages read", leads to Home (`home`, "layout set by the customer"), Save tab (`section`, "Savings Pots") and Help (`section`). Every box is tone 2.

## 4 · The Stripe Dashboard: a business backoffice (IA7)

**Scheme and structure.** The Dashboard sits behind a sign-in and was not seen; Stripe documents it page by page. Its sidebar, as the documentation gives it on 10 November 2026, opens with a first section "where you can access and act on information related to your balances, transactions, customers, and products": Home, Balances, Transactions, Customers and Product catalog. Home "provides analytics and charts about your business performance", and its widgets are added or removed under **Your overview** (Web Dashboard). Then comes a Shortcuts section, "your pinned and most recently visited pages". ## 5 · Airbnb: a marketplace with a purchase lifecycle (IA8, the lifecycle layer)

**Scheme and structure.** Airbnb's public pages are drawn by script, and the home page's text could not be read; this reading rests on Airbnb's newsroom and help centre, read on 10 November 2026. Its makers describe two schemes for finding a stay. The first is geographical: a place typed into the search box. The second, introduced in May 2022, is a topic scheme: "55 categories that organize homes based on their style, location, or proximity to an activity", covering "more than 4 million unique homes" (Airbnb, "The Airbnb 2022 Summer Release: Introducing a new Airbnb for a new world of travel", newsroom, 11 May 2022, [news.airbnb.com, 2022 release](https://news.airbnb.com/2022-summer-release/); vendor's account). In May 2025 the app was rebuilt so that people "book homes, experiences, and services — all in one place", with an Explore tab, "An all-new homepage to discover homes in popular destinations"; a Trips tab, "An advanced travel itinerary with a schedule of your trip"; Messages; and Profile (Airbnb, "Airbnb 2025 Summer Release", newsroom, 13 May 2025, [news.airbnb.com, 2025 release](https://news.airbnb.com/product-releases/airbnb-2025-summer-release/); vendor's account). **Labelling.** The documented labels are short nouns: Explore, Trips, Messages, Profile, and the three kinds of thing booked, homes, experiences and services (2025 release). ## 7 · The comparison across the five

**By level.** The five sort cleanly onto the course's levels, and each confirms one of the level units' central claims on evidence its makers published. GOV.UK, IA5's level, shows that a website's top level is a scheme choice and that a site of this size needs written rules for it: GOV.UK's taxonomy principles are the published counterpart of IA4's R1 to R10 (IA5, section 2; IA4, Deliverable B). The Monzo app, IA6's level, shows the first screen as the place where the units the system acts on must sit (IA6, section 3.5; IA3, B1). The Stripe Dashboard, IA7's level, shows the backoffice organised by object, a list and a detail per object type (IA7, sections 4.2 and 4.3); where IA7 gave Perqo Admin a find field on each list, Stripe adds a search across every object type (IA7, section 4.5). Airbnb and Shopify, IA8's level, show the two halves of a system map: a lifecycle that waits on a second actor, and objects shared across surfaces.

**By scheme and structure.** Four of the five organise their first level by an exact property: Monzo by account, Stripe and Shopify by object type, Airbnb by place. Only GOV.UK, the one whose content is documents, organises by topic, and it is the one whose makers report the most strain in keeping the scheme. That fits IA4's working rule: an exact scheme when the person knows the property, an ambiguous one with a budget for its costs when they come with a need (IA4, section 1.2). GOV.UK's costs are the three topic systems; Airbnb paid for its ambiguous second scheme with a machine-learning classifier and a curation team.

**By search.** Every one of the five has search, and the five use it differently. GOV.UK's is a supplemental route over half a million pages, measured by clickthrough, refinement and exit; Monzo's finds transactions and payees and acts on them; Stripe's is a query language over the object model, with identifiers and addresses that can be shared; Airbnb's was the place box its makers argued in 2022 was not enough, and since September 2026 a bar that takes the guest's own words; Shopify's is split by surface, with categories in the admin and fields in the point-of-sale app. None of the pages read shows search standing in for a weak menu, Katz and Byrne's warning (IA3, section 5.1); none shows the opposite either. That question needs logs the makers have not published.

**By the shaping decision.** In each product one decision explains most of the rest, and in four of the five it is a decision about where the structure's truth lives: in the tags (GOV.UK), in the customer's arrangement (Monzo), in the API's model (Stripe), in the admin (Shopify). Airbnb's is a decision about a second way in. That is IA12's backbone in other words: objects, roles, top-level structure, addresses and the shared vocabulary are the parts expensive to change once built (IA12, section 5), and each shaping decision above fixes one of them.

## Deliverable · C · The lessons for Perqo, and DL-020

*Face of this artefact: the five lessons in full and one proposed decision log entry in IA12's template (IA12, Deliverable D, D.1); fidelity F2. Decision: which of the readings change a filed Perqo artefact, and how the change is proposed. Readers: the IA designer, support, the content team, customer success. Leaves out: the readings themselves (Deliverable A). Owner: the IA designer; version 1, dated 10 November 2026; DL-020 proposed; Perqo is fictional.*

**From GOV.UK: one taxonomy, and its rules written down.** GOV.UK's makers documented what happens when a site keeps several classification systems: duplication, confusion, and publishers tagging "to multiple topics, and to multiple topic systems" (Dub 2021). Perqo has one: IA4's benefits taxonomy serves the app's Benefits, the card offers, the Help centre's search synonyms and Admin's policy editor (IA4, section 1.2 and Deliverable B). The lesson is to keep it one, and the reading supplies a check: GOV.UK's principles tag at the lowest level and never to a topic and its parent, and IA4 gives every catalogue item exactly one narrower term (taxonomy principles, 2019; IA4, B.1 and B.4, R2). IA4's rule is written for catalogue items, so for a Perqo article tagged to both Health and Fitness a reviewer has GOV.UK's principle as a default at C0 and no filed Perqo rule. No filed artefact changes.

**From GOV.UK: the onboarding journey as a step by step.** GOV.UK's step by step is for a journey with "a specific start and end point" that runs through several pages, in an order (Step by step navigation, read 10 November 2026). Perqo's client onboarding is such a journey: IA8 fixed its order, "client and account owner, admin users, groups and policies, budgets, invitations, joining" (IA8, "Perqo facts set by this unit", for section 3.2), and IA10 gave each role a training path through the Help centre's articles (IA10, section 2.5). Drawn as a step by step, the journey shows its gaps at once: of its six steps, two have articles (groups and policies, H-1.2.1 to H-1.2.3; budgets, H-1.1.1 and H-1.1.2), one has a page on perqo.com (joining, Get the Perqo app, S-6), and three have none in IA5's inventory (step 1, which Perqo's own staff do with the client; adding admin users, part of the account owner's work, whose missing article is the open fault IA10 logged; inviting employees) (IA5, Deliverable A, A.3; IA10, section 2.5). That is IA8's gap-finding (IA8, section 1) done by a public pattern. The change adds a page to a filed artefact, so it is proposed below. The pattern's own page says "More user research is needed", so the evidence is design-system guidance, practice, and the proposal is held at C0.

**From Monzo: no change, and the trigger kept.** Monzo's makers name balances, card details and transaction search as the core jobs, and put all three on Home (White, 6 November 2023). The Perqo app shows the four category balances on Home and the card on its own tab, and has no search (IA6, Deliverable B; IA3, checklist E1). IA3 set when that is revisited: "when the Help centre log (E2) shows employees searching for things the app holds" (IA3, Deliverable). Monzo's choice is a competitor's screen at C0 and does not move that trigger.

**From Stripe: the shape of a fallback, and the mapping rule.** IA7 gave Admin no search across objects and a find field on each list, register row AR-34, with "a global search, role-scoped" as what changes if its usability test fails (IA7, "What IA8 and later units take"; IA12, Deliverable B, AR-34). Stripe's documented search is a worked form of that fallback: typed by object, filtered by field, jumping to an identifier, its terms in the address (Dashboard search). It does not raise AR-34's rung. And Stripe's status table shows the rule to keep if a Perqo surface ever summarises a lifecycle: the summary is a label, and the label and its mapping go into the controlled vocabulary before any screen uses them (IA2, section 4, "Rename through the vocabulary"). Perqo's claim states carry the same four words in Admin and the app today (IA2, section 5.3), so nothing changes.

**From Airbnb: an outcome for a waiting state.** Airbnb's Pending ends: no answer in 24 hours, and the request is Expired and refunded (pending status article). Perqo's claim over the limit waits in Approvals, and after five working days is marked Overdue, which "is not a state" (IA7, section 5.2, EX-3). The two are different decisions with different costs: an Expired claim would need a new end state, a rule for who decides it, and words for the employee, which is a rule change routed to the IA review (IA9, D.1). Airbnb published no evidence that expiry serves guests better than waiting, so the comparison names the alternative and EX-3 stands.

**From Shopify: badges stay out of lifecycles; defaults become rules.** Shopify's order statuses page keeps Expiring, which it calls a badge, and Unpaid, a grouping, apart from its true statuses (order statuses). That is IA7's decision on Overdue, met in a published artefact. And Shopify's channel default, every existing product available on a new channel, is a rule set in the admin and felt on every channel, written in a help article's prose (manage channels). Perqo writes such rules as trees and tables (IA8, D.3); the lesson confirms that form for any new cross-surface default. No filed artefact changes.

## What later units take

**DD-IA2** writes the measurement model and the business case for IA work. From this unit it takes the makers' published numbers as examples of how a team reports a structural change, each with what it leaves out: GOV.UK's search figures (14% more clickthrough, 11% fewer refined searches, 8% fewer exits) with no intervals; GOV.UK's list-over-grid result with no figures; Monzo's research counts with no result. None meets IA11's rule of an interval against a mark set beforehand (IA11, sections 3.4 and 4.4), so a business case may cite them as practice, never as evidence for Perqo. GOV.UK's three search measures (clickthrough from results, refinement, exit from the search page) are not among IA11's M-01 to M-11; the nearest is M-07, the zero-result rate, and DD-IA2 decides whether the three join the model. It also takes Deliverable A's form, a register of published claims with their evidence and what they leave untested, as the way a case records the outside evidence it cites.

**DD-IA3** takes taxonomy and governance to scale, in several languages and markets. From this unit it takes GOV.UK's taxonomy principles (2 to 12 child topics, about 12 at any level, the lowest level, never a topic and its parent, bracketed qualifiers for names used twice) to set beside IA4's R1 to R10; GOV.UK's documented governance costs, tagging hard for publishers and, in 2019, the taxonomy's care split across content support, the navigation team and the publishing workflow team (Stephens-Manassiev 2019); and two localisation observations of 10 November 2026: Stripe's English (UK) documentation writes the API value `cancelled` where its unlocalised pages write `canceled`, an identifier that took a language's spelling, against IA5's rule that identifiers carry no words of any language (IA5, section 5.3); and a web search returned Airbnb's help article on pending reservations at the same number, 2810, on seven country domains (airbnb.com, .ca, .co.in, .jp, .dk, .se and .com.br), the stable-key rule IA5 set for localised pages (only the airbnb.com page was read). One source read for this unit and not among the five also goes to DD-IA3: eBay's developer documentation says "Approximately 20 eBay marketplaces each use a default category tree", that the tree "is also subject to change as categories are added, expanded, consolidated, or removed", and that a client compares its cached `categoryTreeVersion` with eBay's (eBay Developers Program, "Finding categories for a listing", [developer.ebay.com, sell categories](https://www.developer.ebay.com/api-docs/sell/static/metadata/sell-categories.html), read 10 November 2026; platform documentation): a versioned taxonomy per market.

**Any later reading of a real product** takes this unit's method: dated observation of public pages, the makers' own documents, the screens not seen named, the register's form with C0 for Perqo, and the RD- identifiers from RD-16.
