# Information Architecture · briefs

The shared preamble (`{GOAL}` is filled with the course goal) and one brief per unit, word for word from the
generator's units_data.py (S-DEC2, 20 Nov 2026). kit/pack.py reads every `## ` section; nothing above the first is read.

## Preamble

Write one unit of a self-study course in the same long-form report format as the Brand + AI units of the Learning Project: a long-form report in Markdown. Length is whatever the topic needs (2,000–6,000 words); do not pad and do not cut to a target. Reader: a mid-level UX designer, three to six years in product teams, with a working grasp of UX practice and little hands-on user-research experience; reads in order (each unit assumes the ones before it), reads on a phone, checks the citations, and wants genuine depth rather than a primer. This course is written to be handed to other designers; it is the Learning Project's one exception to its single-reader rule (decided 29 Oct 2026). Running example: Perqo, a fictional flexible-benefits platform in the technology space, invented for this course (any resemblance to a real company is unintended), with three surfaces: perqo.com, the sales and product website; Perqo Admin, the backoffice where a client company's HR and finance managers configure budgets, policies, approvals and reports; and the Perqo app, the employee client app for seeing balances and spending benefits; a merchant side exists where a unit needs it. Every Perqo fact is invented by the unit that needs it, labelled '(Perqo, fictional)' on its first use in the unit, kept consistent with the Perqo facts in the filed units, and never used as evidence for a claim about the world. Tool-agnostic: name no diagramming or research tool as required; describe every technique so it can be done in any tool or on paper. Tone: direct, no flattery, no filler, no motivational language, no 'not X but Y' constructions. Course goal: {GOAL} Sourcing standard: every claim that carries a number, a named source, a mechanism or a recommendation is sourced where it is made. Acceptable sources are: the field's standard works cited by author, title, edition and year (Rosenfeld, Morville and Arango, Information Architecture: For the Web and Beyond, 4th ed., 2015; Covert, How to Make Sense of Any Mess, 2014; Spencer, A Practical Guide to Information Architecture, 2nd ed., 2014; Brown, Communicating Design, 2nd ed., 2011; Garrett, The Elements of User Experience, 2nd ed., 2011, and his visual vocabulary of 2002; Arango, Living in Information, 2018; Kalbach, Mapping Experiences, 2nd ed., 2020; Young, Mental Models, 2008; Krug, Don't Make Me Think, Revisited, 2014; Resmini and Rosati, Pervasive Information Architecture, 2011; Hedden, The Accidental Taxonomist, 3rd ed., 2022; Welchman, Managing Chaos, 2015; Halvorson and Rach, Content Strategy for the Web, 2nd ed., 2012; Cooper and others, About Face, 4th ed., 2014; and others of the same standing, each with a page or chapter where a specific claim is drawn); Nielsen Norman Group articles and reports cited with author, title, date and URL; peer-reviewed HCI and information-science research with the venue and year; platform, standards and vendor documentation labelled as such (a research-tool vendor's guidance on sample sizes is vendor guidance, not a study); a practitioner's article or talk only with a named author and date, labelled as practice rather than evidence. A diagram redrawn or adapted from a source credits that source in its caption. No real company's information architecture is asserted beyond what is visible on its public screens or documented by the company itself. No uncited figures; no invented articles, authors, studies, URLs, names or numbers; a claim that cannot be sourced is said to be unsourced and is left out of the deliverable. A unit that reads as advice without evidence is a failed unit. Links are given as links, so the course bibliography can collect them. Figures: this is a visual subject, and every unit carries figures (three to eight) where structure is the content: a sitemap, an object map, a flow, a decision tree, a matrix, a map of surfaces. A figure is declared in the unit as a fenced viz block of one of the kinds kit/viz.py draws at build time (the kinds and their fields are in its docstring; the IA kinds added for this course are listed there), never as a screenshot, an embedded image or hand-written SVG; its labels and numbers are the unit's own content, and its caption says what it shows and, for a redrawn diagram, the source. Every figure uses the course notation defined in unit IA2 once that unit is filed. Where the unit depends on current facts (a live product's screens, a published figure, a tool's documentation), search before writing and date the facts. End with the unit's deliverable, built out in full, not described.

## IA1

Unit IA1, What information architecture is, and where it sits. Cover: the definitions of information architecture in the standard works and what each one includes and leaves out (Rosenfeld, Morville and Arango's four systems of organisation, labelling, navigation and search; Covert's sense-making and the difference between information, content and data; Arango's architecture of information environments; Garrett's structure plane between scope and skeleton); the lines a designer has to hold between IA and navigation design, IA and the user interface, IA and content strategy, IA and the engineering data model, with one worked example per line; why IA exists as a discipline (library science and the first web, the Polar Bear book's history, Wurman's coinage), told briefly; what an IA deliverable is for, which decision it supports, and who reads it; a first look at how IA shows up in a system of three surfaces, using Perqo as the example. Figures: the four systems; Garrett's planes with IA located; the Perqo surfaces and who uses each. Deliverable: a one-page definition sheet a product team can be handed (what IA is, what it is not, the four systems in a sentence each, the five questions an IA answers), and the Perqo surfaces map that later units build on.

## IA2

Unit IA2, The shapes IA takes: the artefact catalogue and the course notation. Cover: the catalogue of IA artefacts and what question each one answers: content inventory and audit; sitemap (structural, not page-by-page); object map and object-relationship map; taxonomy and controlled vocabulary; user flow, task flow and wireflow; decision tree; state diagram; navigation model (global, local, contextual, supplemental); service blueprint; the cross-surface interaction map; for each, who reads it, what fidelity it needs at which stage, and what it must not be mistaken for; the published notations (Garrett's visual vocabulary of 2002; Brown's chapters on site maps and flow charts in Communicating Design) and what they standardise; how to read an artefact someone else made, and the five faults to look for first; how to edit one without breaking what others rely on; the single notation this course uses from here on, derived from Garrett and Brown and reduced to what the course's figure kinds can draw. Figures: one small example of each artefact family in the course notation; the legend itself. Deliverable: the course notation sheet (every symbol, line and label rule, with a one-line meaning each) and the artefact decision table (question asked → artefact → fidelity → reader → unit of this course that builds it).

## IA3

Unit IA3, How people find and understand things. Cover: information foraging and information scent (Pirolli and Card, 1999, and the Nielsen Norman Group's articles that apply it), with what a strong and a weak scent look like in a label and a link; mental models as Indi Young and Norman use the term, and the gap between a user's model and a system's model; how people categorise (basic-level categories, prototype effects, and the Polar Bear book's organisation challenges: ambiguity, heterogeneity, differences in perspective, internal politics) and what that means for a navigation scheme; labelling: the forms labels take, why vocabulary mismatch happens, and the evidence on consistency and specificity; search versus browse behaviour and what search logs reveal about failed structures; findability (Morville) and Krug's don't-make-me-think argument, with the limits of each. Figures: the foraging loop; a scent comparison of two labels for the same page; the user-model versus system-model gap; a categorisation example from Perqo's benefits. Deliverable: the findability and labelling checklist (every item sourced to the research it rests on), applied item by item to a Perqo app navigation that the unit invents as the as-is (Perqo, fictional), with the verdict and the fix for each failing item. The as-is navigation is invented to show the faults the checklist catches; it is the structure unit IA6 replaces, and IA6 keeps the labels the checklist passes.

## IA4

Unit IA4, Organisation systems: schemes, structures, objects, taxonomies. Cover: organisation schemes (exact: alphabetical, chronological, geographical; ambiguous: topic, task, audience, metaphor, hybrid) and when each is right, with the Polar Bear book as the spine; organisation structures (hierarchy, its breadth-versus-depth trade-off and the evidence on it; polyhierarchy; database and faceted models; hypertext) and how real systems combine them; faceted classification and controlled vocabularies (Hedden; Rosenfeld, Morville and Arango on metadata and thesauri) and the difference between a taxonomy and a folksonomy; object-oriented UX (Sophia Prater's object mapping, cited to her published articles) as a way to find the nouns before the pages, and where it stops; how an organisation system shows up in navigation, filters, search and URLs. Figures: the scheme catalogue as a tree; breadth versus depth; a facet grid; the Perqo object map. Deliverable: the Perqo object map (objects, attributes, relationships, the calls to action on each object, Perqo facts invented and labelled) and a faceted taxonomy for the Perqo benefits catalogue (facets, values, the rules for adding a value), built in full. The object map covers the whole product, every surface, at the level of objects, attributes and relationships; IA7 extends it for Perqo Admin and renames nothing in it.

## IA5

Unit IA5, Level 1: the sales and product website. Cover: how to run a content inventory and audit (Halvorson and Rach; Spencer) and what to record per item; audience-based, task-based and topic-based structures for a marketing site and the case for each with a B2B software product; page types versus pages, and templates as the unit of IA on a site; depth, breadth and the home-page and landing-page problem; the constraints other functions bring (search-engine structure, campaign landing pages, localisation, the pricing page, the sign-up and log-in boundary between the site and the product) and how the sitemap records them; how a sitemap is read by marketing, by engineering and by a content writer, and the fidelity each needs. Figures: the inventory structure; three candidate structures for perqo.com compared; the chosen sitemap; the template catalogue. Deliverable: the perqo.com content inventory (every page, with type, audience, owner, status; Perqo facts invented and labelled), the sitemap in the course notation, and the page-template catalogue (each template, its purpose, its required and optional modules).

## IA6

Unit IA6, Level 2: the client app. Cover: task analysis as the basis of an app's IA (tasks, frequency, sequence, and the evidence for organising by task rather than by feature); the app map as distinct from a sitemap (screens, states and modals, with the entry points); mobile navigation patterns (tab bar, hub-and-spoke, hierarchical drill-down, and the Nielsen Norman Group's evidence on each, with hamburger menus treated on the evidence); progressive disclosure, empty states, error states and permissions as IA decisions; how a user flow becomes a wireflow and when each is the right artefact; how an app's IA stays consistent with the website's labels and the backoffice's objects (the Perqo object map from IA4). Figures: the task inventory ranked; the app map; the three wireflows; the navigation-pattern comparison. Deliverable: the Perqo app task inventory (tasks, frequency, actor, surface), the app map in the course notation, and three core flows as wireflows: see a balance, spend a benefit, submit a reimbursement claim (Perqo facts invented and labelled). The app map is the to-be for the as-is navigation IA3 invented and checked: every label IA3's checklist passed is kept, every fix IA3 named is applied, and the unit says where it departs from IA3 and why.

## IA7

Unit IA7, Level 3: the backoffice. Cover: what makes backoffice and enterprise IA different (many entity types, roles, permissions, volumes, workflows, and users who are paid to be there; Cooper and others on sovereign posture); object-relationship mapping for a system with companies, employees, budgets, policies, benefits, claims and approvals; roles and permissions as an IA layer (what a role can see, do and reach) and the role-by-section matrix; enterprise navigation (global, local, contextual; list, detail, create and edit patterns; bulk actions; search inside the system) and why a backoffice IA is organised around objects and work queues rather than pages; workflows and states (a state diagram per object that has a lifecycle; approvals, exceptions, audit trails); the boundary between the IA and the engineering data model, and how the two are kept aligned without being the same thing (Evans's ubiquitous language as the shared vocabulary). Figures: the object-relationship map; the role matrix; the navigation model; the claim-approval state diagram; a list-detail pattern. Deliverable: the Perqo Admin IA: the object-relationship map, the role-by-section matrix, the navigation model in the course notation, and the claim-approval state diagram, all with Perqo facts invented and labelled. The object-relationship map extends IA4's Perqo object map for Admin (roles, volumes, lifecycles, work queues) and renames none of IA4's objects, attributes or relationships.

## IA8

Unit IA8, Level 4: the system map. Cover: why a system of several surfaces, several actors and several lifecycles needs a map above the surface maps, and what that map is for (shared understanding, gap-finding, scope decisions); pervasive and cross-channel IA (Resmini and Rosati's heuristics) and the service blueprint (Shostack's origin; Kalbach; the Nielsen Norman Group's definition) as the two published forms the map draws on; the lifecycles that run through Perqo and their stages: the client company's purchase lifecycle (evaluate, trial, contract, onboard, run, renew or churn) and the employee's benefit lifecycle (enrol, learn, decide, spend, claim, review), with the published models of the purchase decision journey named and their evidence labelled; decision trees for the rules that cut across surfaces (eligibility, approval routing, spend limits) and how to draw one so engineering can implement it and sales can explain it; layering: how the product map, the decision trees and the lifecycles are drawn as one map that can still be read one layer at a time, and what to leave out; how the map stays current. Figures: the lifecycle swimlanes; the three decision trees; the product map across surfaces; the layered interaction map, drawn as the layers and then as the whole. Deliverable: the Perqo interaction map built in full in the course notation: the surface layer, the actor and lifecycle layer, the decision-tree layer, the connections between them, and a reading guide of one paragraph per layer (Perqo facts invented and labelled, consistent with IA4 to IA7).

## IA9

Unit IA9, IA in the product development lifecycle. Cover: where IA work sits in the published models of the design and product process (the Double Diamond; Garrett's planes in time; discovery, definition, delivery) and what is wrong with treating it as a phase; IA in an agile delivery (the backbone drawn before stories are cut, the IA kept alongside the backlog, IA debt and how it accrues; evidence and named practice, labelled); what an IA artefact needs to be done at each stage (the definition of done per artefact from IA2's catalogue), and how fidelity rises; versioning and change: who may change the IA, how a change is proposed, recorded and communicated, and the decision log; IA as a living document and the maintenance cadence (what to review, how often, triggered by what); the signals that the IA is failing in production (search logs, support tickets, analytics paths) and the loop forward to the validation methods IA11 covers. Figures: the process models with IA located; the artefact-by-stage grid; the change-control flow; the maintenance cadence. Deliverable: the IA workflow across one Perqo product cycle (stage, IA activity, artefact, owner, consulted, informed), the definition of done for each artefact in the catalogue, and the maintenance cadence (review, trigger, owner).

## IA10

Unit IA10, Touchpoints: what each part of the business needs from the IA. Cover: for each function that touches the IA, the question it actually asks of it, the artefact that answers it, the format and fidelity it can read, and the meeting or document where the exchange happens: product management (scope, roadmap, requirements, what a change touches); engineering (data model, routing, permissions, API and event naming, the shared vocabulary); content, marketing and localisation (labels, templates, search-engine structure); sales and pre-sales (demo paths, the story of the system, proposals and tenders); customer success and onboarding (the client's configuration, training paths); support (ticket taxonomy, the map of where things go wrong); clients themselves (their vocabulary, validation, co-creation, and the risk of building their org chart into the product); legal, security and compliance (data categories, consent, audit); leadership (the one-page map); the failure modes of each touchpoint and how the IA records a disagreement; the published practice on communicating design to each audience (Brown, Communicating Design; Brown, Designing Together) labelled as practice. Figures: the touchpoint matrix; the artefact-to-audience map; one translation example (the same object map read by engineering and by sales). Deliverable: the Perqo touchpoint matrix (function, question, artefact, fidelity, format, meeting, owner, cadence) and three one-page IA briefs written in full for product management, engineering and sales, each built from the same Perqo IA (IA4 to IA8) and saying only what that audience needs.

## IA11

Unit IA11, Validating an IA: evidence, methods, numbers. Cover: the methods that test an IA and what each one does and does not prove: open, closed and hybrid card sorting (Spencer's Card Sorting; the Nielsen Norman Group's guidance), tree testing, first-click testing, search-log analysis (Rosenfeld's Search Analytics for Your Site), analytics path and funnel review, and task-based usability testing of a built navigation; sample sizes and reliability (the published studies on card-sort participant numbers and tree-test sample sizes, cited to the study, with vendor guidance labelled as vendor guidance); the metrics (success, directness, time, agreement) and how to read them; how to run each method cheaply and remotely without naming a tool as required; how to turn results into IA changes and when a result is not strong enough to act on; what to do when there is no access to users (the bridge to IA12). Figures: the method-selection table; a card-sort result as a dendrogram or similarity matrix; a tree-test result; the confidence ladder. Deliverable: a validation plan for the Perqo app IA from IA6 (question, method, participants, what decides, cost), a tree-test script in full (tasks, correct nodes, instructions), a closed card-sort protocol in full (cards, categories, instructions), and the analysis template for each.

## IA12

Unit IA12, When the inputs are missing: building the backbone anyway. Cover: the four shortages a designer meets (no or faulty research; absent, uncooperative or conflicting stakeholders; no content or a content set that cannot be trusted; contradictory or shifting requirements) and the symptoms of each; proxy evidence and where to find it (support tickets, search logs, sales-call notes, analytics, competitors' public screens, the existing system's own structure, the data model, documentation) with the limits of each labelled; the IA as a set of hypotheses: an assumption register that records every structural decision with its evidence, confidence and the test that would settle it (Bland and Osterwalder's assumption mapping, cited); stakeholder mapping and handling (power and interest; the published practice on interviewing and on working with disagreement, Brown's Designing Together; Hall's Just Enough Research), including what to do when the stakeholder who owns the answer will not give it; the minimum viable IA: what must be fixed early because it is expensive to change (objects, roles, top-level structure, URLs, the shared vocabulary) and what can stay open; escalation, the written decision log, and how to make the risk visible without blocking the team; how the backbone is kept revisable once the evidence arrives (the loop to IA9 and IA11). Figures: the triage decision tree; the evidence ladder; the stakeholder map; the register structure. Deliverable: the incomplete-inputs playbook built in full: the triage decision tree, the assumption register filled for Perqo Admin (every structural decision of IA7 with evidence, confidence, test, owner), the Perqo stakeholder map, and the one-page decision log template with three entries filled.

## DD-IA1

Deep dive DD-IA1, Five information architectures read closely. Written last, search on, every observation dated. Cover: five real, publicly observable products chosen to span the course's four levels (a content-rich public site; a consumer app; a business backoffice or developer console; a marketplace or commerce system with a purchase lifecycle; one that spans several surfaces), each read only from its public screens and from what its makers have published about its structure (design-system documentation, engineering or design blog posts, public talks, cited); for each: the organisation scheme and structure, the labelling, the navigation model, the search, the objects, the lifecycle it serves, and the one decision that most shapes it; what each got right on the evidence and where the public structure shows strain; the comparison across the five. Figures: each product's IA redrawn in the course notation from its public screens, with the date observed; the comparison. Deliverable: the five readings and the comparison table (product, level, scheme, structure, navigation model, search, the shaping decision, the lesson for Perqo), nothing asserted about any product beyond what was observed or published.

## DD-IA2

Deep dive DD-IA2, What IA costs and returns. Written last, search on, every figure dated. Cover: the published evidence on findability failure and its cost (usability studies with task-success rates, search-versus-browse figures, the cost of support contacts and of failed self-service, redesign case studies with before-and-after numbers), each with its source, sample, date and label (peer-reviewed, consultancy report, vendor, anecdote); what cannot be measured and the claims the field makes without evidence, named as such; the measurement model for an IA (leading indicators: tree-test success, first-click accuracy; lagging: task success, search exits, support tickets by category, time to find); how to build a business case for IA work in a product company (the cost of change by layer, the cost of not changing, the decision the case supports), and the limits of the exercise. Figures: the evidence table as a compare; the cost-by-layer figure; the measurement model. Deliverable: the IA measurement model (metric, source of data, baseline method, target, cadence) and a business-case template with sourced ranges and labels, filled once for Perqo Admin with every figure traced to its source or marked as the unit's own assumption.

## DD-IA3

Deep dive DD-IA3, Taxonomy and governance at scale. Written last. Cover: what an enterprise taxonomy is and how it differs from a navigation hierarchy (Hedden; Rosenfeld, Morville and Arango on metadata, controlled vocabularies and thesauri; the published standards for thesauri and knowledge organisation systems named as standards); metadata and the shared vocabulary across product, content, data and engineering (Evans's ubiquitous language; Welchman's digital governance by design); ownership: who owns the IA, the taxonomy and each label, and the governance models Welchman describes; change control for a taxonomy (proposal, impact, approval, migration, communication) and for the IA; localisation and the labels that do not survive translation; the failure modes at scale (drift, duplication, the org chart leaking into the structure, orphan categories) and how to detect them; the documented practice of organisations that publish their taxonomy or governance, verified against those publications. Figures: the taxonomy-versus-navigation figure; the governance model; the change-control flow; the drift-detection cadence. Deliverable: the Perqo governance model (roles, decisions, forums, cadence), the taxonomy change-request process in full, and a controlled-vocabulary starter for Perqo (the twenty terms every surface must share, each with its definition, its synonyms to avoid and its owner), Perqo facts invented and labelled.
