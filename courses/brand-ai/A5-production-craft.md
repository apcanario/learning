## 1 · Filming inside a session without breaking it

### What the filed units already fix

The session comes first, and the camera is a guest in it. A3 §1 sets the rules this section works inside: footage is shot inside the session, captured once and filed once; "nothing is filmed twice"; a miss is never re-staged, because a re-filmed scene "replaces what happened with a staged version of it"; the only footage shot on purpose outside a session is the episode's own frame (opening, syllabus on screen, bridges, ending), in the fortnight's single one-hour filming window (A3 §1, §6). A4 adds what the camera may not show: where he lives or starts his runs, and other people without their agreement (A4, disclosure policy clause 2). A3 §6 budgets capture and the weekly journal entry together at half an hour a week, an estimate the hours log tests. So the production question is narrow: what kit and what shot list get usable footage from a run or a karate session at almost no cost to the session itself.

### One phone, one tripod, one lav

**The phone on a tripod.** One static camera set up before the warm-up and left alone. It does three jobs at once: the wide shots of the session, the to-camera take afterwards, and the source for the vertical Short. Two YouTube rules shape how it is set:

- *Frame rate.* YouTube's encoding guidance says "Content should be encoded and uploaded in the same frame rate it was recorded" ([YouTube Help, Recommended upload encoding settings, answer 1722171](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/1722171?hl=en)). Mixing frame rates across a season's clips forces a conversion somewhere (my inference: an edit exports at one rate). His rule: one frame rate for every clip of the season, chosen once and written in the season folder.
- *Resolution, for the Short.* YouTube counts a video as a Short only in a square or vertical frame (A3 §1, from YouTube Help 15424877), and A3 §1 keeps the subject in the centre third of a horizontal frame so a vertical crop holds; B5's step 6 checks that crop on every Short. The arithmetic says what resolution that needs. A full-height 9:16 crop from a 1920 × 1080 frame is 607.5 pixels wide (1080 × 9 ÷ 16), about 32% of the frame's width: the centre third, as A3 has it. That crop has to be enlarged about 1.78 times to fill a 1080 × 1920 vertical frame. The same crop from a 3840 × 2160 frame is 1,215 × 2,160 pixels, larger than 1080 × 1920, so it fills the vertical frame without enlargement (my arithmetic). His rule: record at 3840 × 2160 when the phone allows it. Whether his phone holds that resolution for a full session without overheating or filling its storage was not checked.

**One lavalier microphone.** The one piece of kit bought for sound, for the reasons §2 sets out. Two manufacturer statements give the placement and the reason for it:

- *Distance.* Sennheiser's lavalier manual instructs, for its ME 2 and ME 4: "Attach the microphone to your clothing, keeping a distance of 25 cm (10") from your mouth" ([Sennheiser, Lavalier microphones, instruction manual v1.1, 05/2026](https://docs.cloud.sennheiser.com/en-us/lavalier-mics/lavalier-mics_instruction_manual_05_2026_en.pdf)). That is one manufacturer's instruction for its own products; it is used here as a placement default.
- *Why distance matters.* Audio-Technica states the inverse-square rule: "the volume of the sound will drop by 6 dB every time the distance is doubled from the source", and a microphone "placed too far away from the source may pick up too much of the room" ([Audio-Technica, tips for placing microphones](https://www.audio-technica.com/en-us/support/audio-solutions-question-week-tips-placing-microphones-get-best-possible-sound), undated). A lav at 25 cm against the phone's own microphone on a tripod 3 m away is a ratio of 12 in distance, about 21.6 dB more direct sound at the lav by that rule (20 × log10(300 ÷ 25); my arithmetic, free-field approximation, which a room or a dojo will not match exactly). The room's noise and echo reach both microphones; only the voice gets the 21.6 dB.

**Wind, on runs.** The same Sennheiser manual tells the user of the models it supplies with a windshield (ME 4, MKE Essential, MKE 2, MKE 40) to "Slide the windshield over the microphone head", and says its MZC 1-2 cap for the MKE 1 "attenuares [sic] wind noise by approx. 15 dB" ([Sennheiser, 05/2026](https://docs.cloud.sennheiser.com/en-us/lavalier-mics/lavalier-mics_instruction_manual_05_2026_en.pdf)). That figure is for one product, and the manual does not say when either is needed. That a lav on a run needs a windshield is my inference from that figure; how much a given shield removes depends on the shield.

**No lav in sparring.** In kumite the lav is struck, pulled and rubbed against the gi. His rule: the lav goes on for to-camera pieces and the take, and comes off for sparring, where the phone's own microphone records the room. No source was found on lavalier damage or noise in contact sport; the rule is a precaution.

### What "without breaking it" means in practice

His rules, each from the filed units named:

1. **Set up before the warm-up, in under two minutes, and do not touch the camera again until the session ends.** The session plan is never changed for a shot. Basis: A3 §6, capture "is in the session, never a separate job"; the two-minute ceiling is his convention.
2. **No second take of anything.** If a shot is missed, it is missed. Basis: A3 §1, nothing filmed twice; A4 record rule 4, the miss is always in.
3. **The frame shows no one who has not agreed, and nothing that locates his home or his start point.** Training partners are asked in advance (B3's worked example for template 8 drafts that message; image rights are A8's). Basis: A4, disclosure policy clause 2.
4. **The take is filmed.** A3 §1 records a dictated take before leaving the place the session happened. With the tripod already standing and the lav already on, the take is recorded to camera: one capture that is both the voice note B5's step 1 transcribes and A-roll the edit can use. Basis: A3 §1 (capture once, file once); B5 step 1 (the take's transcript). This is his rule; it adds the camera to a capture A3 already makes.

The brief asks for six shots. A3's checklist B already names what the footage covers: "the start, the hard part, anything that goes wrong, the finish, the watch" (A3, Deliverable 1, B). The list below turns that into six shots, each filmed only if the session produces it. The list is his convention; each line names its basis.

## 2 · Audio: what the evidence says viewers do with bad sound

### The claim as the brief gives it, and what holds

The brief calls audio "the thing viewers forgive least". That is a comparison, and the evidence found does not support the comparison. It does support a narrower claim: poor audio lowers how people judge both the speaker and what is said, with the content held identical.

**The study.** Eryn Newman and Norbert Schwarz, "Good Sound, Good Research: How Audio Quality Influences Perceptions of the Research and Researcher", *Science Communication* 40(2), 246–257, 2018, doi:10.1177/1075547018759345 ([full text, copy hosted by Gwern.net](https://www.Gwern.net/doc/design/visualization/2018-newman.pdf)).

- **Experiment 1.** 97 Amazon Mechanical Turk workers each heard two conference talks from YouTube (physics and engineering), cut to 2–3 minutes, one with good audio and one with poor, counterbalanced. The poor version was the same recording through an echo filter that reduced clarity. Talks with good audio were rated higher overall on a five-point scale, mean difference 0.60 (95% CI 0.41–0.79). By measure: how good the talk was, 0.93; how much they liked the speaker, 0.69; how important the research was, 0.51; how intelligent the speaker seemed, 0.33.
- **Experiment 2.** 99 workers heard two NPR *Science Friday* interviews, one made to sound like a bad phone line. Good audio was again rated higher, mean difference 0.19 (95% CI 0.03–0.36).
- **Comprehension.** Restricted to the 64% of participants in experiment 1 who correctly identified both talks' content, the pattern held, so the effect was not only people failing to understand.
- **The authors' summary.** "Despite identical content, people evaluated the research and researcher less favorably when the audio quality was low." Newman, in the university's release: "it's not just about who you are and what you are saying, it's about how your work is presented" ([ScienceDaily, Australian National University release, 13 Apr 2018](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2018/04/180413093842.htm)).

**What it does not show.** The listeners were online workers rating short scientific talks with audio degraded on purpose; nobody chose to watch, and nobody could leave. The study did not degrade the picture, so it says nothing about whether viewers forgive bad video more than bad audio. It also measures judgement, not viewing time.

**The one comparison found points the other way, under its own conditions.** A US government laboratory report, McFarland and colleagues, *Relating Audio and Video Quality Using CIF Video* (NTIA Technical Memorandum TM-10-472, Institute for Telecommunication Sciences, September 2010; [PDF](https://its.ntia.gov/publications/download/10-472.pdf)), had viewers rate clips with coded audio and video impairments. Video differences explained 78% of the variance in the overall rating and audio 10%; for speech-only clips, 90% and 3%. The authors add that the video impairments covered a much wider quality range than the audio ones, that single-talker audio is "quite easy to code" so the voice impairments were "nearly identical", and that "it is unclear whether this indicates underlying truth, or was merely a consequence of the unbalanced test design"; the result "will not necessarily be valid for other choices of audiovisual material". That is codec damage at low resolution in 2010, a long way from an echoing dojo. It is cited here because it is the only direct comparison of the two found, and it does not support the brief.

> **Where sources disagree.** *The brief:* audio is what viewers forgive least. *Newman and Schwarz (2018):* poor audio lowers judgements of the talk and the speaker with content held identical; they did not test picture quality. *McFarland et al. (NTIA, 2010):* with both degraded, picture explained far more of the overall rating, by the authors' own account possibly because of how the impairments were chosen. *This unit:* the comparison is unsourced and is left out; the cost of bad audio is sourced and is the reason for the lav.

### Two practical reasons that do not depend on the comparison

1. **Automatic captions get worse with noise.** YouTube says automatic captions "might misrepresent the spoken content due to mispronunciations, accents, dialects or background noise", that "You should always review automatic captions", and lists Portuguese among the supported languages ([YouTube Help, Use automatic captioning, answer 6373554](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/6373554?hl=en-GB)). The same holds, by my inference, for the transcripts the edit and B5's chain run on: B5 step 1 files a take from its transcript, and the text-based assembly in §5 cuts the episode from one. A noisy take costs correction time at every step after it.
2. **The fix is cheap and physical.** The 21.6 dB from §1's distance arithmetic is gained by where a microphone sits, before any software touches it.

### Levels and export

- **Export format.** YouTube recommends AAC-LC (or Opus) audio, stereo, at a 48 kHz sample rate and 384 kbps for stereo ([YouTube Help 1722171](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/1722171?hl=en)). His rule: record at 48 kHz from the start so nothing is resampled.
- **Loudness.** The Audio Engineering Society's TD1008 (2021) recommends a distribution loudness of −18 LUFS for "speech-only and 'Assorted' content", as reported by the mastering engineer Ian Shepherd ([Production Advice, 1 Oct 2021](https://productionadvice.co.uk/td1008/)); the AES document itself was not opened. Shepherd's report says TD1008 is written for online radio and streaming services, YouTube among them; it gives distribution levels, and using −18 LUFS as the mix target for an episode is my transfer. No YouTube page opened for this unit states how YouTube adjusts loudness on playback, so that is left out.

## 3 · CTR and percentage viewed: what the heuristics are, and what they mean for structure

### The figures the brief gives, checked at source

**CTR 4–6%.** Neither vendor page found for the brief gives 4–6%.

- AIR Media-Tech's page on a good YouTube CTR (Nataliia Prokopchuk, last updated 5 November 2024) says "most YouTube creators aim for a CTR between 2% and 10%", that below 2% "might mean you need to revisit your visuals", and closes "Aim for that sweet spot of 4% to 10%" ([AIR Media-Tech](https://air.io/en/trending/what-is-a-good-ctr-on-youtube)). It cites no dataset for any of these figures.
- vidIQ's CTR page (updated 27 May 2026) says "A good CTR typically falls between 2–10%", then gives its own ranges by channel size, from 8–20% under 1,000 subscribers down to 1.5–4% at a million and over, described as "patterns we see across vidIQ's user base, generally", with no sample or method ([vidIQ, Getting a good click-through rate](https://vidiq.com/blog/post/6-tips-good-CTR-youtube)).
- YouTube's own statement is the source both vendors echo: "Half of all channels and videos on YouTube have an impressions CTR that can range between 2% and 10%", with a wider range for new videos and those under 100 views; YouTube publishes no target ([YouTube Help, Impressions and click-through rate, answer 7628154](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/7628154?hl=en-GB); A6 §1.2; A9 §2).

A6 §1.2 and A9 §2 already set the course's position: the "4–6% is good" target has no dataset behind it and is not used (Audit 1 erratum). This unit confirms that at source and follows it. vidIQ's 8–20% for small channels is a vendor pattern without a sample, against YouTube's own warning that small and new videos vary more; it enters no decision here. CTR stays where A9 put it: context, and one input to the "change format" verdict (below 2% on three or more of a quarter's uploads; A9, Deliverable 2).

**APV 30–45% on 15–30 minutes.** That figure is on vidIQ's page, labelled as its own guidance: "The ranges below are vidIQ guidance for target bands", with 50–70% under 5 minutes, 40–55% at 5–15, 30–45% at 15–30 and 25–35% above 30 ([vidIQ, YouTube Average View Duration, updated and reviewed 21 Jul 2026](https://vidiq.com/blog/post/average-view-duration/); A9 §2 and row 3). No sample stands behind the bands; the only measured figure on the page is vidIQ's own channel, whose long-form videos "averaged 3 minutes 11 seconds of AVD and 30.3% viewed across 16.5 million views" from June 2025 to June 2026. AIR's CTR page gives no retention benchmark.

The brief's band is for the wrong length. A3 §2 sets the episode at about 10 minutes and 12 at most, which falls in vidIQ's 5–15 minute band, 40–55%. At 12 minutes that is 4.8 to 6.6 minutes of average view duration (A9 §6's arithmetic). The 30–45% band would apply only to an episode of 15 minutes or more, which A2's hours and A3's calendar do not contain.

### What the two numbers mean, read together

CTR is earned by the title and thumbnail, before anyone has seen a frame. Percentage viewed is earned by the episode. YouTube joins them in one sentence: "You can tell if your thumbnail is clickbait if it's getting high CTR but low average view duration and lower than expected Impressions" ([YouTube Help 7628154](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/7628154?hl=en-GB)); its Test & Compare tool picks the winning title or thumbnail on watch time over CTR (A6 §1.2, from YouTube Help 16391400). The package can raise the first number by promising more; the episode then has to keep the promise or the second number falls and the impressions with it. The structure below is built so the package and the first minute say the same thing.

### What that means for structure

**The hook: the first 30 seconds make the title true.** YouTube's Intro metric is the share still watching after the first 30 seconds, and its advice for a weak intro is to "Modify the first 30 seconds of your video and experiment with different styles" and to "Consider changing your video thumbnail and title to better reflect your video content" ([YouTube Help, Measure key moments for audience retention, answer 9314415](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/9314415)). vidIQ's guidance is to "Confirm the title and thumbnail promise immediately, show the payoff, and remove setup the viewer does not need" ([vidIQ, 21 Jul 2026](https://vidiq.com/blog/post/average-view-duration/)). A6's playbook rule 2, B3 template 4 and B5 step 4 already build the script on that 30-second window. The brief says 20 seconds; no source found gives 20, so the template keeps the measured window, 30 seconds, and puts the promise in the first spoken lines.

**Put the strongest moment early.** YouTube's Content tab tips: "If your top moments happen later in your video, consider introducing that content earlier in the video" ([YouTube Help, Content tab analytics tips, answer 12942217](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/12942217)). In the template the hook shows a few seconds of the fortnight's strongest moment, and the full moment sits in its chapter later. That moment is usually also the Short (B5 step 6), so one selection serves both.

**Chapters: several, each a step of the fortnight.** YouTube requires the first timestamp at 00:00, at least three in ascending order, and chapters of at least 10 seconds; it says chapters "add info and context to each portion of the video and let you easily rewatch different parts of the video" ([YouTube Help, Video chapters, answer 9884579](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/9884579?hl=en)). No source found links chapter spacing to retention, and none gives the brief's "every 2–3 minutes". The template sets chapters at about 2.5 minutes because a 12-minute episode with A1 §3's fixed elements falls into seven parts: two 30-second openers, four parts of about 2.5 minutes and a one-minute ending (Deliverable B1); the interval is his convention, and the only sourced rule is YouTube's minimum.

**End on the last line, with no wind-down.** Percentage viewed is the share of the video watched on average (A6 metrics sheet, row Y3): average view duration over the video's length. YouTube notes that "Videos on YouTube generally taper off during the playback period" ([YouTube Help 9314415](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/9314415)). So a closing minute that most viewers never reach adds to the length more than it adds to the average duration, and the percentage falls (my reasoning from the definition; no source found measures the effect of an outro). End screens occupy "the last 5–20 seconds of a video" and need a video of at least 25 seconds (A3 §5, from YouTube Help 6388789); vidIQ's guidance is to "Use an end screen and a clear verbal bridge to another relevant video". In the template, the episode ends on the last line from the takes (B5 step 4: "Ending: the last line, from the takes"), and the final 20 seconds carry that line, a one-sentence bridge to the next episode or the season playlist, and the end screen over it. No subscribe request (A6 rule 8; B5 rules for every step); no recap (his rule).

**When to read the numbers.** None of this is checked in Studio after an upload. A9's checkpoints read CTR and APV on uploads at least seven days old, on fixed dates, and Studio stays closed between them (A9, Deliverable 2; A6 metrics sheet rows Y1, Y3, Y4).

## 4 · Thumbnails in the Shōmen stylesheet, and titles the clickbait rule excludes

### The stylesheet

The brand system is the one B3 records for Shōmen: the Tomorrow typeface, "red #FF2A50 and light blue #54C8FF highlights on near-black", in two locked styles; "Swiss-grid: facts in aligned rows, no decoration. HUD/glitch: the same facts as readouts" (B3, templates 5 and 6). B3 template 5 already asks for an episode's thumbnail in one of those two styles and left the word limit open for this unit ("<UNKNOWN: word limit; A5 covers thumbnails>").

**What YouTube requires of the file.** A 16:9 image, JPG or PNG; YouTube suggests 3840 × 2160 with a minimum width of 640 pixels; up to 2 MB when uploaded from a phone and 50 MB from a computer; custom thumbnails need a verified account; every custom thumbnail must follow the Community Guidelines, and one that breaks them can be rejected and draw a strike ([YouTube Help, Add video thumbnails, answer 72431](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/72431?hl=en-GB)).

**Contrast.** The two highlight colours are not equally legible on near-black. WCAG 2's contrast criterion for web text asks for at least 4.5:1, or 3:1 for large text (18 point, or 14 point bold), computed as (L1 + 0.05) / (L2 + 0.05) on relative luminance ([W3C, Understanding Success Criterion 1.4.3](https://w3.org/WAI/WCAG20/Understanding/contrast-minimum)). That is a standard for web pages; applying it to thumbnail text is my transfer. Computed by script: light blue #54C8FF on pure black is 11.08:1 and red #FF2A50 is 5.70:1; on #1A1A1A, a stand-in for "near-black" since no filed unit gives its value, they are 9.19:1 and 4.72:1. Red against light blue is 1.94:1, below even the large-text threshold.

**The thumbnail rules, his, each with its basis:**

1. **The image is a frame from the fortnight's footage.** Nothing is shot for the thumbnail. Basis: A3 §1, nothing filmed twice; A4 record rule 4.
2. **Text: one line of at most four words, plus at most one number.** Every word and number on a thumbnail is a promise the first 30 seconds have to keep (§3); fewer words are fewer promises to check. The number comes from the script and from A4's publish list (distance, duration, pace, race result, weekly volume, weight change), never an absolute weight. Basis: YouTube's clickbait definitions (below); A4, disclosure policy clause 3. The four-word limit is his convention and fills B3 template 5's open slot.
3. **Swiss-grid for position and numbers, HUD/glitch for a readout.** Swiss-grid: the season label and the syllabus position in aligned rows ("WEEK 6 / 20"), with the number on its own row. HUD/glitch: the same facts as a readout. The text is light blue, or red on near-black; red and light blue never sit on each other (contrast above).
4. **Test the two styles against each other.** Test & Compare takes up to three variants and picks on watch time (A6 §1.2 and playbook YouTube 3). Two of the three variants are the same frame and text in the two styles; the third changes the frame. Over a season the results say which style holds viewers on his channel (A6 metrics sheet, row Y2).
5. **The thumbnail leads with the journey.** Never with a diagnosis, a crisis, a loss or a verdict on himself. Basis: A4, disclosure policy clause 5.
6. **A Shōmen product on screen is named as his brand.** Using the brand's colours on the channel is a style; a product in the frame is a mention. Basis: A4, disclosure policy clause 7.

### YouTube's three statements on clickbait

YouTube describes clickbait in three places, with three different consequences:

| Where | What it says | What follows |
|---|---|---|
| Analytics help, answer 7628154 | "You can tell if your thumbnail is clickbait if it's getting high CTR but low average view duration and lower than expected Impressions." | Fewer impressions: the video is shown less |
| Spam, deceptive practices and scams policy, answer 2801973 | "Using maliciously misleading titles, thumbnails, descriptions or imagery to trick users" into clicking a video "that does not deliver what was promised"; example: a title offering "a full sports match" on a video that "shows only a clip" | Removal, with a warning or a strike for some violations; three strikes in 90 days can end the channel |
| YouTube India blog, 18 Dec 2024 | "Egregious clickbait occurs when the video's title or thumbnail includes promises or claims that aren't delivered"; the post singles out breaking news and current events; examples include a title announcing a resignation the video does not address | Removal "without issuing a strike", "slowly rolling this out in India over the coming months", new uploads first |

Sources: [YouTube Help 7628154](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/7628154?hl=en-GB); [YouTube Help, Spam, deceptive practices and scams policies, answer 2801973](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/2801973?hl=en-GB); [Google India blog, Strengthening enforcement against egregious clickbait on YouTube, 18 Dec 2024](https://blog.google/intl/en-in/products/platforms/strengthening-enforcement-against-egregious-clickbait-on-youtube/). Whether the India enforcement has since been extended to other countries was not checked. The brief's definition (high CTR, low AVD) is the first row, the diagnostic one; the other two turn the same idea, a promise the video does not keep, into policy.

### Titles the rule excludes

Each of these is a promise the episode would not keep, which all three statements describe:

- **The result before it happens.** "Sub-1:45" in the title of an episode from week 6 of the plan. The race result exists only in the finale (A3 §5).
- **A question the episode does not answer.** Already on B3 template 5's and B5 step 5's avoid list.
- **A hidden truth.** "Nobody tells you", "the truth about", "you won't believe": each promises information the episode does not hold (B3, B5 avoid lists).
- **A number the script does not state.** B5 step 5: no "time, weight or result the script does not state".
- **Volume for its own sake.** Whole words in capitals (B3; B5).

What stays: a title that says what happened in the fortnight, true of the first 30 seconds, ending with the season label, 100 characters or fewer (B5 step 5, from the YouTube Data API limit). Both published titles, with their series suffix, are under 100 characters (97 and 96, counted by script from the titles A1 §6 records); whether each is true of its first 30 seconds, A1 §6 does not say.

## 5 · An editing workflow for a 12-minute episode in under four hours

### The target against the evidence

The only time figure in the filed units is A2 §6's vendor rule of thumb: "As a rule of thumb, expect 30 minutes to an hour of editing time per minute of finished video" ([Tasty Edits, updated 17 Sep 2026](https://www.tastyedits.com/how-long-does-it-take-to-edit-a-video/); vendor, no method or data stated, confirmed 2 Nov 2026). At that rate a 12-minute episode is 6 to 12 hours. The brief's target, under 4 hours, is under 20 minutes per finished minute, a third below the rule's floor. A2 planned 5 hours for a 10-minute episode at the floor; A3 §2 kept the episode at about 10 minutes, 12 at most, "until A5's editing workflow shows a lower cost per minute in the hours log", and A3's calendar gives the edit one five-hour window with a hard stop (A3 §6).

No source found measures how long a one-person edit takes under any workflow, so the four-hour target is unsourced. What this section can source is the mechanism each lever relies on; the time each saves is unmeasured, and the hours log (A9, read 2) is what tests the total.

### Four levers

1. **The script exists before the edit.** B5 step 4 cuts the script on Friday of week A from the record and the takes, every line marked with its source and timestamp, with a hook, at least three chapters, the miss and the ending, and a planned runtime of about 10 minutes, 12 at most. The edit assembles a script that already exists. That is the largest difference from the editing the vendor rule describes, which starts from "skimming through the raw footage".
2. **The footage is small by construction.** One static camera, one lav, at most six b-roll shots per session (§1), and the episode's frame filmed in a one-hour window (A3 §6). The selects are bounded before the edit opens.
3. **Assembly from the transcript.** Adobe documents text-based editing in Premiere Pro, where the user can "edit a transcript and view the corresponding changes in the visuals" ([Adobe Help, How to use text-based editing in Premiere Pro](https://helpx.adobe.com/premiere-pro/using/video/text-based-editing-video.html), undated). Blackmagic announced for DaVinci Resolve 20 an "AI IntelliScript to create timelines based on a text script", which "will match the transcribed audio in media clips to the script", plus a transcription tool that generates a subtitle track and voice isolation and dialogue levelling tools ([Blackmagic Design press release via Silicon UK, 4 Apr 2025](https://www.silicon.co.uk/press-release/blackmagic-design-announces-davinci-resolve-20); vendor, written for the public beta). Either turns a marked script into a rough timeline in one step. Which editor he uses is not in any filed unit, current versions and prices were not checked, and neither vendor states a time saving.
4. **One pass per layer, each time-boxed, with a hard stop.** A3 §6 put the edit in one window because switching between kinds of work costs most at the switch (Monsell, Sumner and Waters, as A3 cites them), and gave it a hard end because a long window meets hyperfocus (Hupfeld, Abagis and Shah, as A3 cites them). The template applies the same reasoning inside the window: each layer (picture, b-roll, sound, graphics) is done once, in order, inside its box.

### Where the edit stops

The four hours cover ingest to export. Three jobs that follow the export stay in A3's other slots: the title and thumbnail, chapter timestamps in the description and the caption check go in Tuesday's packaging hour; the Short in Wednesday's half-hour (A3, Deliverable 1, D). The edit hands those slots their inputs: the chapter list, the Short's timestamps for B5 step 6, and two or three candidate thumbnail frames.

### How the template meets A3's window

The template's boxes total 225 minutes, 3 hours 45 minutes, inside A3's five-hour window with 75 minutes left as overrun margin. A3's window stays at five hours in the calendar: the plan changes only on evidence. His rule: if three consecutive edits log under four hours, the window drops to four at the next quarterly checkpoint and the hour returns to the fortnight's slack (A2, Deliverable 2; A9, Deliverable 2, read 2). If edits run over, A2's cut order applies before anything else moves: Instagram to zero, then the Short, then the off-week email to three lines, and last the episode, shortened to about 8 minutes with about 4 hours of editing (A2, Deliverable 2; A3, Deliverable 1, G).

## Deliverable · Production checklist and edit template

Built from §1–§5, 1 November 2026; web facts checked 2 November 2026. Usable without the unit. Each line names its basis; **(his rule)** marks a convention no source sets.

### A · Production checklist

#### Pre: once a season, then before every session

Once a season:

- [ ] Choose one frame rate for the season and write it in the season folder; record every clip at it (YouTube Help 1722171). **(his rule** for one rate per season**)**
- [ ] Set the phone to record at 3840 × 2160 if it can, so a vertical crop from the centre third fills a 1080 × 1920 Short without enlargement (A3 §1; §1 arithmetic). **(his rule)**
- [ ] Set audio recording to 48 kHz wherever it can be set (YouTube Help 1722171). **(his rule** to record at the export rate**)**
- [ ] Kit: phone, tripod, one lavalier with a windshield for outdoor sessions (Sennheiser manual, 05/2026; §1, §2).
- [ ] Ask every training partner who might be in frame, before the season, whether they agree to appear (A4 clause 2; B3 template 8, worked example). Image rights are A8's.

Before every session:

- [ ] Battery and storage checked the night before. **(his rule)**
- [ ] Choose the tripod spot: it shows no one who has not agreed and nothing that locates home or the start point (A4 clause 2).
- [ ] Subject in the centre third of the frame (A3 §1; B5 step 6 crop check).
- [ ] Lav clipped about 25 cm from the mouth (Sennheiser manual, 05/2026; Audio-Technica, inverse square); windshield on outdoors **(his rule;** basis the Sennheiser MZC 1-2 wind figure, §1**)**.
- [ ] Set up in under two minutes, before the warm-up. **(his rule;** basis A3 §6, capture is never a separate job**)**

#### During: the session

- [ ] The session plan is unchanged for the camera. **(his rule;** basis A3 §6**)**
- [ ] Six shots at most, each only if the session produces it: 1 place (no locating landmark); 2 start; 3 the work, wide; 4 the hard part, close; 5 the number, publish-list numbers only; 6 after (A3, Deliverable 1, B; A4 clauses 2 and 3). **(his rule** for the six**)**
- [ ] No second take of anything; a missed shot stays missed (A3 §1; A4 record rule 4).
- [ ] Lav off for sparring; the phone records the room. **(his rule)**

#### Post: after the session, through the edit and the package

After the session:

- [ ] Before leaving: the take, to camera with the lav, three prompts: what I did; what did not work; what changes next session (A3 §1; B5 step 1).
- [ ] Screenshot the watch summary (A3 §1).
- [ ] File clips, take and screenshot in the session's folder, named by date and session type (A3 §1).

Friday, week A (A3's slot):

- [ ] Script cut by B5 step 4: hook in the first 30 seconds, season label once, "week n of N", at least three chapters, one miss, the last line from the takes, about 10 minutes and 12 at most (B5 step 4; A1 §3; A3 §2).

Saturday, week B: the edit window

- [ ] Run the edit template in B, box by box, hard stop at A3's five hours; log the actual minutes per box (A3 §6; A9 read 2).
- [ ] Export: AAC-LC stereo, 48 kHz, 384 kbps; the recorded frame rate (YouTube Help 1722171).
- [ ] Mix speech to about −18 LUFS (AES TD1008, as reported by Shepherd, 2021; the transfer to video is this unit's).

Tuesday, week A: package (A3's hour)

- [ ] Thumbnail: a frame from the footage; one line of at most four words, plus at most one number from the script and the publish list; Swiss-grid or HUD/glitch; light blue or red on near-black, never red on light blue (B3 templates 5 and 6; A4 clauses 3 and 5; WCAG 1.4.3 by transfer). **(his rule** for four words**)**
- [ ] File: 16:9, JPG or PNG, 2 MB from a phone or 50 MB from a computer (YouTube Help 72431).
- [ ] Clickbait test on every title and thumbnail: does the first 30 seconds make every word and number true? No result before it happens, no unanswered question, no "nobody tells you", "the truth about" or "you won't believe", no number the script lacks, no capitals for volume (YouTube Help 7628154, 2801973; Google India blog, Dec 2024; B5 step 5).
- [ ] Test & Compare: the same frame in both styles, plus one other frame (A6 playbook YouTube 3). **(his rule** for the variants**)**
- [ ] Chapters in the description from the edit's chapter list: first at 00:00, at least three, each at least 10 seconds (YouTube Help 9884579).
- [ ] Review the automatic captions and correct mistranscriptions (YouTube Help 6373554).
- [ ] End screen over the last 20 seconds: the season playlist or the next episode (A3 §5; YouTube Help 6388789).
- [ ] Studio stays closed until the quarterly checkpoint (A9, Deliverable 2).

#### B4 · When the template fails

- A box overruns: the next box still starts and runs its full length; the overrun comes out of box 8, the only box that drops work (the fixes at the bottom of its list), and then out of the 75-minute margin before A3's five-hour stop. **(his rule;** basis A3 §6, the hard stop**)**
- The whole edit overruns five hours: logged as an overrun, never carried into the week's other slots (A3, Deliverable 1, E).
- Three edits in a quarter over five hours: the next episode is planned at about 8 minutes, A2's fourth cut, taken directly because the first three cuts free no editing time (A2, Deliverable 2). **(his rule** for skipping to it**)**
- Three consecutive edits under four hours: the window drops to four hours at the next checkpoint. **(his rule)**
