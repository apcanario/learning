# A7 · Monetisation through Shōmen

Every rate in this unit comes from a filed unit, with the source and label that unit gave it; A9 §7 and its expectation sheet hold most of them. Three points were checked against their sources on 2 November 2026 and carry that date: the VoxBooster merch page, YouTube's paid-promotion page, and the two pages that name Jesse Enkamp as Seishin's founder. All money is in US dollars, because every rate and order value used here is quoted in dollars; nothing is converted.

## 1 · The conversion numbers, applied to 2027 audience sizes

A2 §3 hands this unit three rates ("A7 applies them to Shōmen"). A9 §7 found them and graded them. They differ in strength, and the grade travels with each one.

**Owned list to paid.** The brief gives 1–3%. A9 splits that range by the quality of its sources. The two documented cases sit at the bottom of it: Artificial Ignorance had 13,413 free and 121 paid subscribers on 1 January 2025, about 0.9% ([Artificial Ignorance, 11 Feb 2025](https://www.ignorance.ai/p/two-years-of-artificial-ignorance)), and beehiiv cites Generalist World at 43,000 subscribers and 700-plus paying members, about 1.6% ([beehiiv, The State of Newsletters 2026, 6 Jan 2026](https://www.beehiiv.com/blog/the-state-of-newsletters-2026.md)). The 3% end rests on an aggregation of Substack reports that sits behind a paywall and on Teachery's per-offer course guidance, which has no dataset ([Really Good Business Ideas, 4 Sep 2026](https://www.reallygoodbusinessideas.com/p/substack-average-paid-subscriber-conversion-rate); [Teachery](https://make500.teachery.co/lessons/part-2-managing-expectations)); A9 reported both and kept them out of its deliverable. Substack's own statement is that "We tend to see 5-10% of free subscribers convert to paying subscriptions" (quoted in the same Really Good Business Ideas article), a platform claim without a dataset. This unit builds its scenarios on 0.9% and 1.6% and tests 3% and 5% as sensitivities.

**Followers to merch buyers.** VoxBooster states that "0.40% to 1.20% of a creator’s total social media follower base converts into physical merchandise buyers", with an average order of $48.50 ([VoxBooster, 22 Aug 2026](https://voxbooster.com/blog/merch-sales-creator-statistics-2026)). Re-read on 2 November 2026, the page attributes the rate to "Spring Benchmark Studies / Linktree" in its table, to Linktree alone in the text and to "Shopify & Linktree" in its methodology; it links no study and states no sample size or period. It also does not say whether the rate is per drop or over some longer window. A9 called it "the weakest sourced in the unit" and kept it out of its deliverable. It is the only figure that exists for the question, so this unit uses it, says it is weakly sourced each time it carries weight, and follows the brief's reading (per drop) while testing the other reading in the model (Deliverable B, test T1).

**What a paying fan is worth.** Patreon's company-sponsored State of Create survey (1,000 US creators and 2,000 US fans via NewtonX, fieldwork August 2024) reports average annual income of $52 per fan and $110 per paying member ([Net Influencer, 21 Feb 2025](https://www.netinfluencer.com/patreon-2025-state-of-create-report-how-creators-are-breaking-free-from-platform-control/)). The summary does not define "fan" against "paying member". This unit uses the two figures as the low and high price of a year's paid membership; that use is mine.

**The audience sizes A9 makes realistic for 2027.**

- *YouTube subscribers.* At 26 uploads a year the channel sits in vidIQ's 12–51 band, where 8.65% of 1,259,198 sub-1,000 channels crossed 1,000 within 360 days ([vidIQ, 11 Sep 2026](https://vidiq.com/research/youtube-uploads-1000-subscribers/); vendor data, correlational). A9's twelve-month checkpoint therefore expects fewer than 1,000 subscribers (A9, Deliverable 2, quarter 4), and the median channel under 100 subscribers gains about half a subscriber a month ([vidIQ, Subscriber Benchmarks 2026, 20 Jul 2026](https://vidiq.com/research/youtube-subscriber-benchmarks-2026/)).
- *The email list.* A9 row 13 gives three anchors for year one. Written alongside other work: The Introverted Networker reached 2,500 in three years, about 833 a year (my division) ([The Introverted Networker, charts as of 28 May 2025](https://theintrovertednetworker.substack.com/p/how-i-grew-my-newsletter-to-2500)), and The Creator's Playbook reached 2,000 at twelve months ([The Creator's Playbook, 30 Jul 2025](https://thecreatorplaybook.substack.com/p/celebrating-1-year-on-substack)); both are anecdotes. beehiiv's 2025 launches had a median of 8,314 subscribers at the end of year one, a self-selected cohort on a platform that sells growth tools ([beehiiv, 6 Jan 2026](https://www.beehiiv.com/blog/the-state-of-newsletters-2026.md)). - *Instagram followers.* A6 §4 found no reach or views benchmark for personal accounts under 10,000 followers. The only growth figure is brand data: business accounts at 1,000–5,000 followers grew 22.0% in 2025 ([Socialinsider, Instagram benchmarks, updated 22 Oct 2026](https://www.socialinsider.io/instagram-benchmarks); vendor data). Neither his account's follower count nor Shōmen's is in any filed unit. **YouTube ad revenue is zero in 2027.** From 1 February 2027 new Partner Program applicants need 1,000 subscribers and 8,000 qualified watch hours in 365 days ([YouTube Help, Changes to the YouTube Partner Program](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/12843009?hl=en)). ## 2 · Under 10,000 followers, a drop is a few orders

Below 10,000 followers the top of the weakly sourced range is 120 orders per drop, and below 3,000 it is 36 (§1). At 1,000 followers it is four to twelve. Those numbers decide how Shōmen should hold stock, and three facts make them smaller in practice.

**The channel's 2027 audience arrives through running.** Season two, the half marathon, leads the channel until the race in spring or summer 2027, one journey per season (A3 §4); what leads after it is set in no filed unit. The people the channel recruits in the first half of 2027 arrive through episodes about running; Shōmen sells karate gi and kumite apparel. VoxBooster's rate describes creators selling their own merchandise to their own followers, and nothing on the page says whether that merchandise matched the followers' interest. A karate product offered to people who came for running is a weaker match than the rate assumes (my inference; no source measures it). For drops, the follower count that matters is Shōmen's own account plus the karate part of his following, and that is the number the model asks him to enter.

**Orders spread across sizes.** Apparel comes in sizes, and a drop's orders divide among them. As an illustration only (Shōmen's size range is in no filed unit): twelve orders across six sizes is two per size on average, with some sizes selling none. Stock bought ahead of a drop is a bet on a rate that no source meeting the standard supports.

**A drop's operating work sits outside the 5–7 hours.** A2's allocation plans 12.0 of the fortnight's 10–14 hours and keeps up to 2.0 as slack for editing overruns; it has no line for Shōmen (A2, Deliverable 2). Packing, shipping and stock counts come out of Shōmen's time. What the channel can give a drop is what its existing slots already hold: a product named when it is in frame, a line in one email, a Story from his account.

**What that means for inventory (his rules; basis: the arithmetic above).**

1. A new product is sold on pre-order or made to order for its first drop; nothing is bought ahead in bulk on the strength of the merch rate.
2. Stock held for a drop is capped size by size at what the previous drop actually sold. For the first drop, total units held are capped at 0.40% of followers on drop day, the bottom of the range, and anything above that is taken as pre-order.
3. Each drop is read at the next quarterly checkpoint: orders, orders as a share of followers on drop day, and units sold against units held by size. His own rate replaces VoxBooster's from the second drop onward.
4. Pre-order terms (delivery dates, cancellation, the right of withdrawal) are a matter of Portuguese and EU consumer law; they are settled in A8.

## 3 · Seishin and Jesse Enkamp: proof that it exists, and why it is no template

**What is documented.** Jesse Enkamp's channel is the top-ranked karate channel on FeedSpot's list ([FeedSpot, karate channels, updated 2 Jul 2026](https://videos.feedspot.com/karate_youtube_channels/)) and had 1.67 million YouTube subscribers on 27 October 2026, having gained 20,000 between 29 September and 26 October ([vidIQ, updated 28 Oct 2026](https://vidiq.com/youtube-stats/channel/@karatebyjesse/); vendor count, as A2 §1 reports it). The World Karate Federation described him in February 2018 as "the founder of the karate-gi brand Seishin International", and said he had "gathered a massive online community of enthusiasts" ([WKF, 17 Feb 2018](https://wkf.net/news-center-new/meet-internet-sensation-and-karate-nerd-jesse-enkamp/670)). His own site says "I’m “The Karate Nerd” and founder of Seishin International", links a "Karate Gi" menu item to the Seishin site, and also links a seven-day email guide and training programmes hosted on Gumroad ([karatebyjesse.com, read 2 Nov 2026](https://www.karatebyjesse.com/?page_id=2)). CreatorDB describes his audience in prose as "predominantly adult males across the US, UK, and beyond", without figures ([CreatorDB, 25 Jul 2026](https://creatordb.app/creatorstats/karatebyjesse/); vendor narrative).

That is the existence proof: a karate creator can run a channel, an email list and digital products alongside a gi brand he founded, and say so under his own name. It is a documented creator case for the fact of the brand. It is an anecdote for everything else, because none of the pages read gives Seishin's revenue, order counts, founding date, or the share of its sales that comes from the channel.

**Why it is no template.**

1. *Scale.* At VoxBooster's weakly sourced range, 1.67 million subscribers would be 6,680 to 20,040 buyers per drop (my arithmetic), against four to twelve at 1,000 followers. A brand selling thousands of units a drop is a different business from pre-orders at twelve.
2. *Subject.* Enkamp's channel and his brand share one subject, karate, and FeedSpot ranks the channel first in that subject. His channel leads with running until the 2027 race (A3 §4), so the subject of the audience and the subject of the product part ways for a season at least.
3. *Survivorship.* Enkamp is known because he succeeded. Creators who launched a gi brand beside a channel and closed it are in no source found, which is the survivor problem A1 §5 and A9 §1 describe for every case of this kind.
4. *Sequence unknown.* By February 2018 the WKF already described both the brand and the "massive online community". Which came first, and whether the brand needed the audience, is not stated on any page read. A plan that copies the order of events would be copying an order nobody has documented.

What transfers is narrower: a named owner on his own site, an owned list next to the channel, and the brand kept as a separate entity under its own name and account. All three are already in this course's plan (A4 clause 7; A2's Substack pick; Shōmen's own Instagram, B3 template 6).

## 4 · Sponsorship stories as episodes

Shōmen sponsors the three SC Braga athletes through December 2027. Their image rights, consent, credit lines and anything about them as people belong to A8. Wherever the plan below needs a name, a face, a category or a result, it says "settled in A8" and goes no further.

**The label is constant; the content decides the rest.** A4's disclosure policy already fixes what every Shōmen appearance must say: "Every mention of Shōmen says it is my brand" (A4, Deliverable A, clause 7), and a product in frame counts as a mention (A5 §4, thumbnail rule 6). The UK advertising regulators' guide, written with the Competition and Markets Authority, says that whether "promoting your own products/services" or a brand's, "a clear and upfront disclosure is always necessary" ([CAP/ASA with the CMA, *Influencers' guide*](https://www.asa.org.uk/static/9cc1fb3f-1288-405d-af3468ff18277299/INFLUENCERGuidanceupdatev6HR.pdf)); that is UK guidance, and the Portuguese and EU rules are A8's. YouTube's paid-promotion page requires creators to select the paid-promotion setting for "branded content, sponsorships, endorsements, or other commercial relationships", which adds a label at the start of the video; read on 2 November 2026, it says nothing about a creator's own brand ([YouTube Help, answer 154235](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/154235?hl=en)). Because the page is silent, this unit's rule is to select the setting on any upload that shows or names Shōmen (his rule; basis: the CAP/ASA line on own products, and A4 §2 below).

So every sponsorship story carries the same disclosure whether it reads as an episode or as an advert. What differs is whether the story earns its watch time.

**Why an episode.** A4 §2 reports the peer-reviewed study that A2 and A4 use on trust and purchase: in a cross-sectional survey of 407 Indonesian women who follow beauty influencers, perceived expertise and trustworthiness predicted the parasocial bond, and the bond predicted purchase intention; trustworthiness was measured partly as honesty "when recommending" ([Muhmin, Syah and Anggraeni, *Frontiers in Communication*, 2026](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/communication/articles/10.3389/fcomm.2026.1723759/full); correlational, a population far from his, as A4 says). A4 draws the consequence for Shōmen: the trust the record builds "is spent or kept at the moment he recommends something he owns". An advert asks for that trust and gives nothing back; an episode in the journey format pays into it, because the format names the miss and publishes the number whichever way it moved (A1 §3; A4, record rules 4 and 5). That last step is my inference from the study and the format; no study measures sponsorship content told as a story against the same content told as an advert.

The platform mechanism points the same way without settling it. Instagram ranks Reels on watch time, likes and sends, with sends weighing slightly more for people who do not follow the account (Mosseri, as [Social Media Today, 22 Jan 2025](https://www.socialmediatoday.com/news/instagram-shares-algorithm-insights-2025/738034/) reports it; A6 §2.1). A post holds watch time only if a stranger wants to see the end of it; a story with a stake and a dated outcome gives a reason to, and a product shot alone gives none (my inference; no benchmark for either exists, A6 §4).

**What makes an instalment an episode (his rules; basis: A1 §3's fixed elements, A4's record rules).**

1. A series label and a number: "Shōmen × SC Braga, 2027 · n of 5".
2. The brand's plan for the quarter stated first, the way a season states its syllabus.
3. What happened from Shōmen's side: kit supplied, fitted, worn, repaired, replaced.
4. The miss is Shōmen's own: a late delivery, a wrong size, a seam that failed, a stock-out. A4's record rule 4, "The miss is always in", applies to the brand. The athletes' losses are their facts; whether any result appears at all is settled in A8.
5. One number from Shōmen's records, published every instalment whichever way it moved (A4, record rule 5, applied to the brand).
6. What comes next, with a date.
7. The athletes appear only as A8 settles: by name, by role or not at all.

**Where the instalments run.** The channel's long-form episodes lead with one journey per season (A3 §4), the half marathon until the 2027 race, and the weekly email's second line is for his other live journeys (A3 §3). The sponsorship story therefore runs on Shōmen's own Instagram account, in B3 template 6's two locked styles, drafted on Sonnet 5.5 at low effort (B2, Shōmen posts) and checked by him. It leads a long-form episode only if the override in §5 fires, when it becomes the natural opener of a Shōmen season.

## 5 · The override: Shōmen first, Instagram as home

The default plan keeps YouTube long-form as the home (A2, Deliverable 2) and Shōmen in the margins of the channel. The brief asks for the case where that inverts: Shōmen becomes the priority, and Instagram, where A2 already places "Shōmen and brand contact", becomes the home.

**What Instagram offers a brand.** Reels mostly show accounts the viewer does not follow ([Instagram, Ranking Explained, updated 31 May 2023](https://about.instagram.com/blog/announcements/instagram-ranking-explained)); Trial Reels test an opener on non-followers first, available from 1,000 followers on a public account by Mosseri's July 2025 statement (A6 §2.1, playbook Instagram 4); and in Buffer's within-account analysis weekly follower growth rose 0.26% in weeks with 3–5 feed posts against 0.12% with 1–2, each relative to weeks with none ([Buffer, 13 Aug 2025](https://buffer.com/library/how-often-to-post-on-instagram/); vendor data, feed posts only). Instagram listed Portugal for Subscriptions in September 2023, and no newer official page was found ([Instagram, updated 20 Sep 2023](https://creators.instagram.com/blog/bringing-subscriptions-to-more-creators-on-instagram); A2 §2).

**What it costs.** A6 §2.2 documents that Instagram's reach is rented: recommendation can be withdrawn without touching followers, the exits are narrow (the bio link, and Story link stickers that reach followers only), and the metrics are redefined by the platform. In Patreon's survey only 24% of users' Instagram time went to creators they follow ([Net Influencer, 21 Feb 2025](https://www.netinfluencer.com/patreon-2025-state-of-create-report-how-creators-are-breaking-free-from-platform-control/); company-sponsored, self-report). And no source found measures how an Instagram post's views accumulate over months, so A2 §4 treats Instagram as producing value in the week a post goes out, against YouTube's own statement that its system recommends videos "regardless of when that video was uploaded" ([YouTube Help, answer 141805](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/141805?hl=en)). Moving the home moves the work from an asset that can keep drawing views to a feed that, on the available evidence, does not.

**What a switch does to a channel's numbers is unmeasured.** A9's twelve-month review excludes a change of platform for that reason: "no source found measures what moving platform does to a channel of this size" (A9, Deliverable 2, quarter 4). The nearest evidence is about mid-stream changes of format mix: channels that shifted their Shorts share by more than 30 points had lower median subscribers, with the cause unresolved ([AIR Media-Tech, 28 May 2026](https://air.io/en/audience-growth/do-youtube-shorts-help-your-long-form-videos-grow-data-from-18000-channels); vendor, correlational, larger channels). Repeated gaps of 14 days or more are associated with fewer views per video ([AIR Media-Tech, 8 Jun 2026](https://air.io/en/audience-growth/does-posting-more-on-youtube-help-we-studied-18000-channels-to-find-out); vendor, correlational).

**What decides it.** The override is a business decision, made on Shōmen's own numbers, and kept separate from A9's four channel verdicts. It needs three things to be true at once, each something he can read on a checkpoint date: Shōmen's own conversion beats the top of the weakly sourced range twice running; Shōmen's work has already pushed the publishing week past its budget for two quarters; and the channel's per-video numbers are not rising. Two drops in 2027 (March and September) are both readable only from the 16 November 2027 review, which is A9's twelve-month review and excludes a platform change. The first date the override can fire is therefore the quarter-5 review, Tuesday 15 February 2028 (A9's quarter 5 on A3's shifted dates, 91 days after 16 November 2027; my arithmetic). It cannot change 2027, so the revenue model below is unaffected by it. The rule, its trigger and what follows are written out in Deliverable A, part 7.
