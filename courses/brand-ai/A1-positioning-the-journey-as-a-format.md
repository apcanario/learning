# A1 · Positioning: the journey as a format

It was used on 28 October 2026 only to verify the creators' numbers and descriptions, the platforms' name and character rules, three academic citations, and whether the two vlog episodes could be found. ## 1 · What a positioning is, and what it is not

Al Ries and Jack Trout, who made the term common in marketing, defined positioning as something done to the mind of the prospect (*Positioning: The Battle for Your Mind*, McGraw-Hill, 1981; wording checked against [Graham Mann's book notes](https://grahammann.net/book-notes/positioning-al-ries-jack-trout), which quote it as "Positioning is what you do to the mind of the prospect, not what you do to the product"). The notes summarise their reason as "The mind rejects complexity". That is marketing theory from product advertising, without a dataset; it is used here as a definition.

Applied to one person, the definition separates two things that are easy to confuse.

**A description of the person** lists attributes: 35, Porto, a senior director in software, ADHD, Wado-Ryū and kumite, a half marathon to run, a history course, an apparel brand. Every item is true, and a stranger who reads the list can repeat none of it a day later, because nothing ranks the items or joins them.

**A positioning** is one sentence about what the work does, short enough that a stranger who watched one episode can say it to someone else. The course already holds its own test for this, in the Frame on course A's page: the positioning has failed if "after six pieces in the format nobody can describe the channel back to him in one sentence". At one episode every two weeks, six pieces is about twelve weeks (my arithmetic). That test is his rule; no source sets the number six.

Three consequences follow for this brand.

- **The sentence describes the method, and the person appears inside it.** ADHD, the job and the hours are facts that make the method harder and the record more honest; they belong in the expansion and the channel description, and they cannot carry the sentence alone.
- **The sentence must hold when the topic changes.** Karate has ended as a competitive season; the half marathon, the weight loss and the history course come next. A sentence built on karate expires with the season. §2 asks what evidence exists that a method can hold where a topic would not.
- **The positioning is distinct from the audience sentence.** A2's Deliverable 1 states who the audience is and what they pay for; this unit states what the channel is. The course goal needs both.

## 2 · Method or topic: what the evidence says about variety

The course's Frame gives the claim as a reason for the brand: "Generalist brands die on variety and live on a repeatable format." The brief asks why method-based brands survive variety and topic-based ones do not. The evidence found supports a weaker version, and it is graded here by source class.

**Platform-official: YouTube names format as an alternative to topic.** YouTube's tips for growing casual and regular viewers say: "Try creating content that's more consistent and similar in topic or format, or work with the same host", and "Create a series of videos from topics that have already proven popular with your viewers" ([YouTube Help, answer 13615784](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/13615784?hl=en)). The "or" matters: the platform treats a consistent format, or the same host, as a route to returning viewers that does not require a consistent topic. The page gives no effect sizes. The same page lists, under new viewers, "Consider experimenting with your content to appeal to a wider audience." Two limits, both in A6 §1.2: YouTube states the casual and regular segments have no effect on reach, and Regular viewers cannot exist before month six by definition (A9 §2).

**Platform mechanism: a varied channel is judged video by video.** YouTube's growth team describes discovery as per viewer and per video (A6 §1.1), and Todd Beaupré said the system tries not to over-weight a channel's history where it does not predict the next video ([Search Engine Journal, 4 Mar 2024](https://www.searchenginejournal.com/youtube-algorithm-myths-debunked-insights-from-the-growth-team/510091/)). My inference from those two statements: changing topic does not by itself cut a new video off from recommendation; the risk of variety falls on returning viewers, the people who came for the last topic. That is where a fixed method is supposed to help, and it is exactly what YouTube's tip above addresses.

**Vendor data: successful channels repeat a world more than a subject.** OverseerOS, a vendor of YouTube analytics tools, analysed 1,620 English-language long-form videos with at least a million views each, from 163 channels with five or more such videos (published 21 August 2026). In 97.5% of the channels every qualifying video had a different extracted topic query; the median channel's most repeated topic entity appeared in 20.0% of its winners; inside 91 channels with both kinds, videos that repeated an entity had a median of 4.53 million views against 4.65 million for videos that did not, which the article labels exploratory. Its conclusion: "Successful channels repeated a recognizable world far more often than they repeated the exact same subject" ([OverseerOS, 21 Aug 2026](https://www.overseeros.com/blog/stick-to-one-niche-youtube)). The limits are severe: every channel in it already succeeded, so there is no comparison group of channels that varied and failed; topics were machine-extracted; and the study does not classify channels as single-topic or multi-topic. It shows that topic variety is common among winners. It does not show that variety is safe.

**Marketing theory, peer-reviewed, about products.** The nearest academic literature is on brand extensions: a known brand put on a new product. Aaker and Keller found that attitudes toward an extension were higher when consumers perceived a "fit" between the original and the new category and when the original brand was seen as high quality ([Aaker and Keller, *Journal of Marketing* 54(1), 1990](https://doi.org/10.1177/002224299005400102); abstract read on [CiteDrive](https://www.citedrive.com/en/discovery/consumer-evaluations-of-brand-extensions/)). A secondary analysis of the original study and seven replications found support for the model overall, with the weight of each component varying by brand and culture, and concluded that "it is premature to make firm conclusions about theory on the basis of only one study" ([Bottomley and Holden, *Journal of Marketing Research* 38(4), 2001](https://researchers.mq.edu.au/en/publications/do-we-really-know-how-consumers-evaluate-brand-extensions-empiric/)). These studies concern products; no study found tests extension fit for a creator. The transfer is mine: if viewers judge a new journey by whether it fits what the channel is, a channel defined by its method makes every new journey fit by construction, and a channel defined by karate makes a half marathon a poor fit.

**Creator cases: survivors, labelled as such.** §5 sets out five channels whose identity is a method and whose topics vary: Mike Boyd's skills ran from violin to lock-picking to a reverse-steering bicycle in one year, and Matt D'Avella's thirty-day experiments ran from quitting sugar in 2019 to zone 2 training in 2026. They show that a method brand can carry variety for years. They were chosen because they succeeded, so they cannot show that method brands survive more often than topic brands.

**What no source shows.** No dataset found compares the survival or growth of method-defined and topic-defined channels, and no source found shows that topic-based brands fail when they vary. That half of the brief's claim is unsourced. A9 §8 reports that channels shifting their Shorts share by more than 30 points had lower median subscribers; that measures a change of format and does not bear on a change of topic.

**The claim at the strength the evidence allows.** YouTube itself names a consistent format, alongside a consistent topic or the same host, as a route to returning viewers (platform-official, no effect size). Successful channels vary their subjects far more than they repeat them (vendor, survivor sample). Product brands extend further when the new product fits what the brand means (peer-reviewed, products only). Five method-defined channels have carried varied topics for years (anecdote, survivors). Nothing measured says a topic brand would have failed in their place. So the working claim is: **a fixed, visible method is the best-supported way to keep returning viewers across a change of topic; that a topic-defined channel would lose them is plausible and unmeasured.** The deliverable is built on the first half only.

His own channel is where the claim gets tested. A2 §1 puts it in measurable form: the signal is the same viewers returning across journeys, read from the Regular-viewer segment. A9's checkpoints record that segment from quarter 3 (3 August 2027) as a baseline; the season change from karate to the half marathon is the first point at which a drop or hold can be seen, and there is no benchmark to compare it with.

## 3 · The journey template as the recognisable format

The thesis already contains the method: he gets consumed by a journey, builds a syllabus, records the process so he does not forget it, and ships the artefact. Written as stages:

1. **Consumed.** A pursuit takes hold, and a target is set: sub-1:45 in a half marathon; −15 kg; a history course written for himself.
2. **Syllabus.** He writes the plan before starting: what to learn, in what order, how progress is measured, when it ends.
3. **Process.** The sessions, the reading, the training blocks, recorded as they happen.
4. **Record.** The log of what happened, misses included. "Failures stay in the record" is a rule of the brand, and the record is what makes the misses usable later.
5. **Artefact.** The finished thing goes out: a race result, an edited season, a written course.

Stages become a format only when a viewer can see them. On the evidence in §2 and §5, a method is recognisable when it shows up in the same places every time. In the cases checked, that place is the title or the series name: Mike Boyd's "Learn Quick" round-ups, Matt D'Avella's "for 30 days" titles, Michelle Khare's series name "Challenge Accepted" (§5). For this channel the equivalent is a small set of fixed elements, his rules derived from YouTube's series tip and the cases:

- **The season label.** Each journey is a numbered season (karate was season one, per the course's Frame), named in the title, the playlist and the first ten seconds.
- **The syllabus on screen.** Shown in full in a season's first episode; every later episode says where in it this one sits ("week 6 of 20").
- **The miss, named.** Every episode states one thing that did not work. This is what turns "failures stay in the record" into something a viewer can see and repeat.
- **The artefact at the end.** A season closes on the shipped thing and hands over to the next season.

How the capture, recap and derivative layers produce those elements inside 5–7 hours a week is A3's job; A6's playbook already sets "hold one recognisable format per season" as YouTube rule 6 and the choice of search-shaped or browse-shaped titles as rule 1.

## 4 · Three candidate umbrella names

The umbrella name was left open when the borrowed name was removed from the brief on 27 October 2026. Each candidate below is judged on four tests drawn from this unit: does it name the method (§2), can a stranger repeat it (§1), does it hold every journey (§3), and is it free on the platforms (checked where possible). Platform rules that bound the choice, checked 28 October 2026:

The display name can be changed later; the decision is reversible at low cost.
- A YouTube handle is one of the "short and unique channel identifiers" YouTube assigns, 3–30 characters, letters or numbers, with underscores, hyphens and periods allowed except at the start or end ([YouTube Help, answer 11585688](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/11585688?hl=en)). The handle is the scarce part.
- The YouTube Data API documents a maximum of 30 characters for the channel title ([YouTube Data API, Channels](https://developers.google.com/youtube/v3/docs/channels)).
- Instagram's username limit of 30 characters is reported by third parties ([Bitly, updated 29 Sep 2026](https://bitly.com/blog/instagram-character-limit/)); no Instagram page found states it.

What was checked on 28 October 2026: YouTube About pages were opened for the handles @Consumed and @thesyllabus, and both are in use by unrelated channels. Fetches of @syllabusofone and of Instagram profile pages failed (YouTube returned an error to the fetch tool; Instagram blocks automated fetching), so the availability of those handles is **not known**. No name was checked for trade marks or for use on other platforms.

**1. Consumed.** The first word of the thesis.
- For: names the trigger, the part of the method a viewer feels first; one word; works as a title slot ("Consumed: the half marathon").
- Against: the YouTube handle @Consumed is taken (checked). The word also means used up, which reads against a brand about shipping something. It names the start of the method and nothing after it, so a stranger repeating it would not know about the syllabus or the record.

**2. Syllabus of One.** Names the step that is his.
- For: the syllabus is a stage none of the five comparables in §5 names in its About text or the titles cited there (their methods are speed, thirty days, a challenge, a goal, discomfort; my reading), so the name is distinctive in the sense of §1. It holds every journey, karate and history alike, and it matches what he already builds: Academy is a set of syllabi he wrote for himself. 15 characters, inside every limit above.
- Against: "syllabus" can read as a study channel, which a karate or running viewer may not click. @thesyllabus is taken (checked); @syllabusofone is unconfirmed. "Of one" says the plan is personal; it does not say the attempt is recorded.

**3. His own name.** The pattern of three of the five comparables (Mike Boyd, Michelle Khare, Matt D'Avella), each of whom runs the channel under their own name and carries the method in the About text, a series name or the titles (§5).
- For: survives any change of method or format, needs no rename later, and is the name ADHD is already public under. Handles built on a full name are often free (my inference; not checked).
- Against: the name says nothing about the method. D'Avella's case shows the cost: his About page does not mention the thirty-day format at all, so the method has to be carried entirely by titles. A stranger repeats "that guy" before they repeat the format, which is the weaker position on §1's test.

The recommendation, with its reasons in his decision format, is in the deliverable.

## 6 · The two vlog episodes against the positioning

The episodes could not be opened. On 28 October 2026 web searches for both exact titles, including searches restricted to youtube.com, returned other videos and, for the first title, John C. Maxwell's book and videos about it; YouTube's own search page refuses automated fetching. The channel name and handle are not known to this unit. What follows is an analysis of the two titles only, and it says nothing about what the episodes contain.

**Episode 1: "Sometimes You Win, Sometimes You Learn. Mostly It Sucks".** The first two clauses are a stock saying: the earliest use Barry Popik records is a tweet of 10 September 2007, and John C. Maxwell published a book titled *Sometimes You Win, Sometimes You Learn* in 2013 ([Barry Popik, The Big Apple](https://barrypopik.com/blog/sometimes_you_win_sometimes_you_learn)). The third clause is his.

- *Expresses the positioning:* "Mostly It Sucks" is the rule that failures stay in the record, put in the title. It refuses the motivational ending the saying usually carries, which matches the brief's tone.
- *Contradicts or misses it:* the title names no journey, no syllabus and no artefact, so a stranger cannot tell from it that this is a karate season, or that a method is being run. The first two thirds belong to a book, which is what a search for the title returned (my observation above). On A6's playbook rule 1, it is a browse-shaped title with no words a stranger would type.

**Episode 2: "Being a Better Athlete Made Me a Worse Person".** A confession of cost, built on a reversal.

- *Expresses the positioning:* it records what the pursuit cost, which is the record stage at its most honest, and it is the kind of outcome a syllabus never plans for.
- *Contradicts or misses it:* the subject of the sentence is the person ("made me"); the method is absent. A stranger would repeat "the guy who says sport made him worse", a confessional identity the positioning does not claim and A4's disclosure policy has to govern. Like episode 1, it names no sport and no stage.

**Read together.** Both titles carry the brand's tone and its rule about failure; neither carries its method. They read as the end of the template (record, verdict) with the beginning (consumed, syllabus) missing. That is consistent with a first season that was not yet built as a format; the template in §3 fixes it from the next episode on. For the two episodes already out, A6's rule 5 keeps them public, and the cheapest fix is metadata: add both to a "Season 1 · Karate" playlist and open each description with the season label, so a viewer who arrives from the half-marathon season can place them. That is his rule from YouTube's series tip, untested.

## Deliverable · Positioning statement, channel name and profile copy

Built from §1–§6, 28 October 2026. Nothing below claims an audience size, a growth rate or a name's availability that this unit did not check.

### The positioning statement

**One sentence.** He gets consumed by one pursuit, writes himself a syllabus for it, records the whole attempt with the failures left in, and ships what he made, one journey per episode.

**Expansion (60 words).** Each season is one journey: karate first, then a sub-1:45 half marathon in 2027, alongside losing 15 kg and a self-taught history course. Every journey runs the same way: consumed, syllabus, process, record, artefact. The topic changes; the method and format stay fixed. He has ADHD and a full-time job, works inside 5–7 hours a week, and publishes the misses.

**How it is tested.** The Frame's test: after six episodes in the format, viewers can say the sentence back (his rule). The measured test: Regular viewers held across the change from season one to season two, read at A9's quarterly checkpoints from quarter 3; there is no benchmark, so only the direction counts.
