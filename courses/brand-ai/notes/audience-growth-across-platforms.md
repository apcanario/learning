# One home platform, two years, no pivots

With 5–7 hours a week, the evidence says build on YouTube long-form (one 10–25 minute episode every two weeks, plus one or two Shorts cut from it), hold a weekly email list from day one, treat Instagram as a low-cost relationship channel fed by the same material, and stay off TikTok entirely. The most robust finding across every dataset in this research is not about platform choice at all: it is that **consistency on one platform for 2–3 years beats breadth**, that **strategy switches mid-stream are the documented loser**, and that the overwhelming majority of channels that fail simply stop publishing (**83% of sub-1K YouTube channels uploaded nothing in a 360-day window**) ([vidIQ Research](https://vidiq.com/research/youtube-uploads-1000-subscribers/)). At a bi-weekly cadence the realistic timeline is roughly **2–3 years and 40–70 videos to 1,000 subscribers**, another 1–2 years to 10,000, and no documented solo creator in any adjacent niche reached 50,000 in under four years. Median early views will be in the low hundreds and that is normal, not a signal to change course. Retention is driven by a repeated format and predictable schedule, not by topic breadth, which is a direct warning against spreading five journeys across a single channel in year one. Conversion from audience to buyer runs at **1–3% of an engaged, owned list** for a paid product and roughly **0.4–1.2% of social followers per merch drop**, so Shōmen sales from an audience under 10,000 will be a few orders per drop, not a business line. Portugal adds very little (no karate or running creator of scale, platform payouts largely unavailable) and English is the right call; the karate niche itself is thin above the top three channels, which is the single most under-exploited opening in the whole picture. Much of the quantitative material here is correlational and vendor-published, and the report flags it as such throughout.

## Growth is per-video recommendation, not subscriber push, on every platform

The mental model to drop first is "followers see my posts." YouTube's Senior Director of Growth, Todd Beaupré, said in September 2026 that **subscribers skip roughly 90% of uploads** in their feed, that suggested videos rather than the subscriptions feed are the biggest source of views from subscribed channels, and that ranking is assembled per viewer per session from watch history, context, time of day and device ([PPC Land](https://ppc.land/subscribers-skip-90-of-uploads-in-their-feed-youtube-director-says/)). The system judges individual videos, not channel averages, and YouTube says explicitly there is no "penalty box" for breaks and no permanent damage from one weak video ([Search Engine Journal](https://www.searchenginejournal.com/youtube-algorithm-myths-debunked-insights-from-the-growth-team/510091/)). That has two practical consequences for a bi-weekly publisher: a gap between uploads is not punished by a mechanism, and the only levers that matter are per-video packaging (click-through rate on impressions) and per-video satisfaction (average view duration, satisfaction surveys). YouTube's own definition of clickbait is high CTR plus low average view duration plus fewer impressions than expected, and such videos are recommended less ([YouTube Help](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/7628154?hl=en-GB)). Half of all channels sit at a **2–10% impressions CTR**; vendor heuristics put a healthy target at 4–6% CTR and **30–45% average percentage viewed on a 15–30 minute video** ([vidIQ](https://vidiq.com/blog/post/average-view-duration/)). The widely repeated folklore of "test audiences of 10–100 viewers" and "layered rollouts" has no official source; YouTube has only confirmed that early impressions go to diverse audiences including people who never watched the channel.

Instagram works on the same logic with different weights. Adam Mosseri named **watch time, likes and sends** as the top three ranking signals in January 2025, with sends weighted more for reach to non-followers and likes more for followers ([Social Media Today](https://www.socialmediatoday.com/news/instagram-shares-algorithm-insights-2025/738034/)). Reels and the newer Trial Reels (shown only to non-followers, requires 1,000 followers) are the only formats built to reach strangers; Stories reach followers only; carousels get a "second chance" resurfacing to followers who swiped past ([Lia Haberman](https://liahaberman.substack.com/p/the-best-instagram-content-formats)). The reach a small account can expect is modest: in Socialinsider's brand dataset, a 1–5K account averages **580 views per Reel and 993 per carousel**, and annual follower growth at that tier **fell from 38% in 2024 to 22% in 2025** ([Socialinsider](https://www.socialinsider.io/instagram-benchmarks)). Creator accounts engage roughly three times brand accounts in Metricool's data, so the absolute numbers are a floor, but the direction (halving growth rates year on year) is the point. TikTok's only official explanation of the For You feed dates from 2020 and states that follower count and past hits are "not direct factors" ([TikTok Newsroom](https://newsroom.tiktok.com/en-us/how-tiktok-recommends-videos-for-you/)); measured 2025 data shows 1–5K brand accounts averaging just **350 views per video** while growing followers 150% a year, an arms race in which output rose 40% and views per video fell 23% ([Socialinsider](https://www.socialinsider.io/blog/tiktok-engagement-rate-study/)). Across all three, the honest summary is that growth comes from the occasional piece the system pushes to non-followers, not from steady per-post reach to the people who already follow.

Email is the one channel where the follower count means something, and the numbers back that up: beehiiv reports a **41.24% average open rate** across 65,000 newsletters in 2025 against a 3.23% click rate ([beehiiv](https://www.beehiiv.com/blog/beehiiv-the-state-of-newsletters-2026)). Patreon's 2025 survey found only 24% of users' Instagram time, 32% of YouTube time and 38% of TikTok time is spent on creators they already follow ([Net Influencer](https://www.netinfluencer.com/patreon-2025-state-of-create-report-how-creators-are-breaking-free-from-platform-control/)); the survey is company-sponsored, but a four-in-ten open rate against single-digit feed reach is a different order of magnitude regardless of method. The catch is that a standalone list has no discovery engine. Substack says recommendations plus its app drive **50% of all new subscriptions** on the platform ([On Substack](https://on.substack.com/p/substacks-recommendations-network)), and beehiiv says newsletters using its Recommendations grew 2.75x faster, so a list started before any social audience exists is defensible only if it lives on a network with built-in discovery.

## Bi-weekly long-form means two to three years to 1,000 subscribers

The benchmark data is unusually good on YouTube and almost nonexistent elsewhere, so this section is YouTube-first. vidIQ's study of 448,413 channels created in the first half of 2025 puts the **median 30-day view count of a new long-form channel's first upload at 180, rising to 272 by the tenth**, with the best of the first ten reaching a median of 1,948; 57.8% of long-form channels got at least one of their first ten videos past 1,000 views and 30.8% got one past 10,000 ([vidIQ Research](https://vidiq.com/research/youtube-first-10-videos-study/)). These are survivor numbers (channels that quit before ten uploads are excluded), so the true median is lower. Reaching 1,000 subscribers is not rare in the abstract (40.6% of all channels with any subscriber have crossed it) but it is rare in any given year: of 32.9 million sub-1K channels, **1.5% crossed 1,000 in the 360 days to September 2026**, and the share depended heavily on volume, with **1.86% for channels adding 1–11 videos, 8.65% for 12–51, and 30.77% for 52+** ([vidIQ Research](https://vidiq.com/research/youtube-uploads-1000-subscribers/)). A bi-weekly cadence (about 26 videos a year) sits in the middle band. Among channels that did reach 1,000, roughly 68% had uploaded 40 or more videos ([vidIQ](https://vidiq.com/blog/post/how-many-videos-to-get-1000-subscribers/)). No large-sample study reports calendar time to milestones; the only durations available are a seven-channel anecdotal sample showing **5–26 months to 1K and 29–36 months to 10K** ([Social Video Playza](https://www.socialvideoplaza.com/en/articles/how-long-does-it-take-to-get-1000-subscribers-on-youtube)), which is illustrative, not evidence.

Putting those together gives a planning table for a bi-weekly long-form cadence. The figures are derived from the correlational sources above, not measured for this cadence specifically, and the 10K→50K row is the weakest because the only data there is from named creators who took 4–6 years.

| Stage | Realistic range | What the data says | Confidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0 → 1,000 subs | 18–36 months, 40–70 videos | 8.65% of sub-1K channels at 12–51 uploads/yr cross 1K in a year; ~68% of channels that reach 1K have 40+ videos | Moderate (large samples, correlational) |
| 1,000 → 10,000 | A further 12–30 months | Median monthly growth at 1K–10K is only +13 subs (0.51%/mo) across all channels; 2K–10K channels had the highest tier-up rate (16% in a year) | Low–moderate |
| 10,000 → 50,000 | 2–4 further years | Comparable running/ADHD creators took 4–6 years to 150–400K; no dataset isolates this band | Low (case-based) |

Two things cut against the pessimism. First, each additional upload compounds: median growth rises monotonically with uploads per month across 10.2 million channels, and the biggest single step is from "occasional" to "repeatable" ([vidIQ Research](https://vidiq.com/research/youtube-upload-frequency-study/)). Moving from bi-weekly to weekly roughly doubles the growth rate in that data (the 1–3/month tier versus 4–7/month), which is the strongest argument for finding a way to a weekly rhythm even if episodes get shorter. Second, back-catalogue discovery is real on YouTube in a way it is not on TikTok; one practitioner estimate puts the crossover point where old videos out-earn new uploads at around month six and 24–26 videos ([Humble&Brag](https://humbleandbrag.com/blog/new-youtube-channel-average-views)), and YouTube itself says videos can gain most of their audience a month or more after upload. The one case in the notes of an unusually fast year one (an assistant professor who published 88 videos in 52 weeks and reached 20K subscribers) shows what weekly-plus output with an existing professional network can do, and also that it took 1.7 videos a week, which is outside a 5–7 hour budget ([Sidika Tunc Candogan](https://sidikatunccandogan.substack.com/p/first-year-as-a-youtuber)).

Newsletter benchmarks need to be read with suspicion. beehiiv's headline for 2025 launches is a **median of 482 subscribers in month one and 8,314 by the end of year one** ([beehiiv](https://www.beehiiv.com/blog/beehiiv-the-state-of-newsletters-2026)), but that cohort is self-selected and survives only if it kept sending on a platform that sells growth tools. Every first-person part-time account in the research lands far lower: 100 subscribers after four months and 2,000 at twelve ([The Creator Playbook](https://thecreatorplaybook.substack.com/p/celebrating-1-year-on-substack)); 2,500 after three years alongside a corporate job and family ([The Introverted Networker](https://theintrovertednetworker.substack.com/p/how-i-grew-my-newsletter-to-2500)); 42,000 after three years full-time, with the first 1,000 described as "the slowest" ([Amy Suto](https://www.amysuto.com/desk-of-amy-suto/how-to-grow-a-substack)). Plan on the lower range: a few hundred in year one, low thousands by year three, with growth concentrated in the back half.

## One home platform wins; the losers switched mid-stream

No controlled study compares "one platform first" against "everywhere at once" for solo creators, and the report should not pretend one exists. What the large-sample data shows consistently is that consistency on a platform beats breadth and that platform-specific formats do not transfer. Buffer's analysis of 52 million posts found that accounts skipping a week fell below their own baseline growth (a "no-post penalty"), that top-decile accounts post more often and more consistently, and that format performance varies enough by network that content working on one often fails on another ([Buffer](https://buffer.com/resources/state-of-social-media-engagement-2026/)). AIR Media-Tech's study of 18,000 English-language YouTube channels found that the **"transitional" cohort that shifted its Shorts/long-form mix by more than 30 points mid-year had the lowest median subscribers in every niche**, below both stable strategies (in Entertainment at 100K–1M: 51,600 versus 72,400 and 73,600) ([AIR Media-Tech](https://air.io/en/audience-growth/do-youtube-shorts-help-your-long-form-videos-grow-data-from-18000-channels)). AIR is a vendor selling audits and says the data is correlational and quality-uncontrolled; the direction is still the most useful single finding for an ADHD creator, because the failure signature documented across every stalled case in the notes (no niche, cadence swinging from five posts a week to zero, overcommitting, burnout from doing everything at once) is the pivot pattern, not the volume pattern ([The Creator Playbook](https://thecreatorplaybook.substack.com/p/celebrating-1-year-on-substack); [Amy Suto](https://www.amysuto.com/desk-of-amy-suto/how-to-grow-a-substack)). Amy Suto's only flat months came from publishing more often than she could sustain, and her rule of picking a cadence you can hit "on your worst week, not your best one" is the right frame for a 5–7 hour budget.

The case for a second *format* on the *same* platform is stronger than the case for a second platform. The same AIR dataset finds a **25–40% Shorts share of uploads correlates with more median subscribers in Fitness (+41%), Business (+39%) and Education (+37% at small size)**, while long-only wins in Gaming, Beauty and Entertainment, and long-form performance weakens above roughly 55% Shorts ([AIR Media-Tech](https://air.io/en/audience-growth/do-youtube-shorts-help-your-long-form-videos-grow-data-from-18000-channels)). Metricool's 800,000-video sample points the same way: channels posting about five Shorts a month averaged 46% more views per long-form video than channels posting none, and the benefit vanished above 35 Shorts a month ([Metricool](https://metricool.com/youtube-trends/)). Nobody has shown that Shorts subscribers go on to watch long-form; the one Shorts-to-long-form conversion figure cited anywhere (0.04% to 0.5%) is unsourced. Treat Shorts as a cheap top-of-funnel bet that costs an hour per episode, not as distribution for the long-form. The same logic kills TikTok as a feeder: the funnel from TikTok or Reels to YouTube has **no measured conversion rate in any source**, cross-linking is structurally weak (only 2.7% of TikTok creators with 10K+ followers link a YouTube account) ([Click Analytic](https://www.clickanalytic.com/creator-economy-report/tiktok/)), and YouTube's own short-form surface is far more generous to small accounts in brand data (**15,160 views per Short at under 5K followers versus 350 on TikTok and 625 on Reels**) ([Socialinsider](https://www.socialinsider.io/blog/tiktok-vs-instagram-reels/)). Add that Portugal is excluded from TikTok's Creator Rewards Program ([Quasa](https://quasa.io/media/tiktok-creator-rewards-program-eligible-countries-in-2026)) and that the EU's February 2026 DSA preliminary findings may force a redesign of the recommender in Europe ([Crowell & Moring](https://www.crowell.com/a/web/xjuaM4g6zeeVU5v229RCpe/tiktok-to-challenge-categorically-false-dsa-addictive-design-findings.pdf)), and TikTok is a third platform with its own native format, no monetisation, regulatory product risk and no evidence of feeding the home channel.

Time budgets settle the rest. Vendor rules of thumb put long-form editing at **30–60 minutes per finished minute** ([Tasty Edits](https://www.tastyedits.com/how-long-does-it-take-to-edit-a-video/)), so a 10–12 minute episode is roughly 2 hours of scripting and filming plus 4–6 hours of editing spread over two weeks, with two or three Shorts cut from it in another hour; that is 5–6 hours a week and fully consumes the budget. A writing-first track (one weekly newsletter at 2–3 hours, two or three derived LinkedIn or Instagram posts at 1–1.5 hours, 15 minutes a day on comments) also costs 5–6 hours. Running both is 10–12 hours. Justin Welsh's template brings a newsletter issue down to 45 minutes only because it recycles his own best social posts ([Justin Welsh](https://justinwelsh.me/newsletter/my-secret-to-writing-a-great-newsletter-in-45-minutes)); for a video-first creator the equivalent is a short email per episode that carries the video, one thing learned, and a question, which is 30–45 minutes rather than an original essay. Instagram fits in the residual time only as a derivative: a 3–5 post per week rhythm roughly doubles follower growth versus 1–2 in Buffer's 2.1 million post regression (+0.26%/week versus +0.12%) ([Buffer](https://buffer.com/library/how-often-to-post-on-instagram/)), but each post must come from footage and stills already shot for the episode or it is a second production line. LinkedIn deserves a mention it rarely gets in creator advice: Buffer's 2025 data has it as the highest median engagement network (~6.2%), personal profiles out-engage company pages 2.60% to 1.60%, carousels average 1,451 impressions against 606 for video, and links in the post body cost 27% of impressions ([Metricool](https://metricool.com/linkedin-trends/)). For a senior corporate professional it is the one network where learning-in-public and athlete-discipline content lands on an audience that already respects the day job; the risk literature on employer conflict is about politics and posting on company time, not about side projects, and side projects "tend to be more acceptable to employers than strong personal stances" ([Globe and Mail](https://arc-dev.theglobeandmail.com/business/article-building-a-personal-brand-even-if-your-employer-doesnt-want-you-to)).

## Audiences stay for a repeated format, and leave when the creator stops or drifts

Retention has a thin but consistent evidence base. YouTube's own guidance for growing "Regular" viewers (the tier that returned six or more times in the past year, introduced in July 2025) is to create content "more consistent and similar in topic or format," to build series from topics that already worked, to publish regularly and to respond to comments ([YouTube Help](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/13615784?hl=en)). AIR's 18,000-channel study adds that channels on a predictable schedule earned more views per subscriber than channels at the same volume posting on random days, that going dark for 14+ days three or more times a year reduced views per video, and that in Health & Fitness the median subscriber count peaked at **2–5 videos a month (172,000) and fell sharply at 10–20 a month (77,700)**, with the same 2–5 peak in Education ([AIR Media-Tech](https://air.io/en/audience-growth/does-posting-more-on-youtube-help-we-studied-18000-channels-to-find-out)). Bi-weekly sits inside that band. Third-party guidance treats a Regular-viewer share above 10% as excellent for a new channel, with 20–40% the range for established ones, and warns the metric only becomes meaningful after about 20 uploads ([Vidpros](https://vidpros.com/youtube-returning-viewers/)); these are not YouTube-published benchmarks. The format evidence from comparable creators is by example rather than by study, but the examples agree: Goal Guys (560K, bounded fitness and productivity goals since 2015), Mike Boyd (2.7M, compressed skill challenges), Nick Bare's episodic Hyrox Prep series and Hellah Sidibe's run streak are all repeated, bounded pursuits, and discovery in each case came from one breakout video while the season structure kept people coming back ([Social Blade](https://socialblade.com/youtube/handle/goalguys/realtime); [yespress.io](https://yespress.io/mike-boyd.md); [Tatler Asia](https://www.tatlerasia.com/lifestyle/sports/hyrox-youtube-channels)). "Athens 2026, then half marathon 2027" maps directly onto that pattern; the five journeys in the brief do not, at least not in year one. The best examples all started as a single, specific, documented pursuit and widened later.

The community mechanics with measured deltas are small and specific. Patreon's 2022 census of 13,000 creators found those with a named community had **4–8% higher monthly retention**, and daily promoters earned 75% more than monthly promoters ([Net Influencer](https://www.netinfluencer.com/2022-patreon-creator-census/)). beehiiv's data says newsletters that recommend others are 32x more likely to be recommended back ([beehiiv](https://www.beehiiv.com/blog/beehiiv-the-state-of-newsletters-2026)). Discord and Circle communities have no published retention data for solo creators. Email churn is widely quoted at 20–30% a year but every figure found is unsourced aggregator material; the one measured benchmark, a 0.15% unsubscribe rate per send ([EmailListValidation](https://emaillistvalidation.com/blog/average-email-list-churn-rate-per-year-by-industry/)), compounds to 7–8% a year at weekly cadence, so the rest of any decay is disengagement rather than opt-out.

The honesty question has a real answer and a real cost. A 2026 structural-equation study of 407 followers found that expertise and trustworthiness, not attractiveness, predict the parasocial relationship that in turn predicts purchase intention ([Hidayat et al., Frontiers in Communication](https://public-pages-files-2025.frontiersin.org/journals/communication/articles/10.3389/fcomm.2026.1723759/text)); that is the mechanism by which openness about ADHD and mental health builds an audience that buys. The same bond is what the Administrative Science Quarterly 2026 study of 54 successful creators calls "dysfunctional entanglement": hypersensitivity to comments and metrics, distress at algorithm changes and a desire to quit, which the creators who recovered managed by delaying comment reading, depersonalising critique and anchoring in self-set standards ([PsyPost](https://www.psypost.org/new-study-sheds-light-on-the-psychological-burden-of-having-a-massive-social-media-audience)). The New Media & Society study of creator burnout found the fear "If I take a break, I'm going to get punished by the algorithm" is pervasive and largely unfounded on YouTube, which says it does not over-weight unpredictive history ([Cornell Chronicle](https://news.cornell.edu/node/333256)). For the audience side, the notes found no study quantifying over-disclosure risk; the men's-mental-health and ADHD audience that exists prefers long-form talking formats (ADHD Chatter at 408K with 83K average views per video at about 1.2 uploads a week; HealthyGamerGG at 3.49M with 91% of its audience on YouTube) over relatability Shorts, whose biggest exemplars show flat growth or a deliberate retreat from the format ([OutlierKit](https://outlierkit.com/channel/adhd-chatter-podcast); [CreatorDB](https://creatordb.app/creatorstats/healthygamergg/)).

## Conversion runs at one to three percent of an owned list

The buyer numbers are low and should be planned as such. Substack's official guidance is that 5–10% of free subscribers go paid; an independent aggregation of hundreds of publication reports puts the **median at 3%, with only 20% of publications above 5%** ([Really Good Business Ideas](https://www.reallygoodbusinessideas.com/p/substack-average-paid-subscriber-conversion-rate)), and a named AI newsletter reports a steady ~1% (121 paid of 13,413 free) ([Artificial Ignorance](https://www.ignorance.ai/p/two-years-of-artificial-ignorance)). Practitioner guidance for mini-courses sold to an engaged email list is 2–3% of subscribers per launch ([Teachery](https://make500.teachery.co/lessons/part-2-managing-expectations)). Patreon's free tiers convert to paid at roughly 0.7% a month by Sacra's estimate ([Sacra](https://sacra.com/c/patreon/)), and Patreon reports $52 a year per fan and $110 per paying member ([Net Influencer](https://www.netinfluencer.com/patreon-2025-state-of-create-report-how-creators-are-breaking-free-from-platform-control/)). Merch is the weakest-sourced figure: an aggregator attributes **0.4–1.2% follower purchase conversion at a $48.50 average order** to Spring and Fourthwall benchmarks it does not link ([VoxBooster](https://voxbooster.com/blog/merch-sales-creator-statistics-2026)). Applied to 5,000 engaged followers that is 20–60 orders per drop, and the one athlete-apparel case with a hard number (Alpha/AC1, ~125K Instagram followers) discloses only a 67% returning-customer rate, which points to retention of buyers rather than conversion of followers as the lever for a small gi brand ([Startland News](https://startlandnews.com/2022/12/alpha-ac1-2/)). The existence proof for creator-led gi sales is Jesse Enkamp's Seishin, built on a 1.6M-subscriber audience over roughly fifteen years ([WKF](https://www.wkf.net/news-center-new/meet-internet-sensation-and-karate-nerd-jesse-enkamp/670)); it is a proof that the path exists, not a template for a 1K channel.

Platform-native money is close to zero at this scale and in this country. YouTube's Partner Program needs 1,000 subscribers and 4,000 watch hours for ad revenue, rising to **8,000 hours from 1 February 2027** ([AIR Media-Tech](https://air.io/en/monetization/youtube-partner-program-requirements-2026-the-complete-guide), citing YouTube Help; verify before relying on it), and once there the median RPM is **$1.23 in Health & Sport against $10.22 in Education & Science** across 300 managed channels ([AIR Media-Tech](https://air.io/en/air-data-findings/which-youtube-niche-makes-the-most-money-in-2026-ranked-by-real-rpm-and-cpm)), which means the ancient-history thread would earn roughly eight times the karate thread per view if it ever had views. Instagram Gifts was rolled out to 22 EU states but not Portugal as of the last Meta announcement found (November 2023) ([Meta for Creators](https://creators.facebook.com/giving-more-creators-around-the-world-ways-to-earn-money)), and TikTok's Creator Rewards excludes Portugal. Brand deals are the realistic small-creator income: compiled rate cards put a nano (1–10K) Instagram post at $50–500 and a micro (10–100K) post at $500–5,000 ([ContentGrip](https://www.contentgrip.com/influencer-marketing-rate-card/)), and a Brazilian kata athlete with 85K Instagram followers lists $240 for a post and story ([OpenSponsorship](https://opensponsorship.com/profiles/nicole-jordao)). The wider income distribution is the sobering frame: more than half of surveyed creators earn under $15,000 a year ([Influencer Marketing Hub](https://influencermarketinghub.com/creator-earnings-report-2025/)), only 28% of European creators with a brand deal work full-time, and 23% of those men earn under €500 a month ([Kolsquare](https://www.kolsquare.com/en-gb/free-resources/voices-of-the-creator-economy-by-kolsquare-newtonx)).

## Karate is the thin niche; running and ADHD are the crowded ones

The adjacent categories differ enormously in crowding, and that should shape what the channel is "about" when a stranger first sees it. Running is dense: the English-language top tier runs from Total Running Productions at 807K through fifteen channels above 100K to a long tail at 13–70K ([FeedSpot](https://videos.feedspot.com/running_youtube_channels/)), and the verified mid-tier over-35 or comeback stories (Mark Lewis 399K, Hellah Sidibe 294K, Ben Felton 157K, Ben Parkes) all began as one-person training logs with a personal stake and took four to six years, with Lewis losing 1,000 subscribers in the 28 days to late October 2026, a hint that the mature channels are plateauing ([vidIQ](https://vidiq.com/youtube-stats/channel/UC6dr32gbapkzgw_Eyr6VsJw)). ADHD is denser still, with How to ADHD at 1.9M and a dozen channels above 100K ([FeedSpot](https://videos.feedspot.com/adhd_youtube_channels/)); the one under-served angle is the late-diagnosed high achiever, validated by exactly one large case, Alex Partridge's ADHD Chatter, which reached around 100,000 monthly downloads within about a year ([National Diversity Awards](https://nationaldiversityawards.co.uk/2024-shortlist/alex-partridge)). Learning-in-public is dominated by Mike Boyd and Goal Guys but no verified creator does self-taught ancient history as a documented pursuit. Build-in-public with AI is saturated on X in generic entrepreneur spaces and is a text-platform category, not a video one ([SoftwareSeni](https://www.softwareseni.com/building-in-public-the-10-year-distribution-strategy-behind-solo-founder-revenue/)).

Karate is the opening. The WKF has about 818K YouTube subscribers and will stream all major competitions on YouTube from 2026 ([FrancsJeux](https://www.francsjeux.com/en/2026/01/08/The-International-Karate-Federation-is-betting-on-YouTube/108893/)); Jesse Enkamp is at 1.62–1.67M and adding 20–30K a month while his Instagram is flat to declining ([vidIQ](https://vidiq.com/youtube-stats/channel/@karatebyjesse/); [CreatorDB](https://creatordb.app/creatorstats/karatebyjesse/)); and below those two the FeedSpot karate list drops to TEAM KI at 117K, Shotokan Karate at 52K and then a tail under 40K ([FeedSpot](https://videos.feedspot.com/karate_youtube_channels/)). **A channel at 10–20K subscribers would sit inside the top ten for karate on YouTube.** No over-35 karate athlete documenting a competitive comeback was found anywhere, and the audience is adult, male and US/UK-heavy, which matches an English-first creator. The category is narrow, so it caps at perhaps one to two million addressable people rather than the tens of millions in running, but a thin niche with a clear leader and an empty mid-tier is where a bi-weekly channel can become visible fastest, and it is the niche the gi brand sells into.

Portugal subtracts more than it adds, and the subtraction is small. The country has 7.59M YouTube and 6.35M Instagram users, essentially saturated ([DataReportal](https://datareportal.com/reports/digital-2026-portugal)), but no fitness, running or karate YouTuber of scale; the top Portuguese running influencers live on Instagram at 4–40K followers and six of the top eleven already post in English, with the biggest Portuguese-language runner at 14.6K ([Modash](https://www.modash.io/find-influencers/portugal/running)). A Porto-based "AI leader and multi-sport" profile already exists at 6.2K followers, which shows the persona is viable and small. Brazil is the real Portuguese-language pool (150M YouTube users, Portuguese a top-five YouTube language) but its fitness space is bodybuilding channels at 1.3–3.9M in Brazilian Portuguese ([HypeAuditor](https://hypeauditor.com/top-youtube-fitness-brazil/)); an English-first European creator would not capture it without a separate PT-BR track, which is a second production line. The right use of Portuguese is as texture (a line, a caption, an occasional PT-subtitled episode around a Portuguese event), not as a strategy. No data was found on Portuguese-language RPM or on AI Overview impact on Portuguese search, both of which are gaps.

## Recommendation: YouTube bi-weekly plus a weekly email, Instagram as residue, no TikTok

For 5–7 hours a week, the mix the evidence supports is a single YouTube channel publishing **one 10–20 minute episode every two weeks, with two or three Shorts cut from each episode** (keeping Shorts at 25–40% of uploads), a **short weekly email** on a network with built-in discovery (Substack or beehiiv) that carries the episode in alternate weeks and a 300-word note in the others, and **Instagram fed only from already-shot material** at two or three posts a week with Stories for the people already there. TikTok gets nothing. LinkedIn gets one or two derived text posts a week only if the hours exist after the other three, with the newsletter link in the comments rather than the body. The budget breaks down as roughly 4–5 hours per fortnight on the episode (script, film, edit), 1 hour on Shorts, 30–45 minutes a week on the email, 30 minutes a week on Instagram and 15 minutes a day on comments; it is tight and any week it cannot be met, the email and comments survive and the episode slips, never the reverse. The first season is one journey (Athens 2026 into the half marathon 2027), told in a repeated format, with ADHD and mental health as the honest through-line rather than a separate topic, and ancient history and AI held back until the channel has a Regular-viewer share worth measuring (about 20 uploads in). Expect 150–300 views per episode in year one, a few hundred email subscribers, and 1,000 subscribers somewhere between month 18 and month 36; review the strategy at twelve months and not before, because the data says the channels that change course in month six are the ones that end up smallest.

Override this if any of the following hold. If after 20 episodes the per-video average percentage viewed on 10–20 minute videos sits below 25% and CTR below 3% at 1,000+ impressions, the problem is packaging or format, not the platform, and the fix is a shorter, tighter episode, not a platform move. If the weekly email consistently takes more than an hour, drop it to fortnightly rather than dropping it. If Shōmen becomes the priority rather than the audience, the mix inverts: Instagram becomes the home (it is where karate athletes and buyers are, where 39% of creators list a public email against 4.6% on TikTok ([Click Analytic](https://www.clickanalytic.com/creator-economy-report/tiktok/)), and where brand collaborations are priced), YouTube becomes a bi-monthly flagship, and seeding product through 50–100K-follower karate athletes at a few hundred dollars a post replaces organic growth as the channel. And if the EU forces a redesign of TikTok's recommender or the karate audience visibly migrates to short-form, the Shorts share can rise toward the 40% ceiling without a second platform; the data never supports adding a third native format.

## Conclusion

The research reframes the question. "Which platform" matters less than three things the data agrees on: a cadence you can hit on your worst week, a format strangers can recognise in one thumbnail, and refusing to pivot for at least a year. Every stalled creator in the notes broke one of those; every one who reached the mid-tier held all three for longer than felt reasonable. The hard constraint is not the algorithm but the budget, and at 5–7 hours the arithmetic forbids two production lines, so the real decision is the order in which the five journeys get told, not whether they all get told at once.

The less obvious implication is that the thinnest niche is the strategic one. Running and ADHD are where the audience is, but karate is where a 10K channel is visible, where the gi brand sells, where attention is measurably shifting to long-form YouTube, and where nobody is doing the comeback story. The other threads are what will make the channel a person rather than a category once it has an audience to carry them; the evidence on format drift says they are a year-two problem. What the research could not supply is any measured data on calendar time to milestones, on short-form-to-long-form conversion, on newsletter churn, or on Portuguese-language economics, so the numbers above are planning ranges built from correlational, mostly vendor-published samples, and the first twenty episodes are the only dataset that will actually settle them.
