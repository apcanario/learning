# A9 · What to expect: benchmarks

Every number in this unit was checked against its source on 28 October 2026. Platform rules, thresholds and live channel counts carry that date unless another is given.

## 1 · What these numbers are, and what they cannot tell you

Every figure below is a base rate for other people's channels. Three properties of the sources decide how much weight each one can carry.

**Most large samples are vendor data.** The biggest YouTube datasets come from vidIQ, Metricool and AIR Media-Tech, which sell tools or services to creators and draw on their own indexes or client bases. vidIQ's own methodology for its uploads study says the sample "is not random" and is not a census of YouTube, and that its comparison groups were not matched on starting subscribers, channel age, resources or quality ([vidIQ, uploads-to-1,000 study, 11 Sep 2026](https://vidiq.com/research/youtube-uploads-1000-subscribers/)). Every cadence and growth relationship in this unit is correlational. Where a figure is vendor data it is labelled as such.

**Several samples are survivors.** vidIQ's first-ten-videos study excludes every channel that "gave up before publishing ten same-format videos in their first 90 days" ([vidIQ, First 10 Videos, 28 Jul 2026](https://vidiq.com/research/youtube-first-10-videos-study/)). Its study of how many videos channels had when they reached 1,000 subscribers counts only channels that got there, so it has no denominator and cannot give a chance of success at any upload count ([vidIQ, 9 Jun 2025](https://vidiq.com/blog/post/how-many-videos-to-get-1000-subscribers/)). Survivor samples overstate what a typical new channel sees.

**No large-sample study reports calendar time to a milestone.** None of the datasets found gives months from launch to 1,000, 10,000 or 50,000 subscribers. Every "months to" figure in this unit is one of two things: upload counts converted to months at his cadence (26 uploads a year, the arithmetic shown), or anecdote from named or unnamed creators, labelled as anecdote.

One mechanism frames how to read all of it. YouTube ranks videos per viewer and per video, and subscribers are a weak channel for views. Todd Beaupré, YouTube's senior director of growth and discovery, said in a Creator Insider interview published 1 September 2026 that "probably all your videos have about maybe a 10% or less click-through rate" in the subscribers traffic source, and that "even your best videos, 90% of the time, your subscribed audience isn't deciding to watch"; he described the subscriptions feed as small next to the suggested list on the watch page ([PPC Land, 2 Sep 2026](https://ppc.land/subscribers-skip-90-of-uploads-in-their-feed-youtube-director-says/)). That is a secondary report of an interview, with no sample behind the 10%. In an earlier interview Beaupré said YouTube aims "to not overemphasize historical data if that data isn't particularly predictive of future video performance", and that a weak video does not irreversibly harm a channel ([Search Engine Journal, 4 Mar 2024](https://www.searchenginejournal.com/youtube-algorithm-myths-debunked-insights-from-the-growth-team/510091/)). The consequence for this unit: subscriber count is a lagging stock that summarises past reach, and the per-video numbers (views, click-through rate, percentage viewed) are the ones that move first. The checkpoints at the end read those first.

## 2 · Year one, per video

**Views.** vidIQ tracked 137,355 long-form channels created between January and June 2025 that posted at least ten long-form uploads in their first 90 days. The median 30-day view count was about 180 for the first upload, 211 for the fifth and 272 for the tenth; the median of each channel's best video among its first ten was 1,948. Of those channels, 57.8% had at least one of the first ten past 1,000 views and 30.8% had one past 10,000 ([vidIQ, First 10 Videos, 28 Jul 2026](https://vidiq.com/research/youtube-first-10-videos-study/)). Two corrections apply before he uses these. The cohort posted ten videos in 90 days, about one and a half times his cadence: at 26 uploads a year his tenth video lands around week 18. And the cohort excludes channels that quit early, so the true medians are lower. Read the other way, 42.2% of channels that did keep posting (100 minus 57.8) had no video past 1,000 views in their first ten. That outcome is common among channels that continued, so on its own it says nothing about whether to stop.

**Click-through rate.** YouTube's own guidance is that "half of all channels and videos on YouTube have an impressions CTR that can range between 2% and 10%", and that new videos or channels, or videos with fewer than 100 views, "can see an even wider range" ([YouTube Help, Impressions and click-through rate](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/7628154?hl=en-GB)). The same page says YouTube recommends a video when it is relevant to the viewer and its average view duration suggests viewers find it interesting, and that a high CTR combined with low view duration and lower-than-expected impressions can indicate clickbait. YouTube publishes no "good" CTR target. Vendor CTR targets of 4–6% circulate widely (A5's brief carries one), but none found carries a dataset, so this unit uses none of them.

**Average percentage viewed (APV).** vidIQ publishes target bands it labels "vidIQ guidance", without a sample: 50–70% for videos under 5 minutes, 40–55% for 5–15 minutes, 30–45% for 15–30 minutes, 25–35% above 30 minutes ([vidIQ, average view duration, reviewed 21 Jul 2026](https://vidiq.com/blog/post/average-view-duration/)). For a 10–20 minute episode, the 5–15 and 15–30 bands bracket it. These are vendor heuristics and are labelled that way in the deliverable.

**Regular viewers.** YouTube replaced the returning-viewers metric in July 2025 with three segments, and said the new ones are "not a direct equivalent" of the old metric ([Search Engine Journal, 2 Jul 2025](https://www.searchenginejournal.com/youtube-adds-new-viewer-metrics-to-track-audience-loyalty/550289/)). YouTube defines casual viewers as having "watched your channel at least once per month for one to 5 months in the past year", and regular viewers as having "watched a video on your channel at least once per month for more than 6 months in the past year" ([YouTube Help, Understand new, casual and regular viewers](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/10246996?hl=en)); its tips page phrases the threshold as returning "for at least six months in the past year" ([YouTube Help, New, casual and regular viewers tips](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/13615784?hl=en)). The segment counts months with a view, not visits. Two consequences follow. A channel cannot have Regular viewers until it has existed for six or seven months, so the metric is empty for the first two quarters by construction. And YouTube publishes no benchmark for the Regular share; the share benchmarks in circulation were written for the retired returning-viewers metric and do not transfer. The checkpoints record his Regular count as his own baseline and judge only its direction.

## 3 · From 0 to 1,000 subscribers

The best single dataset is vidIQ's study of 32,926,088 channels that had fewer than 1,000 subscribers and at least one video, compared with themselves 360 days later. Of those, 500,409 crossed 1,000, about 1.52% (the page gives the totals; the percentage is my division). The share depended on how many videos the channel added in the year ([vidIQ, 11 Sep 2026](https://vidiq.com/research/youtube-uploads-1000-subscribers/)):

| Videos added in 360 days | Channels | Crossed 1,000 |
|---|---|---|
| None (no net change in video count) | 27,360,211 | 0.20% |
| 1–11 | 3,423,276 | 1.86% |
| 12–51 | 1,259,198 | 8.65% |
| 52 or more | 883,403 | 30.77% |

That first row is 83.1% of the sample showing no net change in video count over the year. vidIQ warns that this "does not prove a channel published nothing", since uploads can be offset by removals or changes in visibility. Video counts include Shorts, long-form and livestreams, and the 52-or-more group does not prove a weekly schedule.

A bi-weekly channel adds 26 videos a year and sits in the 12–51 band: about one channel in twelve in that band crossed 1,000 within the year. A weekly channel sits in the 52-plus band at 30.77%. The groups were not matched, so the gap is an association: channels able to post weekly may also have more time, money or existing audience.

vidIQ's earlier study of 5,330,249 channels created within the previous three years that reached 1,000 subscribers (all vidIQ users) gives the upload count at the moment they crossed. 68% had 40 or more videos; 9.9% had fewer than ten; the largest single group, 30.89%, had 150 or more ([vidIQ, 9 Jun 2025](https://vidiq.com/blog/post/how-many-videos-to-get-1000-subscribers/)). Converted at 26 uploads a year (my arithmetic): video 40 arrives around month 18, video 70 around month 32, and video 150 around month 69. So the 37.1% of channels that crossed with 40–149 videos would, at his cadence, have taken between about one and a half and nearly six years, and the 30.89% that needed 150 or more would have taken longer than that.

The median growth rates show why time alone does not get a channel there. Among the channels that added videos in the 90 days to 20 July 2026 (11,752,034 in all; 3,332,098 in the three sub-1,000 bands), the median monthly gain was 0.5 subscribers in the 1–99 band, 1.4 in the 100–499 band and 3.7 in the 500–999 band ([vidIQ, Subscriber Benchmarks 2026, 20 Jul 2026](https://vidiq.com/research/youtube-subscriber-benchmarks-2026/)). A channel at the median of the smallest band gains about six subscribers a year. Channels that reach 1,000 are therefore not median channels growing steadily; they are channels where a few uploads outperform, which matches the first-ten study, where the best of the first ten had about ten times the views of the first.

Two anecdotes bracket the range and neither is evidence. A YouTube coach, Dexxter Clark, lists seven of his own and clients' channels, with 1,000 subscribers reached at 5, 7, 18, 18, 22 and 26 months where his table gives a value; the page is undated and its prose and table disagree on three channels ([Social Video Plaza](https://www.socialvideoplaza.com/en/articles/how-long-does-it-take-to-get-1000-subscribers-on-youtube)). At the fast end, an assistant professor posted 88 long-form videos in her first year from 28 July 2023, about 1.7 a week, and ended the year above 20,000 subscribers and 600,000 views with $300 or more in revenue; her first video drew 164 views ([Sidika Tunc Candogan](https://sidikatunccandogan.substack.com/p/first-year-as-a-youtuber)). That cadence is outside a 5–7 hour week.

For scale: 40.6% of the 61,186,246 channels with at least one public subscriber have 1,000 or more ([vidIQ, Subscriber Benchmarks 2026](https://vidiq.com/research/youtube-subscriber-benchmarks-2026/)). The milestone is common across all channels that have ever existed, and rare in any single year for any one channel.

## 4 · From 1,000 to 10,000

The stock figures come from the same snapshot: 40.6% of channels with a subscriber have at least 1,000 and 7.90% have at least 10,000, so about 19% of channels past 1,000 are also past 10,000 ([vidIQ, Subscriber Benchmarks 2026](https://vidiq.com/research/youtube-subscriber-benchmarks-2026/); the ratio is my division). That is a cross-section of all channels at one moment and is not a conversion rate; it mixes channels of every age.

The median growth rate in the 1,000–9,999 band, across 6,192,753 channels that added videos in the 90 days to 20 July 2026, was 13 subscribers a month, or 0.51% ([vidIQ, Subscriber Benchmarks 2026](https://vidiq.com/research/youtube-subscriber-benchmarks-2026/)). Compounded at 0.51% a month, 1,000 becomes 10,000 in about 38 years (my arithmetic). The median rate is therefore not a route anyone takes; the channels that cross 10,000 are in the upper tail.

Cadence moves that tail. vidIQ's frequency study of 10,210,278 channels that already had 1,000 subscribers (April 2025 to March 2026, Shorts and long-form counted together) found median monthly growth of 0.89% in views and 0.10% in subscribers for channels posting 1–3 times a month, against 1.32% and 0.39% for channels posting 4–7 times ([vidIQ, Upload Frequency Study, 8 Jul 2026](https://vidiq.com/research/youtube-upload-frequency-study/)). Moving from the bi-weekly tier to the weekly tier goes with about 1.5 times the view growth and 3.9 times the subscriber growth (my division). At the bi-weekly tier's median of 0.10% a month, a 1,000-subscriber channel gains about one subscriber a month. vidIQ states the relationship is correlational and that larger, healthier channels may simply have the resources to post more.

Metricool's 2026 YouTube report (799,718 videos from 71,177 accounts in its dataset, published 29 July 2026) gives the only tier-transition rates found: comparing 2025 with 2026 (the report does not define the window), 16.01% of accounts in its 2,000–10,000 band moved up a tier, the highest of any band, against 9.59% of accounts under 2,000, 7.01% at 10,000–100,000 and 2.11% at 100,000–1,000,000; 10.08% overall ([Metricool, YouTube Trends 2026](https://metricool.com/youtube-trends/)). This is vendor data from accounts using Metricool, not a random sample. The same report found that channels under 2,000 subscribers saw long-form views rise 267% year on year while average view duration fell more than 58%, which the report calls "thinner" views.

Durations exist only as anecdote. In the Social Video Plaza table, the four channels that reached 10,000 did so at 29, 31, 32 and 36 months, which is 9 to 14 months after each crossed 1,000 (my subtraction from the table) ([Social Video Plaza](https://www.socialvideoplaza.com/en/articles/how-long-does-it-take-to-get-1000-subscribers-on-youtube)). Those four are the survivors of a small, self-reported, undated sample. No sourced figure gives the typical months from 1,000 to 10,000, and the expectation sheet says so.

## 5 · From 10,000 to 50,000

No dataset isolates this band. What exists:

- vidIQ's 10,000–99,999 band, 1,756,249 channels that added videos in the 90 days to 20 July 2026, had a median gain of 109 subscribers a month, or 0.48% ([vidIQ, Subscriber Benchmarks 2026](https://vidiq.com/research/youtube-subscriber-benchmarks-2026/)). Compounded, 10,000 to 50,000 at that rate takes about 28 years (my arithmetic): again the median describes no actual path.
- In the same snapshot 1.30% of channels have 100,000 or more against 7.90% with 10,000 or more, so about 16% of channels past 10,000 are past 100,000 (my division). vidIQ publishes no 50,000 line.
- Metricool's tier-up rate for 10,000–100,000 accounts is 7.01% over its 2025–2026 comparison period ([Metricool](https://metricool.com/youtube-trends/)).
- In the Social Video Plaza anecdote, the same four channels went from 10,000 to 20,000 in 3, 5, 10 and 12 months ([Social Video Plaza](https://www.socialvideoplaza.com/en/articles/how-long-does-it-take-to-get-1000-subscribers-on-youtube)).

The documented creator cases are survivors, chosen because they succeeded, and none of the sources found dates their 10,000 or 50,000 milestones:

- **Hellah Sidibe** started a run streak on 15 May 2017; his first video, "why I run every day", went up on day 163 and was an instant hit, and his HellahGood channel had 276,000 subscribers when CNN profiled him in September 2022 ([CNN via LocalNews8, 22 Sep 2022](https://localnews8.com/sports/cnn-sports/2022/09/22/when-his-hopes-of-a-soccer-career-faded-hellah-sidibe-started-running-every-day-hes-still-going-five-and-a-half-years-later-2/)). FeedSpot lists the channel at 294,000 on 12 October 2026 ([FeedSpot, running channels](https://videos.feedspot.com/running_youtube_channels/)). Read across the two sources, that is about 18,000 subscribers in the four years after the channel took roughly five years to reach 276,000.
- **Ben Felton** began posting training videos during lockdown (the article gives no year), furloughed from work as a chef, as "simply something to do" ([Gazette, undated](https://www.gazette-news.co.uk/news/24124243.ben-running-colchester-runner-ben-felton/)); Ben Is Running had 157,000 subscribers on 12 October 2026 ([FeedSpot](https://videos.feedspot.com/running_youtube_channels/)).
- **Mark Lewis**'s channel was created on 11 September 2018 and had 399,000 subscribers and 514 videos on 27 October 2026, having lost 1,000 subscribers between 30 September and 27 October 2026 ([vidIQ, channel stats](https://vidiq.com/youtube-stats/channel/UC6dr32gbapkzgw_Eyr6VsJw)).

Those cases show one-person training channels in an adjacent niche sitting at six figures six to nine years after they started. They do not show when any of them passed 10,000 or 50,000, so the months for this band stay unsourced.

What 10,000 to 50,000 means depends on the niche. On FeedSpot's karate list, ranked by a popularity score that weighs subscribers, views and uploads, positions eight to ten on 2 July 2026 had 20,500, 16,100 and 16,500 subscribers ([FeedSpot, karate channels](https://videos.feedspot.com/karate_youtube_channels/)). In karate, the lower half of this band is already top-ten territory. Which niche leads each piece is A2's question.

## 6 · The owned list and the first money

**Email list.** beehiiv reports that newsletters launched on its platform in 2025 had a median of 482 subscribers in month one and 8,314 at the end of year one, across a platform base of 65,000-plus newsletters and 28 billion emails, with a 41.24% average open rate and a 3.23% click rate; median time to first dollar for 2025 launches was 66 days ([beehiiv, The State of Newsletters 2026, 6 Jan 2026](https://www.beehiiv.com/blog/the-state-of-newsletters-2026.md)). This is platform-published data on a self-selected cohort using a platform that sells growth tools. Three documented cases, all anecdotes; the two written alongside other work land far lower:

- The Creator's Playbook reached 100 subscribers about three months after a July 2024 launch and 2,000 at twelve months (a promotional line on the same page says 3,000; the main text says 2,000), with posting that swung from five times a week to none, and an explicit note that doing everything at once "burned me out" ([The Creator's Playbook, 30 Jul 2025](https://thecreatorplaybook.substack.com/p/celebrating-1-year-on-substack)).
- The Introverted Networker reached 2,500 over three years from a first issue in July 2022, written alongside "a full-time job, a family, and very limited hours" ([The Introverted Networker, charts as of 28 May 2025](https://theintrovertednetworker.substack.com/p/how-i-grew-my-newsletter-to-2500)).
- Amy Suto reached 42,000 in three years, says "the first 1,000 subscribers are the slowest", and lists publishing more often than she could sustain as the cause of her only flat months ([Amy Suto, 4 Jul 2026](https://www.amysuto.com/desk-of-amy-suto/how-to-grow-a-substack)).

**YouTube ad revenue.** Today the Partner Program's ad tier needs 1,000 subscribers plus either 4,000 qualified watch hours in 12 months or 10 million qualified Shorts views in 90 days ([YouTube Help, YPP overview, checked 28 Oct 2026](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/72851?hl=en)). From 1 February 2027, new applicants need 1,000 subscribers plus "8,000 qualified watch hours in the last 365 days, or 20M qualified Shorts views in the last 90 days"; existing members keep their status; the fan-funding tier stays at 500 subscribers with 3,000 watch hours or 3 million Shorts views ([YouTube Help, Changes to the YouTube Partner Program](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/12843009?hl=en); announced 10 August 2026 per [PPC Land, 12 Aug 2026](https://ppc.land/new-youtube-creators-face-8-000-hour-bar-for-ad-revenue-from-february-2027/)). A channel starting in November 2026 will in practice enter under the 8,000-hour rule: YouTube says entry thresholds for new creators change on 1 February 2027, without saying whether qualifying or applying decides. From the same date a YPP channel counts as active only with 1,000 qualified watch hours in 365 days, 1 million qualified Shorts views in 90 days, or 2 long-form videos or 5 Shorts uploaded every 90 days; the bi-weekly plan clears the upload test.

What 8,000 hours means at his format (my arithmetic): a 12-minute episode at vidIQ's 40–55% APV band holds a viewer for 4.8 to 6.6 minutes, so 8,000 hours is about 72,700 to 100,000 long-form views in 365 days, or about 2,800 to 3,850 views per episode across 26 episodes. The first-ten medians in §2 are 180 to 272.

What the money is worth once there: AIR Media-Tech's study of 300 managed channels (3,595 monetised channel-months, May 2025 to May 2026) found a median RPM of $1.23 in Health & Sport (interquartile $0.72–1.53; small channels $0.76) and $10.22 in Education & Science ([AIR Media-Tech, RPM by niche, 1 Jul 2026](https://air.io/en/air-data-findings/which-youtube-niche-makes-the-most-money-in-2026-ranked-by-real-rpm-and-cpm)). That is vendor data from AIR's own managed channels. At the Health & Sport median, 100,000 views earn about $123 (my arithmetic).

## 7 · The share of followers who ever buy

No source found measures the share of a creator's followers who ever buy anything. Every available figure is per offer, per month, or a ratio of two totals at one moment, and most come from email lists or paid tiers, not from social followers. The brief's question can only be answered with proxies, and the proxies vary in quality.

**Platform-official and documented cases (meet the standard):**

- Substack says: "We tend to see 5-10% of free subscribers convert to paying subscriptions, with 10% being a rate to aim for" (quoted in [Really Good Business Ideas, 4 Sep 2026](https://www.reallygoodbusinessideas.com/p/substack-average-paid-subscriber-conversion-rate)). This is the platform's own claim, without a dataset.
- Artificial Ignorance, an AI newsletter, had 13,413 free and 121 paid subscribers on 1 January 2025, about 0.9%, which the author calls a "consistent ~1%" ([Artificial Ignorance, 11 Feb 2025](https://www.ignorance.ai/p/two-years-of-artificial-ignorance)).
- beehiiv cites Generalist World at 43,000 subscribers and 700-plus paying members, about 1.6% (my division) ([beehiiv, 6 Jan 2026](https://www.beehiiv.com/blog/the-state-of-newsletters-2026.md)).
- Patreon's company-sponsored State of Create survey (1,000 US creators and 2,000 US fans via NewtonX, fieldwork August 2024) reports average annual income of $52 per fan and $110 per paying member ([Net Influencer, 21 Feb 2025](https://www.netinfluencer.com/patreon-2025-state-of-create-report-how-creators-are-breaking-free-from-platform-control/)).

**Below the standard (reported here, kept out of the deliverable):**

- An aggregation of hundreds of publicly shared Substack reports over about five years puts the median paid conversion at 3%, with 20% of publications above 5% ([Really Good Business Ideas, 4 Sep 2026](https://www.reallygoodbusinessideas.com/p/substack-average-paid-subscriber-conversion-rate)). The dataset is paywalled and cannot be checked.
- Teachery's course guidance says to expect "about 2-3% of your email audience to purchase your mini-course", and only if the audience is engaged and has heard from you regularly for months ([Teachery](https://make500.teachery.co/lessons/part-2-managing-expectations)). That is per offer, practitioner guidance, with no dataset.
- Sacra's analyst page gives Patreon 60 million free members and about 400,000 free members converting to paid each month, without a percentage and without dating the 60 million ([Sacra](https://sacra.com/c/patreon/)). Dividing the two gives about 0.67% a month; that ratio is mine and ties two figures the page does not tie to one period.
- Merch: VoxBooster gives 0.40–1.20% of social followers buying physical merchandise and a $48.50 average order, attributed variously across its text and tables to Linktree, Spring, Shopify, Fanjoy and Fourthwall, with no study linked ([VoxBooster, 22 Aug 2026](https://voxbooster.com/blog/merch-sales-creator-statistics-2026)). This is the figure most relevant to Shōmen and the weakest sourced in the unit. It does not enter the deliverable; A7 deals with Shōmen's numbers.

What the sound figures support: on an owned list, the documented cases sit around 1–2% paying at a given moment, and the platform's own claim is 5–10%. For social followers buying merchandise, there is no figure that meets the standard.

## 8 · The plateau, and why most quit there

**Where the stall sits.** The largest group in any dataset is the channel that has stopped adding videos: 83.1% of sub-1,000 channels showed no net change in video count over 360 days, and they crossed 1,000 at 0.20% ([vidIQ, 11 Sep 2026](https://vidiq.com/research/youtube-uploads-1000-subscribers/)). The first plateau is below 1,000, and its most common form is a channel with no new uploads.

**Growth slows with size.** Median monthly subscriber growth falls from 1.26% in the 1–99 band to 0.51% at 1,000–9,999, 0.48% at 10,000–99,999, 0.30% at 100,000–999,999 and 0.24% above a million ([vidIQ, Subscriber Benchmarks 2026](https://vidiq.com/research/youtube-subscriber-benchmarks-2026/)). Metricool's tier-up rate falls from 16.01% at 2,000–10,000 to 7.01% and then 2.11% ([Metricool](https://metricool.com/youtube-trends/)). Mature channels stall too: Mark Lewis lost 1,000 subscribers in the four weeks to 27 October 2026 ([vidIQ](https://vidiq.com/youtube-stats/channel/UC6dr32gbapkzgw_Eyr6VsJw)), and HellahGood added about 18,000 in the four years after September 2022 (from the two sources in §5). In percentage terms every channel is on a flattening curve; in absolute terms the curve can still rise.

**Why creators say they stop.** No study found records the subscriber count or month at which creators quit, so "most quit at the plateau" is an inference from where the stall concentrates, not a measured fact. Surveys give reasons, with the usual weaknesses (self-report, sponsor interest):

- Billion Dollar Boy, a creator agency, surveyed 1,000 US and UK creators (published 7 July 2025): 52% said they had experienced burnout as a direct result of their career and 37% were actively considering leaving; creative fatigue (40%), demanding workloads (31%) and constant screen time (27%) were the leading causes, and among those who had burned out, financial instability ranked as the most severe (55%) ([Billion Dollar Boy, 7 Jul 2025](https://www.billiondollarboy.com/news/over-half-of-creators-face-burnout)). Agency survey.
- Linktree surveyed almost 2,000 creators (November 2024): 59% said producing consistent content is a struggle, 55% named algorithm unpredictability as a barrier, and 70% earned under $49,000 in the past year ([Passionfruit, Nov 2024](https://passionfru.it/linktree-creators-109807/)). Vendor survey.
- In Patreon's company-sponsored survey, 78% of creators said burnout affects their motivation and 75% said platforms punish creators who are not constantly publishing ([Net Influencer, 21 Feb 2025](https://www.netinfluencer.com/patreon-2025-state-of-create-report-how-creators-are-breaking-free-from-platform-control/)). YouTube's growth team rejects that belief for its own platform: Search Engine Journal reports Beaupré dismissing the idea of a penalty box for breaks or falling views (§1 source), though AIR's correlational data below links repeated gaps to fewer views per video.

The peer-reviewed work describes the mechanism. Nguyen and Duffy, in *New Media & Society* (7 July 2026; 58 creator-authored pieces, 62 news accounts, 78 interviews), found burnout normalised and often "unspeakable", with the line "If I take a break, I'm going to get punished by the algorithm" a recurring theme ([Cornell Chronicle, 8 Jul 2026](https://news.cornell.edu/node/333256)). Pillemer, Harrison, Murphy and Park, in *Administrative Science Quarterly* (54 highly successful creators, 74 interviews), describe "dysfunctional entanglement": metrics become the main source of validation, underperformance causes distress, and many creators express a desire to quit; those who recovered did so by distancing from audience input (for example waiting a day before reading comments), depersonalising critique and anchoring in their own standards ([PsyPost, 20 Feb 2026](https://www.psypost.org/new-study-sheds-light-on-the-psychological-burden-of-having-a-massive-social-media-audience)). That sample is creators with very large audiences, not small channels.

**The money behind the decision.** More than half of 3,000-plus creators surveyed by Influencer Marketing Hub earn under $15,000 a year ([Influencer Marketing Hub, Aug 2025](https://influencermarketinghub.com/creator-earnings-report-2025/)). Of 783 European creators with 5,000-plus followers and at least one brand deal (France, Germany, Italy, Spain, the UK and the Nordics, May–June 2025), 28% work full-time and 23% of the men earn under €500 a month ([Kolsquare, 7 Jul 2025](https://www.kolsquare.com/en-gb/free-resources/voices-of-the-creator-economy-by-kolsquare-newtonx)). Portugal is not in that sample.

**Pivots.** A radical change in format mix mid-stream is associated with worse outcomes. AIR Media-Tech's study of 18,000 English-language channels (March 2025 to March 2026) found that 1,247 channels which shifted their Shorts share by more than 30 points had lower median subscribers than stable strategies; in Entertainment at 100,000–1,000,000 monthly views, 51,600 against 72,400 and 73,600 ([AIR Media-Tech, 28 May 2026](https://air.io/en/audience-growth/do-youtube-shorts-help-your-long-form-videos-grow-data-from-18000-channels)). AIR states it cannot separate the pivot causing the decline from struggling channels being the ones that pivot. Going dark for 14 or more days three or more times a year is associated with fewer views per video, and a predictable schedule with more views per subscriber than the same volume on random days ([AIR Media-Tech, 8 Jun 2026](https://air.io/en/audience-growth/does-posting-more-on-youtube-help-we-studied-18000-channels-to-find-out)). Both are vendor, correlational, and drawn from channels far larger than his.

Put together, the documented sequence runs: per-video numbers far below expectation, subscriber growth that looks flat at the median, a belief (contradicted by YouTube) that a missed week is punished, metrics read as a verdict on the person, then either a cadence collapse or a pivot. The checkpoints below are built against each step: they judge the system by uploads before subscribers, compare per-video numbers with the base rates above, read Studio on fixed dates only, and allow format changes inside the channel before any change of platform.

## Deliverable 2 · Quarterly checkpoints

**How to run them.** A quarter is 13 weeks, which at bi-weekly cadence is 6 or 7 uploads. Quarter one starts on the day of upload one. If upload one goes out in the week of 3 November 2026 (the start date in A3's brief), the reviews fall on the dates below; if not, shift every date by the same number of days. Each review is one sitting on that date. Studio and follower counts are read for the checkpoint on that day and not between; comments are answered in the daily comments slot without opening Studio. This extends the distancing strategy from the ASQ study (§8), whose example is not reading comments for 24 hours after posting, from comments to metrics and from a day to a quarter; that extension is mine, untested. Every review is written into the record, misses included.

**The same seven reads every quarter.**

1. Uploads published this quarter against plan (7 in quarters 1, 3, 5, 7; 6 in quarters 2, 4, 6, 8).
2. Hours logged per week against the 5–7 hour budget, and how many weeks went over.
3. Median 30-day views across uploads at least 30 days old; the single best upload so far.
4. Impressions CTR for each upload at least 7 days old.
5. APV for each upload, against vidIQ's band for its length.
6. Subscribers: total and net gain this quarter; Regular viewers (from quarter 3).
7. Watch hours in the last 365 days against 8,000; email list size; paid members or orders per offer, if any offer ran.

**The four verdicts.** Each one names the evidence it rests on. The thresholds are his own decision rules, set from the base rates; no source sets them.

- **Continue:** 6 or more uploads in the quarter and hours inside budget, whatever the subscriber count. Basis: rows 5–7 of the sheet; subscriber count lags per-video performance (§1).
- **Change format** (packaging, structure or length, same channel, same cadence): 6 or more uploads, and CTR below 2% on three or more of the quarter's uploads, or APV below its vidIQ band on three or more. Basis: YouTube recommends on relevance plus view duration and judges each video on its own (YouTube Help 7628154; Search Engine Journal, Mar 2024); a shift of more than 30 points in Shorts share is associated with lower median subscribers (AIR, May 2026, correlational, cause unresolved), so a format change leaves the Shorts share where it was.
- **Cut the load:** hours over 7 in three or more weeks of the quarter. The episode gets shorter before the cadence slips. Basis: going dark 14+ days three or more times a year is associated with fewer views per video (AIR, Jun 2026, vendor); Amy Suto's only flat months came from publishing more than she could sustain (anecdote).
- **Stop or rebuild the system:** fewer than 3 uploads in two consecutive quarters. Three a quarter annualises to 12, the floor of the 12–51 band; below it the base rate is the 1–11 band, 1.86% a year. Basis: row 5. A stop is recorded with its reason and the numbers at the time.

No quarter before quarter 8 uses subscriber count as grounds to stop. Basis: row 6; at his cadence the 40-upload line that 68% of 1,000-subscriber channels had passed arrives around month 18.
