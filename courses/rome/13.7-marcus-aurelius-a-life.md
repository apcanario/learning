<h2>Sources for the life</h2>
<p>Module 7.3 tells the reign: the adoptions of 138, the joint accession with Lucius Verus, the Parthian war, the plague with Galen's description and the dispute over its toll, the invasion of Italy, the auction of the palace furnishings and the column. This module takes the man, and the evidence for him is unusually intimate and unusually uneven.</p>
<p>The <em>Historia Augusta</em>'s <em>Marcus</em> is the fullest narrative; module 7.3 grades it among the sober lives resting on good early material, though it reports gossip it half disbelieves. Cassius Dio, a senator under Commodus, survives here only in Xiphilinus' eleventh-century epitome and Byzantine excerpts; he is cited by the Loeb book numbers, with Boissevain's in brackets (72(71).34). Herodian's book 1 opens with Marcus' last illness. Then come three witnesses no other emperor has: Fronto's letters, written between master and pupil over a quarter of a century; the <em>Meditations</em>, whose book 1 is a list of debts (deep dive module 13.8 takes the book; this module uses two entries, 1.7 and 1.16, for the life, and single details from 1.6 and 1.17); and the images, the column in the Piazza Colonna and the bronze horseman on the Capitol. The standard modern life is Anthony Birley's <em>Marcus Aurelius</em> (1966), revised as <em>Marcus Aurelius: A Biography</em> (1987).</p>

<h2>Family and boyhood, 121–138</h2>
<p>Marcus was born at Rome in a house on the Caelian on 26 April 121, in the year of his grandfather's second consulship (<em>Historia Augusta</em>, <em>Marcus</em> 1.5). He was Marcus Annius Verus, of a family from Ucubi (the <em>Historia Augusta</em>'s Succuba) in Baetica (1.4), part of the Spanish senatorial network that had produced Trajan and Hadrian. His father, also Annius Verus, died while praetor, when Marcus was a small child; the boy was brought up by his paternal grandfather, consul for the third time in 126 and prefect of the city (1.2, 1.10). His mother was Domitia Lucilla, daughter of Calvisius Tullus (1.3), heiress to brickworks near Rome known from their stamped bricks. His aunt Galeria Faustina (1.3), his father's sister, was the wife of Antoninus, the future emperor: the family link that made the adoptions of 138 possible.</p>
<p>The <em>Historia Augusta</em>'s account of his names is confused: it says he was first called Catilius Severus, after his mother's grandfather, and Annius Verus only after he took the toga of manhood (1.9–10), while modern accounts give Marcus Annius Verus as the name he bore until the adoption. More secure is Hadrian's pun on it. The childless emperor noticed the boy early, enrolled him among the knights at six and called him <em>Verissimus</em>, "most truthful" (4.1); in his eighth year he was made a Salian priest (4.2). The nickname stuck: Justin Martyr's <em>First Apology</em>, addressed to Antoninus about 150, is addressed to the emperor and to his son Verissimus the philosopher (<em>First Apology</em> 1).</p>
<p>His education was domestic and lavish. The <em>Historia Augusta</em> lists more than a dozen teachers, Greek and Latin grammarians, rhetors, a jurist and philosophers (2.2–3.6). At twelve he put on the rough Greek cloak and slept on the ground until his mother persuaded him to accept a couch strewn with skins (2.6); Marcus credits Diognetus, who first acquainted him with philosophy, with his wish for a plank bed and a skin (<em>Meditations</em> 1.6). Dio, describing the emperor on campaign, adds a body so frail that at first it could not bear the cold, and a habit of eating only at night (72(71).6).</p>

<h2>The heir, 138–161</h2>
<p>Hadrian's chosen successor Aelius Caesar died on 1 January 138. Hadrian then adopted Antoninus on condition that he adopt both Marcus and Aelius' young son Lucius (module 7.3). Marcus was sixteen; the <em>Historia Augusta</em> makes him eighteen (5.1). It also says he dreamt that night that his shoulders were made of ivory and found them strong enough for the load (5.2; Dio 72(71).36.1 tells the same dream). He became Marcus Aelius Aurelius Verus, was made quaestor and Caesar, and held the consulship with Antoninus in 140 (6.3–4).</p>
<p>Antoninus rearranged his marriage. Marcus had been betrothed at Hadrian's wish to a daughter of Aelius Caesar; the new emperor broke it off and offered his own daughter Faustina, Marcus' first cousin and much younger (4.5, 6.2). They married in 145. When their first child, a daughter, was born in 147, Marcus received the tribunician power, the formal sign of a partner in rule (6.6), and Faustina was named Augusta. Modern reconstructions from coins and inscriptions count at least fourteen children, two pairs of twins among them; most died young. Commodus and his twin were born at Lanuvium on 31 August 161 (<em>Historia Augusta</em>, <em>Commodus</em> 1.2), weeks after Marcus' accession; the twin died at four. Of the sons, only Commodus outlived his father.</p>
<p>For twenty-three years Marcus lived beside Antoninus, and the <em>Historia Augusta</em> says that in all that time he spent only two nights away from him (7.2–3). The <em>Meditations</em> keeps the deepest trace of this apprenticeship. The longest entry in book 1 (1.16) is a portrait of Antoninus as a pattern of rule: mildness, and steadiness in judgements once reached after due deliberation; indifference to so-called honours; love of work; readiness to hear anyone who had something to offer the common good; patience with free criticism; frugality with public money; and the ability, said of Socrates, both to do without and to enjoy the things most people can neither forgo without weakness nor enjoy without excess. It is a list of habits, not of deeds. Marcus learned to govern by watching a man who never left Italy and who made the business of empire a daily routine.</p>

<h2>Teachers: Fronto, Herodes and Rusticus</h2>
<h3>Fronto</h3>
<p>Marcus Cornelius Fronto, from Cirta in Numidia, was the leading Latin orator of the age and suffect consul in 142. He taught Marcus rhetoric and remained his correspondent until his death, perhaps about 166–167. The letters were lost until 1815, when Angelo Mai, at the Ambrosian Library in Milan, read them under a later text in a palimpsest from Bobbio whose leaves had been reused for the acts of the Council of Chalcedon; more leaves of the same manuscript turned up in the Vatican, and Mai published them in 1823. The texts are damaged and the order of the letters is reconstructed (Edward Champlin, "The Chronology of Fronto", <em>Journal of Roman Studies</em>, 1974). What survives is the most personal correspondence of any Roman emperor: the young Marcus writes of the grape harvest, of his sore throat, of his love for his master in terms extravagant even by the standards of ancient friendship, and the older Fronto writes of his gout and of the death of a grandson. Fronto's letters on eloquence, written to the emperor, argue that a ruler must speak well and that philosophy is no substitute; they read as an attempt to win back a pupil who had gone over to the philosophers.</p>
<h3>Herodes Atticus</h3>
<p>His Greek rhetoric teacher, Herodes Atticus, belongs to Greek module 7.4, which tells his career, his buildings and his trial before Marcus at Sirmium in 174. Herodes and Fronto were rivals; in a lawsuit of the early 140s Fronto was to speak against him, and the letters show Marcus asking his teacher to keep the attack within bounds.</p>
<h3>Rusticus and the turn to philosophy</h3>
<p>About 146, aged twenty-five, Marcus wrote to Fronto that he was reading Aristo's books, which both encouraged him and made him miserable by showing how far his own character fell short; he was ashamed that at twenty-five he had absorbed nothing of good doctrines and purer principles (Fronto, <em>Ad M. Caesarem</em> 4.13). The letter is usually read as a turn from rhetoric to philosophy.</p>
<aside class="dispute"><strong>Where sources disagree.</strong> Which Aristo? Edward Champlin ("The Chronology of Fronto", 1974; <em>Fronto and Antonine Rome</em>, 1980) argued for the Trajanic jurist Titius Aristo, so that the turn was from rhetoric to the study of law, and Birley (1987) accepted this. Pierre Hadot (1984) and Herwig Görgemanns ("Der Bekehrungsbrief Marc Aurels", <em>Rheinisches Museum</em>, 1991) held that the moral language of the letter fits only the Stoic Aristo of Chios. On the second view this is a conversion to philosophy; on the first, if a conversion at all, it is one from rhetoric to jurisprudence.</aside>
<p>The man who directed the turn was Quintus Junius Rusticus, probably the grandson of the Arulenus Rusticus whom Domitian killed (deep dive module 13.4 tells that death). Marcus' entry for him (<em>Meditations</em> 1.7) is the most practical in book 1: from Rusticus he took the idea that his character needed correction and training; to avoid the sophist's ambition, treatises on theory and showy exhortation; to give up rhetoric, poetry and fine writing; to write letters simply, like the one Rusticus wrote to his mother from Sinuessa; to be easily reconciled with those who had offended; to read closely and not be content with the gist; and to know the notes of Epictetus' lectures, which Rusticus lent from his own library. Deep dive module 13.6 sets out Epictetus' teaching and module 13.8 weighs the debt.</p>
<p>Dio names Rusticus and Apollonius of Nicomedia (the <em>Historia Augusta</em>'s Apollonius of Chalcedon, 2.7) as Marcus' Stoic teachers, Fronto and Herodes as his rhetors (72(71).35), and adds that even as emperor Marcus went to hear Sextus, the Boeotian philosopher (71.1.2), whom the <em>Historia Augusta</em> calls Plutarch's nephew, from Chaeronea (3.2). The <em>Historia Augusta</em> says Marcus consulted Rusticus on public and private business, greeted him with a kiss before the praetorian prefects, made him consul a second time and asked the Senate for statues after his death (3.3–5). Rusticus held that second consulship in 162 and became prefect of the city; in that office, about 165, he condemned Justin Martyr, the man who had addressed Marcus as Verissimus (<em>Acts of Justin</em>). The emperor's philosophy teacher and the Christian apologist met across a tribunal.</p>

<h2>Emperor with a brother, 161–169</h2>
<p>Antoninus died on 7 March 161. Marcus at once made Lucius his equal as Augustus (<em>Historia Augusta</em>, <em>Marcus</em> 7.5; module 7.3), married him to his daughter Lucilla (Dio 71.1), and in 164 escorted her as far as Brundisium on her way to the wedding at Ephesus, turning back because people said he wanted the glory of finishing the Parthian war himself (<em>Marcus</em> 9.4–5). Module 7.3 tells the war.</p>
<p>The ancient portrait sets a serious elder brother against a pleasure-loving younger one: the <em>Historia Augusta</em> says Lucius' vices offended Marcus, who concealed and excused them. Marcus' own account is different. In book 1 he thanks the gods for a brother whose character roused him to take care of himself and who pleased him by his respect and affection (<em>Meditations</em> 1.17). Fronto's letters to both emperors show a working partnership, with Lucius asking his old teacher to write a history of the war from the dispatches he would send. When Lucius died of a stroke early in 169, travelling with Marcus from the northern front (<em>Marcus</em> 14.8), Marcus had him deified; the <em>Historia Augusta</em> reports rumours that Faustina or Marcus poisoned him and rejects the charge against Marcus (<em>Verus</em> 10–11).</p>

<h2>The northern years and the rain miracle</h2>
<p>Marcus left Rome for the Danube in the autumn of 169 and did not return for seven years. Module 7.3 gives the invasion of Italy, the auction of the palace furnishings and the campaigns against the Marcomanni, the Quadi and the Iazyges; here the wars stay in outline. The headquarters were Carnuntum, on the Danube below Vienna, in the early 170s, then Sirmium on the Save. Galen, summoned to the emperors in 168, persuaded the emperor to let him stay in Rome as physician to the young Commodus (Galen, <em>On Prognosis</em>), and so missed the camps. The emperor who had never commanded an army spent his late forties and early fifties hearing embassies and cases in a frontier fortress, often unwell; Dio says he took the drug called theriac, not from fear of poison but for his weak chest and stomach (72(71).6).</p>
<p>The war produced one story that both pagans and Christians claimed. Dio tells how the Quadi trapped a Roman force in summer heat without water; a sudden storm fell on the Romans, who caught the rain in their shields and helmets, while hail and lightning struck the enemy. Dio adds that an Egyptian magician in Marcus' company, Arnuphis, was said to have called on Hermes, god of the air, to bring it (72(71).8–10). The <em>Historia Augusta</em> credits Marcus' own prayers, which brought down a thunderbolt on an enemy siege engine and rain for his thirsty men (<em>Marcus</em> 24.4). The column shows both: a burning siege engine struck by lightning, and a winged rain god streaming water over Roman soldiers while the enemy drown beneath him.</p>
<p>The Christian version came quickly. Apollinaris of Hierapolis, a contemporary, said the storm answered the prayers of Christian soldiers of the legion from Melitene, which the emperor then named the Thundering Legion; Tertullian, writing in 197, claimed a letter of Marcus himself attesting it (Eusebius, <em>Church History</em> 5.5.1–6; Tertullian, <em>Apology</em> 5). The legion, the Twelfth Fulminata, had in fact borne that name since at least Nero's reign. A letter in which Marcus credits the Christians and forbids their prosecution survives appended to Justin's <em>First Apology</em> and is a Christian forgery. Xiphilinus, epitomising Dio, broke into his source to insist that the Christians, not Arnuphis, had done it (72(71).9). Claudian, two centuries later, offered the choice between Chaldaean spells and the emperor's virtue, and inclined to the second (<em>On the Sixth Consulship of Honorius</em>).</p>
<aside class="dispute"><strong>Where sources disagree.</strong> Dio gives the miracle to Arnuphis and Hermes, the <em>Historia Augusta</em> to Marcus' prayer, Apollinaris and Tertullian to Christian soldiers; each version serves its teller. The date is also contested: the literary sources point to 174, but Péter Kovács (<em>Marcus Aurelius' Rain Miracle and the Marcomannic Wars</em>, 2009) argued from the scene's early place in the column's spiral that it happened in 171, and that no coin of Marcus can be tied to it. The reviewer of the book, Andy Fear (<em>Bryn Mawr Classical Review</em>, 2009), found the conclusion reasonable on its premises, which rest on reading the frieze as a chronological sequence.</aside>

<h2>175: Avidius Cassius and Faustina</h2>
<p>Gaius Avidius Cassius, a Syrian from Cyrrhus and the general who had taken Seleucia and Ctesiphon in the Parthian war, held a command over the whole East by 175. That spring, on a false report that Marcus had died, he had himself proclaimed emperor, and Egypt and most of Syria accepted him (Dio 72(71).22–23). Marcus made terms with the Iazyges (72(71).17), the Senate declared Cassius a public enemy (<em>Historia Augusta</em>, <em>Marcus</em> 24.9), and Dio gives the emperor a speech to his soldiers regretting only that he would be denied the chance to forgive (72(71).24–26). Before Marcus could march, a centurion named Antonius cut Cassius down; his dream of empire had lasted three months and six days (72(71).27). Marcus would not look at the head and had it buried (72(71).28; <em>Marcus</em> 25.3). He destroyed the rebels' papers unread, pardoned most of Cassius' supporters, and asked the Senate that no senator be put to death in his reign (Dio 72(71).28–30; <em>Marcus</em> 25.6).</p>
<p>The revolt drew Faustina into the story. Dio says she had secretly urged Cassius on, fearing that Marcus, then gravely ill, would die and leave the young Commodus without protection, and promised herself to Cassius as wife if he took the throne (72(71).22.3). The <em>Historia Augusta</em> says only that "some say" Cassius rose at her wish (<em>Marcus</em> 24.6).</p>
<aside class="dispute"><strong>Where sources disagree.</strong> Dio states Faustina's complicity as fact and gives her a plausible motive, a mother's fear for her son's succession if Marcus died. The <em>Historia Augusta</em> reports it as rumour, and its life of Avidius Cassius prints letters in which Faustina urges Marcus to punish the rebels without mercy, which would clear her; that life is among the most padded with invention (module 7.3), and the letters are generally held to be forged. Marcus himself, in book 1, thanks the gods for a wife so obedient, so affectionate and so simple (<em>Meditations</em> 1.17).</aside>
<p>Faustina had travelled with Marcus to the northern camps, and coins of the 170s honour her as Mother of the Camp. She went east with him after the revolt and died in the winter of 175 at Halala, a village in the foothills of the Taurus (<em>Marcus</em> 26.4). Dio offers gout or suicide to escape conviction for her compact with Cassius (72(71).29). Marcus asked the Senate to deify her, refounded Halala as a colony with a temple in her honour (<em>Marcus</em> 26.9), later known as Faustinopolis, and founded a new order of Faustinian girls, as Antoninus had for her mother. The Senate voted silver images and an altar at which brides were to sacrifice (Dio 72(71).31). The sexual rumours belong to the later sources: gladiators and sailors at Caieta, a Chaldaean prescription of blood that made Commodus a gladiator by birth, and Marcus' reported answer to those who urged divorce, that if he sent his wife away he must return her dowry, the empire (<em>Marcus</em> 19.1–9). The <em>Historia Augusta</em> itself says Marcus cleared her in his letters (23.7), and Dio's epitome says only that he tolerated her offences (72(71).34).</p>
<p>From the East Marcus came home by way of Athens, where in 176 he was initiated at Eleusis (<em>Marcus</em> 27.1) and endowed the chairs of philosophy and rhetoric (Greek module 7.4; deep dive module 13.3 reads what they show about the schools).</p>

<h2>177: Commodus Augustus and the martyrs of Lyon</h2>
<p>When Cassius rose, Marcus summoned Commodus from Rome and gave him the toga of manhood (Dio 72(71).22). Promotion followed fast. Commodus was hailed imperator, rode in his father's triumph on 23 December 176 (<em>Historia Augusta</em>, <em>Commodus</em> 12.4–5), entered the consulship on 1 January 177 aged fifteen, and in the course of the year became Augustus, a rank the coins and inscriptions of 177 attest, and his father's colleague in the tribunician power (<em>Marcus</em> 27.5). The revolt had shown what an emperor's death without an adult heir would mean, and the old fiction of adopting the best man had never applied to an emperor with a son (module 7.3). The renewed war in the north, Dio says, made Marcus marry him to Bruttia Crispina sooner than he wished (72(71).33).</p>
<p>In the same year, by Eusebius' date, the seventeenth of the emperor (<em>Church History</em> 5, preface 1), the Christians of Lyon and Vienne were arrested and killed. The churches' letter to the communities of Asia and Phrygia, quoted at length by Eusebius (5.1–2), is the earliest detailed account of a persecution in the West. A mob drove the Christians from baths and markets; the governor had them arrested and tortured. Vettius Epagathus, a young man of rank, offered to speak in their defence and was taken himself (5.1.9–10). The bishop Pothinus, more than ninety years old, died in prison two days after his interrogation (5.1.29–31). The deacon Sanctus of Vienne answered every question, name, city and status, with "I am a Christian" (5.1.20). Biblis, who had denied, recanted her denial under torture (5.1.25–26). Attalus of Pergamum was paraded with a placard reading "This is Attalus the Christian" until the governor learned he was a Roman citizen and wrote to the emperor (5.1.44).</p>
<p>The reply is the point at which Marcus enters the story. The emperor wrote that those who confessed should be put to death and those who denied released; the governor beheaded the citizens and sent the rest to the beasts at the great festival (5.1.47), the Phrygian physician Alexander among them (5.1.49–51). The slave woman Blandina, who had outlasted every torment, was killed last, tossed by a bull, after the fifteen-year-old Ponticus (5.1.53–56). The bodies were burned and the ashes swept into the Rhône (5.1.62). The letter never names the emperor; it says "Caesar", and by 177 Commodus too was Augustus. The reply simply applied Trajan's rule to Pliny (module 2.5): the name itself was punishable, and apostasy cleared it.</p>
<aside class="dispute"><strong>Where sources disagree.</strong> The <em>Church History</em> dates the persecution to 177, but Eusebius' own <em>Chronicle</em> puts it in the seventh year of the reign, 167; Timothy Barnes (<em>Early Christian Hagiography and Roman History</em>, 2010) argued that Eusebius' date may be wrong altogether, so that the persecution could even fall after Marcus' death. On responsibility, Tertullian called Marcus a protector of the Christians (<em>Apology</em> 5), and Paul Keresztes ("Marcus Aurelius a Persecutor?", <em>Harvard Theological Review</em>, 1968) argued that contemporaries such as Melito and Athenagoras addressed him with confidence and that the persecutions were local, not imperial policy. James Oliver and Robert Palmer ("Minutes of an Act of the Roman Senate", <em>Hesperia</em>, 1955), publishing the senatorial decree of the late 170s that cut the cost of gladiatorial games and let the priests of the Gallic provinces buy condemned criminals, <em>trinqui</em>, cheaply for the arena, suggested that the Christians of Lyon served as such victims; no source says so.</aside>
<p>Christians of his own day did petition him. Melito of Sardis complained of new decrees in Asia that let informers plunder Christians, and asked whether they were the emperor's (Eusebius, <em>Church History</em> 4.26.5–6); Athenagoras addressed his <em>Embassy for the Christians</em> to Marcus and Commodus together. The only mention of Christians in the <em>Meditations</em> sets the soul's readiness to die against their mere obstinacy (11.3), and the phrase may be a later gloss. Mill's charge against the persecuting emperor, and the Victorian argument over Lyon, are module 13.8's.</p>

<h2>Death, 180, and the image</h2>
<p>Marcus left for the second northern expedition in August 178, after hurling the bloodied spear from the temple of Bellona into enemy land in the old rite of war (Dio 72(71).33). He died on 17 March 180 (72(71).33), at Vindobona or at Sirmium (module 7.3 sets out the two claims). Dio gives his age as fifty-eight years, ten months and twenty-two days, his reign as nineteen years and eleven days (72(71).34). When the tribune asked for the watchword, Dio says, he answered: go to the rising sun, for I am already setting (72(71).34). The <em>Historia Augusta</em> has him rebuke his weeping friends for not thinking of the plague, and commend his son to them "if he proves worthy" and to the gods (<em>Marcus</em> 28.4–6); Herodian composes a deathbed speech entrusting the young emperor to his counsellors (1.4).</p>
<aside class="dispute"><strong>Where sources disagree.</strong> Dio, a senator who lived through the next reign, says Marcus died not of the illness he had but at the hands of his physicians, who wished to please Commodus (72(71).33). The <em>Historia Augusta</em> describes an illness he made worse by refusing food and drink in order to die, and its mention of the plague in his last words has suggested to many readers that the epidemic killed him (<em>Marcus</em> 28.3–4). Herodian (1.3–4) has a natural death after a long illness. No source names the disease.</aside>
<p>Dio closes with the verdict that Marcus survived and preserved the empire amid extraordinary difficulties, and that only one thing kept him from complete happiness: his son, with whose accession history descended from a kingdom of gold to one of iron and rust (72(71).36.3–4). The <em>Historia Augusta</em> goes further and claims Marcus foresaw what Commodus would be and wished him dead (<em>Marcus</em> 28.10). Gibbon put the case against him in one clause, that "he sacrificed the happiness of millions to a fond partiality for a worthless boy" (<em>Decline and Fall</em>, chapter 4), and reported it as an objection rather than his own verdict. The defence is that there was no choice: a son who had been Augustus for three years could not be passed over without civil war.</p>
<p>The images outlived the verdicts. The Senate and people hailed him as a god, and the <em>Historia Augusta</em>, written some two centuries later, says that his statues still stood among the household gods in many homes (<em>Marcus</em> 18.3–6). The column, finished by 193, and its frieze belong to modules 7.3 and 10.3. The gilded bronze horseman, his right hand stretched out in a gesture of address or clemency, survived the Middle Ages at the Lateran because it was taken for Constantine (module 10.4). In 1538 Pope Paul III had it moved to the Capitol, where Michelangelo designed the piazza around it; the original now stands indoors in the Capitoline Museums, a copy outside. It is the only bronze equestrian statue of a Roman emperor to survive whole, and it shows the bearded, heavy-lidded man of the late portraits, not the general of the column.</p>

<h2>Self-test</h2>
<ol class="quiz">
<li>What did Hadrian's nickname for Marcus mean, and where does it surface outside the <em>Historia Augusta</em>?<details><summary>Answer</summary><p><em>Verissimus</em>, "most truthful", a pun on Verus (<em>Marcus</em> 4.1). Justin Martyr addressed his <em>First Apology</em> to Antoninus and to his son Verissimus the philosopher (<em>First Apology</em> 1).</p></details></li>
<li>How did family ties make the adoptions of 138 possible, and what did Antoninus change in Marcus' marriage plans?<details><summary>Answer</summary><p>Marcus' aunt Galeria Faustina was Antoninus' wife, so Marcus was Antoninus' nephew. Antoninus broke Marcus' betrothal to Aelius Caesar's daughter and married him to his own daughter Faustina, Marcus' cousin (<em>Marcus</em> 6.2), in 145.</p></details></li>
<li>What does <em>Meditations</em> 1.16 show about how Marcus learned to rule?<details><summary>Answer</summary><p>It portrays Antoninus through habits rather than deeds: mildness, steadiness after deliberation, indifference to honours, love of work, openness to advice and criticism, frugality with public money. Marcus learned government in twenty-three years at Antoninus' side, apart only two nights (<em>Marcus</em> 7.2–3).</p></details></li>
<li>How were Fronto's letters recovered, and why does their survival matter?<details><summary>Answer</summary><p>Angelo Mai read them under the acts of the Council of Chalcedon in a palimpsest from Bobbio, publishing the Milan leaves in 1815 and the Vatican leaves in 1823. They are the only intimate correspondence of a Roman emperor, covering the pupil, the heir and the emperor.</p></details></li>
<li>Why does the identity of "Aristo" in Marcus' letter of about 146 matter?<details><summary>Answer</summary><p>If he is the Stoic Aristo of Chios (Hadot, Görgemanns), the letter records a conversion to philosophy; if the jurist Titius Aristo (Champlin, Birley), it records a turn from rhetoric to the study of law.</p></details></li>
<li>What did Rusticus give Marcus, according to <em>Meditations</em> 1.7, and how did he later meet a Christian apologist?<details><summary>Answer</summary><p>The sense that his character needed training, a distaste for rhetoric and sophistic display, plain letter-writing, careful reading, and Epictetus' lecture notes from his own library. As prefect of the city about 165 he condemned Justin Martyr (<em>Acts of Justin</em>).</p></details></li>
<li>How do the pagan and Christian versions of the rain miracle differ, and what weakens the Christian one?<details><summary>Answer</summary><p>Dio credits the Egyptian Arnuphis and Hermes (72(71).8), the <em>Historia Augusta</em> Marcus' prayers (<em>Marcus</em> 24.4); Apollinaris and Tertullian credit Christian soldiers of the Melitene legion (Eusebius, <em>Church History</em> 5.5). The legion already bore the name "Thundering", and the letter of Marcus supporting the claim is a forgery.</p></details></li>
<li>What does Dio allege about Faustina in 175, and why do historians hesitate?<details><summary>Answer</summary><p>That she secretly urged Avidius Cassius on, fearing for Commodus if Marcus died (72(71).22.3). The <em>Historia Augusta</em> calls it a rumour, the letters clearing her in its <em>Avidius Cassius</em> are forged, and Marcus' own tribute to his wife (<em>Meditations</em> 1.17) points the other way.</p></details></li>
<li>What was the emperor's ruling in the Lyon case, and why do historians dispute his responsibility?<details><summary>Answer</summary><p>Confessors were to die, those who denied to be freed (Eusebius, <em>Church History</em> 5.1.47), applying Trajan's rule. The letter names only "Caesar", Eusebius' two dates disagree (177 and 167), and the trigger may have been local: Oliver and Palmer linked the deaths to cheap criminals for the games after the decree on gladiatorial costs, while Keresztes argued Marcus was no persecutor by policy.</p></details></li>
<li>How do the sources explain Marcus' death, and what verdict does Dio pass on the reign that followed?<details><summary>Answer</summary><p>Dio blames physicians who wished to please Commodus (72(71).33); the <em>Historia Augusta</em> an illness he worsened by refusing food, near talk of the plague (<em>Marcus</em> 28.3–4). Dio says Commodus was the one flaw in his happiness and that with him history fell from gold to iron and rust (72(71).36.4).</p></details></li>
</ol>

<h2>Carry forward</h2>
<ol class="carry">
<li>Marcus was made by a household as much as a school: Hadrian's notice, Antoninus' daily example, Fronto's letters and Rusticus' correction. Book 1 of the <em>Meditations</em> is his own account of that making, and it lists characters, not doctrines.</li>
<li>The philosopher-emperor spent his reign at war, in revolt and in mourning, and the stories told about him (the rain miracle, Faustina's guilt, Commodus' parentage) were contested from the start by pagans, Christians and senators, each claiming him for their own case.</li>
<li>The adoptive ideal ended with the first emperor who had a son: Commodus' rapid promotion after 175 was a response to the danger the revolt exposed, and the reign's two dark marks for later readers, Lyon and the succession, both fall in 177.</li>
</ol>
