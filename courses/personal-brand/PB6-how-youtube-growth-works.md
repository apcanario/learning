# How YouTube growth works

## 1 · How YouTube says its recommendations work

Every YouTube page cited in this unit was opened on 12 November 2026, and every platform rule, metric definition, feature and benchmark carries that date unless another is given. The labels are PB1's: **platform-official** (YouTube's own help pages and blog), **executive statement** (a YouTube employee speaking, usually reported by the press), **vendor** (a company's dataset drawn from its own users or index), **correlational**, **survivor sample**, **derived** (arithmetic done here), and one more for this unit: **convention**, a rule this unit sets that no source sets.

The running example is Mara Venn (Mara, fictional): 38, a full-time accountant who restores mid-century furniture at weekends and wants to teach restoration to people who have no workshop. Her channel, One Box Restoration, publishes one 10-minute video of one piece every two weeks from Tuesday 5 January 2027, and she works only on her phone (PB1, PB2, PB4, PB5). Her numbers are illustrations, never evidence.

**What YouTube's help centre says.** YouTube's page on recommendations lists where they appear (the Home page, "Up Next" beside or after a video, the Shorts player, some shelves on destination pages and channel pages) and the signals it uses: watch history, search history, channel subscriptions, likes, dislikes, "Not interested" and "Don't recommend channel" choices, and satisfaction surveys, which it says help the system understand "satisfaction, not just watch time". It says the system learns from "over 80 billion pieces of information", that for Up Next the video being watched is the main signal, and that the Home page relies primarily on watch history ([YouTube Help, How YouTube recommendations work, answer 16089387](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/16089387?hl=en)). Platform-official. The page does not mention clicks, upload time or how a new channel is treated.

**The older, fuller account.** In September 2021 Cristos Goodrow, YouTube's VP of engineering, described the signals at more length: "Clicking on a video provides a strong indication that you will also find it satisfying", although "clicking on a video doesn't mean you actually watched it"; watch time ("which videos you watched and for how long") is a signal; YouTube measures "valued watchtime", where "Only videos that you rate highly with four or five stars are counted"; and "people are more likely to be satisfied by videos that they share or like" ([YouTube Official Blog, On YouTube's recommendation system, 15 Sep 2021](https://blog.youtube/inside-youtube/on-youtubes-recommendation-system/)). Platform-official, five years old; the 2026 help page names the same families of signal.

**What YouTube tells creators.** Its creator-facing page says "What matters is how viewers respond to each video when it's recommended to them", that "An individual video's underperformance does not penalize a channel overall", and that "Long-term channel performance can be affected, however, if a particular viewer consistently stops watching videos"; recommendations do not prioritise monetised videos, and "uploading a video as unlisted before making it public should not significantly impact its overall performance" ([YouTube Help, Good to know about recommendations, answer 16559651](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/16559651?hl=en)). Platform-official.

**What its head of discovery has said.** Todd Beaupré, who leads YouTube's growth and discovery team, said in 2024: "A lot of creators think of YouTube as pushing videos out to a bunch of people, but it's actually more the reverse"; "For the most part, the algorithm for Discovery is focused more on individual videos"; "We aim to not overemphasize historical data if that data isn't particularly predictive of future video performance"; and "If your last video wasn't so great and your next video is great, we want to realize the potential of each video" ([Search Engine Journal, 4 Mar 2024](https://www.searchenginejournal.com/youtube-algorithm-myths-debunked-insights-from-the-growth-team/510091/)). In an interview on Creator Insider (YouTube's channel for creator briefings) published 1 September 2026 he said "Each viewer gets a different ranking", and that inside the first hour "videos are reaching people who've never watched the channel before"; PPC Land's report paraphrases him as saying that a channel with no viewer history is matched on topic, and that if no interested audience appears, impressions stop ([PPC Land, 2 Sep 2026](https://ppc.land/subscribers-skip-90-of-uploads-in-their-feed-youtube-director-says/)). Executive statements reported by the press; the 2026 paraphrases are PPC Land's words.

**What this adds up to for a small channel.** On YouTube's own account, each video is offered to viewers one at a time, ranked for each viewer from what that viewer has watched, liked, dismissed and rated, and is judged by how those viewers respond. A channel's past matters only where it predicts the next video. YouTube publishes no weights, no thresholds and no account of how many impressions a new channel's first video gets; the topic match Beaupré describes is the only statement found about a channel with no history, and it is a paraphrase.

## 2 · Traffic sources: where a view starts and what each rewards

Studio sorts every view by where it started. YouTube's definitions ([YouTube Help, Understand your YouTube video reach, answer 1714921](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/1714921?hl=en-GB)), with what YouTube says decides each:

| Traffic source | YouTube's definition | What decides it, on YouTube's own pages |
|---|---|---|
| Browse features | "Traffic from the Home, Subscriptions, Watch Later, trending/Explore and other browsing features" | The per-viewer ranking; the Home page "relies primarily on watch history" (answer 16089387) |
| Suggested videos | "Traffic from suggestions that appear next to or after other videos and from links in video descriptions" | The video being watched is the main signal for Up Next (answer 16089387) |
| YouTube search | "Traffic from YouTube Search results" | Relevance, engagement and quality for the query (answer 16090438; §7) |
| Shorts | "Traffic from the Shorts vertical viewing experience" | A personalised feed of short vertical videos (answer 16089387; §5) |
| Notifications | "Traffic from notifications and emails sent to your subscribers" | Subscribers who opted in; Beaupré called notifications the only push mechanism (PPC Land's paraphrase, 2 Sep 2026) |
| Channel pages, playlists, end screens, external, direct or unknown | Your channel or others', playlists, creator end screens, sites and apps that embed or link, and "direct URL entry, bookmarks, signed-out viewers and unidentified apps" | What a viewer chose after arriving, or what happens off YouTube |

Read with §1, the large sources reward different things. Browse and Suggested reward a video that the viewers it is shown to go on to watch and rate well, whoever they are. Search rewards a video whose words match a query and whose searchers stay. Suggested also rewards being the obvious next video: among YouTube's reasons for an old video gaining views is "You released a new video in a series, prompting viewers to go back and watch older episodes" ([YouTube Help, YouTube performance FAQ, answer 141805](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/141805?hl=en)). Platform-official.

**Subscribers are one input among many.** Beaupré said that among subscribers "probably all your videos have about maybe a 10% or less click-through rate", that "even your best videos, 90% of the time, your subscribed audience isn't deciding to watch", and that "The number one place that people are watching videos from channels they're subscribed to" is the suggested list on the watch page, with the subscriptions feed small by comparison in PPC Land's paraphrase (PPC Land, 2 Sep 2026; executive statement, no sample given). YouTube's FAQ says "Viewers, on average, are subscribed to dozens of channels and may not return for every new upload" (answer 141805), and, for channels whose older subscribers have gone quiet, points creators to the Unique viewers metric in the Audience tab (answer 16559651). Platform-official.

**How big each source is for a channel like yours is unmeasured.** The only large split found is Metricool's, from 799,718 videos on 71,177 accounts comparing 2025 with 2026: "More than 61% of all organic views in the sample came from the Shorts feed", subscribers 11%, YouTube search 9%, related videos 5.77%, playlists 5.05% and channel page visits about 3%; browse is not reported, and the sources are not formally defined ([Metricool, YouTube Trends 2026, 29 Jul 2026, modified 4 Aug 2026](https://metricool.com/youtube-trends/)). Vendor data from Metricool's users, dominated by Shorts volume; it is not a long-form or small-channel split.

## 3 · Click-through rate and retention, as Studio reports them

**Click-through rate.** "The impressions click-through rate measures how often viewers watched a video after seeing a registered impression on YouTube"; impressions on external websites and end screens are not counted ([YouTube Help, Impressions and click-through rate FAQ, answer 7628154](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/7628154?hl=en-GB)). The Android reach page calls the pair "Thumbnail impressions" and "Thumbnail impressions click-through-rate" (answer 9314355). What the FAQ says about reading it, verbatim:

- "Half of all channels and videos on YouTube have an impressions CTR that can range between 2% and 10%."
- "New videos or channels (like those less than a week old) or videos with fewer than 100 views can see an even wider range."
- "It's also common for videos with fewer impressions and views to have higher click-through rates and average view duration."
- "If a video gets a lot of impressions (such as if it appears on the homepage), it's natural for the CTR to be lower."
- "Differences in click-through rate might be due to traffic sources, rather than the title or thumbnail."
- "it's best to compare CTRs between videos over the long term", and "Avoid checking your click-through rate immediately after uploading."
- "You can tell if your thumbnail is clickbait if it's getting high CTR but low average view duration and lower than expected Impressions."

Platform-official. YouTube publishes no target. The 2%–10% range describes where half of all channels and videos sit; it says nothing about where a channel should sit, and the FAQ itself says a low rate can come from wide reach. A search on 12 November 2026 found the same as PB1: no click-through dataset with a stated sample. The figure that circulates most, an average organic CTR of "4–6%", comes from a marketing agency that names its period (January to June 2026) and its data sources but no number of channels or videos ([Focus Digital, Average YouTube CTR, 11 Sep 2026](https://focus-digital.co/average-youtube-ctr-organic-paid-benchmarks-2026/)); vendor heuristic, no sample, and not used here.

**Retention.** Studio's retention report gives three readings:

- **Intro**: "what percentage of your audience still watched your video after the first 30 seconds" ([YouTube Help, Measure key moments for audience retention, answer 9314415](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/9314415?hl=en)).
- **Typical retention**: "the engagement that the last 10 of your videos of a similar length have maintained" ([YouTube Help, Content tab analytics tips, answer 12942217](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/12942217?hl=en)); a further click compares the video "to all YouTube videos of similar length" (answer 9314415).
- **Spikes and dips**: "Spikes are moments in your video that were rewatched or shared"; "Dips mean that viewers are abandoning or skipping at that specific part of your video". These key moments need a video "at least 60 seconds long" with "at least 100 views" (answer 9314415).

YouTube's Content tab tips add that "A typical intro is about 30 seconds long" and that "Videos that have 50% of the audience or more watching after the 30-second mark" appear in an "above typical intros" list (answer 12942217). That 50% is where YouTube draws a list, which PB5 §3 also noted; it is not called a target. Platform-official.

For **average percentage viewed**, the only external bands found are vidIQ's: "5 to 15 minutes: aim for 40 to 55% viewed", presented as "vidIQ guidance for target bands", with the advice that your "best baseline is your own comparable library" ([vidIQ, YouTube Average View Duration, updated 21 Jul 2026](https://vidiq.com/blog/post/average-view-duration/)). Vendor heuristic; no sample is given for the bands. The same article reports vidIQ's own long-form videos averaging 30.3% viewed across 16.5 million views from 26 June 2025 to 25 June 2026, below its own band; that is one channel, and it shows how loosely the band describes even a large, practised channel. PB1 row Y3 uses the band; it stays a heuristic.

**Which of the two YouTube weighs when they disagree.** Its own testing tool says so: Test & Compare will "optimize tests for overall watch time over other metrics, like click-through-rate" ([YouTube Help, A/B test titles & thumbnails, answer 16391400](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/16391400?hl=en)). Platform-official. With the clickbait sentence above, YouTube's position is that a click which leads to little watching is worth less than fewer clicks that lead to more.

The FAQ's sentences give a reading order for one upload, drawn below. The branches are YouTube's; comparing an upload "against your own uploads" is this unit's convention, because YouTube publishes no absolute line.

**Testing packaging on a phone.** Test & Compare tests "up to 3 different titles and thumbnails", is "currently only available on computers through YouTube Studio", needs YouTube's advanced features enabled (a channel setting the creator turns on in Studio), cannot test Shorts, and may return "Not enough impressions" or "Inconclusive", in which case "the first title and thumbnail you upload will be the default title and thumbnail for your video" (answer 16391400). A channel at the views PB1 expects (row Y1: a median of about 180 to 272 thirty-day views on uploads 1 to 10, from a survivor sample) is likely to get inconclusive results (inference). A creator who works only on a phone, as Mara does (PB5 §7), cannot run it at all. What remains is the check YouTube's own pages support: read each upload's click-through rate, impressions and Intro at a checkpoint, and change a package only where the numbers point to the package. YouTube says "Changing a video's title or thumbnail can lead to a shift in its performance, potentially resulting in more or fewer views", that such changes "are recommended when a video has a lower click-through rate (CTR) and is receiving fewer impressions than usual", and that "it's advised not to change what is already working well" (answer 16559651). Platform-official. One upload changed and compared before and after is a test with one subject and no control; it is recorded as such.

## 4 · How long a long-form video keeps working

**What YouTube says.** "Many viewers don't watch videos in chronological order or decide what to watch based on when a video was published"; "Our recommendation system aims to deliver the right videos to the right viewers, regardless of when that video was uploaded"; and an older video may gain views because "The topic your video is about is increasing in popularity", "New viewers are discovering your channel and 'binging' your old videos", "More viewers are choosing to watch your video when it's offered to them in recommendations", or a new episode of a series sent viewers back (answer 141805). Beaupré, in PPC Land's paraphrase, said some videos peak on day one and others build most of their audience a month or more after publication (PPC Land, 2 Sep 2026). YouTube's search page names no upload-date factor (answer 16090438), so a query typed in two years' time is matched on the same terms (inference from the page). Platform-official; executive statement reported by the press.

**Watch hours keep counting.** For the Partner Programme, YouTube counts "Qualified watch hours gained from long-form videos that you've set to public"; hours from private, unlisted or deleted videos and from Shorts do not count ([YouTube Help, YouTube Partner Programme overview and eligibility, answer 72851](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/72851?hl=en-GB)). From 1 February 2027 a new applicant needs "8,000 qualified watch hours in the last 365 days, or 20M qualified Shorts views in the last 90 days", with 1,000 subscribers ([YouTube Help, Changes to the YouTube Partner Program, answer 12843009](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/12843009)). Platform-official. Every public long-form video adds to a rolling 365-day total for as long as it is watched; deleting or unlisting one removes its hours.

**What the vendor data shows about the launch window.** Metricool's sample: videos usually peak in their first three days and about 83% of interactions arrive within ten days, while YouTube content "keeps earning views for months" (Metricool, 29 Jul 2026). Vendor data; interactions, which are not views, and no share of later views is given. vidIQ measured first-week views on 38,229,639 long-form videos published 15 August to 5 September 2026: the median was 234, 37.7% had fewer than 100 and 70.1% fewer than 1,000, and the top 1% of videos took 54.1% of all first-week views, the next 9% took 34.6%, the next 40% took 10.8% and the bottom 50% took 0.5% ([vidIQ Research, YouTube first-week views, 16 Sep 2026](https://vidiq.com/research/youtube-first-week-views/)). vidIQ says the cohort is "not every upload on YouTube or a representative census", that the data "does not establish algorithmic causes", and that "results are not adjusted for channel size". Vendor data; long-form only; one three-week cohort.

**What is not measured.** No source found gives the share of a small channel's views that come from videos older than a few weeks, or how long a long-form video keeps drawing views on a channel of any size. The mechanism (old videos stay eligible, search matches whenever a query is typed, public hours keep counting) is platform-official; its size is unmeasured. The concentration in the vidIQ cohort says something about the odds for one upload: half of all long-form videos shared 0.5% of first-week views, so a typical upload's first week is small, and most of whatever a channel gains in a year can come from a few uploads (PB1 §2 reached the same inference from the first-ten study). Row YT8 in the deliverable measures the back catalogue on your own channel from your second checkpoint.

## 5 · Shorts: what they do for subscribers and long-form views

The question is whether time spent on Shorts brings subscribers and long-form viewers to a long-form channel. The evidence runs both ways, and none of it measures the transfer itself.

**For Shorts.**

- YouTube: "Experimenting with new content formats, such as Shorts, VODs, or livestreams, will not inherently confuse the algorithm"; "a viewer that often engages with your long-form videos might see your Shorts recommended"; and "Any perceived effect on a channel from new formats is typically due to how viewers react" (answer 16559651). Platform-official; this describes long-form viewers seeing Shorts, the reverse of the transfer in question.
- The link exists: a creator can "add a related video after you upload your Short to help direct viewers to your other YouTube content"; it "is only available with advanced feature access", and the steps start in YouTube Studio ([YouTube Help, Add a related video to your YouTube Shorts, answer 14075157](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/14075157?hl=en)). YouTube's own guide to the feature, 14 July 2026, cites "billions of daily views" in the Shorts feed and gives no figure for how many viewers follow the link ([YouTube Official Blog, related videos traffic guide, 14 Jul 2026](https://blog.youtube/creator-and-artist-stories/youtube-related-videos-traffic-guide/)). Platform-official; the rate is unpublished.
- Metricool: channels posting around five Shorts a month averaged 8,789 views per long-form video, against a global average of 5,985 and 4,364 for channels posting no Shorts; channels posting 35 or more a month fell back to about the level of those posting none; Metricool adds "Correlation isn't causation here, and channels that post Shorts may simply be more consistent overall" (Metricool, 29 Jul 2026). Vendor data; correlational.
- Reach per upload: in vidIQ's first-ten study, the median Short drew about 1,362 views in 30 days on a new channel's first upload and 1,344 on its tenth, against about 180 and 272 for long-form ([vidIQ, YouTube First 10 Videos Study, 28 Jul 2026, updated 30 Jul 2026](https://vidiq.com/research/youtube-first-10-videos-study/)). vidIQ says the formats "are shown on separate scales and are not directly compared to each other", that "Views are not subscriber counts", and that channels that gave up before ten videos "are excluded entirely". Vendor data; survivor sample.

**Against, or no difference.**

- AIR Media-Tech, from 18,000 English-language channels with at least 40 videos and 10 long-form videos between March 2025 and March 2026, compared median subscribers of long-only and mixed channels: "Home and DIY shows near-identical performance for Long-Only and Long+Short Mix across most segments", 95,100 against 94,600 subscribers at 100,000 to 1 million monthly views and 251,500 against 256,000 at 1 to 10 million ([AIR Media-Tech, Do YouTube Shorts help your long-form videos?, 28 May 2026](https://air.io/en/audience-growth/do-youtube-shorts-help-your-long-form-videos-grow-data-from-18000-channels)). The page says "this is correlational data" and "This dataset measured subscriber counts, not cross-format viewing rates directly". Vendor data; correlational; channels far larger than a new one.
- OverseerOS compared 370 long-form videos on 37 channels before and after each channel's first Short: age-adjusted, the ratio of after to before was 1.021, with 19 of 37 channels improving, and it "found no consistent evidence that simply adding Shorts automatically damages long-form YouTube performance"; a stricter subset of 16 channels showed a ratio of 0.754, so it does not claim Shorts have no effect; it cannot see impressions, click-through, retention or traffic sources, and its channels chose when to start ([OverseerOS, Do YouTube Shorts hurt long-form views?, 1 Sep 2026](https://www.overseeros.com/blog/do-youtube-shorts-hurt-long-form-views)). Vendor data; small sample; no subscriber finding.
- Shorts do not build the long-form watch hours the Partner Programme counts: "any qualified watch hours from Shorts views in the Shorts feed won't count" (answer 72851). Platform-official.
- Shorts' own reach does not grow across a new channel's first ten: vidIQ calls it "no incremental growth, in fact a slight decline over time" (vidIQ, 28 Jul 2026). And a Short's life is short: OverseerOS followed 9,982 Shorts at least 30 days old for about eight days, and "Among 1,546 Shorts starting below 1,000 views, none gained another 1,000 views during that interval" ([OverseerOS, Do YouTube Shorts get views after 30 days?, 8 Nov 2026](https://www.overseeros.com/blog/do-youtube-shorts-get-views-after-30-days)); the authors say "The research corpus is selected, not random." Vendor data.

**Not measured.** The share of Shorts viewers who go on to watch the same creator's long-form, and whether subscribers gained through Shorts watch long-form. AIR states a "0.04% to 0.5% Shorts-to-long-form conversion" for channels that set up the link well; the page gives no definition, method, source or sample for it (AIR, 28 May 2026), so it is not used.

**For Mara.** PB3 gave Shorts no hours at 6 hours a week, and PB4 and PB5 left that for this unit to revisit. The evidence here does not reverse it. The one transfer that would justify the hours is unmeasured; the dataset that covers her niche finds no subscriber difference between mixed and long-only channels; Shorts-feed hours do not count towards the watch-hour line PB1 sets against her (row Y10); and her plan already uses 24.0 of 24 hours per four weeks (PB3, D2). PB5's Reels (30 to 60 seconds, vertical) could also be uploaded to YouTube at little editing cost; she does not do it in year one (Mara, fictional), because each upload also needs a title, a description and replies to its comments, which her budget has no hours for, and because a Short's views would sit in the same Studio totals as her long-form and blur the per-video reads her verdicts depend on (her rule, convention). The twelve-month review (PB10) may test Shorts for one season if her logged hours show slack. A reader with 10 hours a week, or one whose positioning favours short pieces, has a different budget and the same evidence: the reach per Short is real (vidIQ), the transfer to long-form is unmeasured, and a one-season test read on the Shorts traffic source and subscribers gained is the only way to find out.

## 6 · Cadence: what the evidence says

**What YouTube says.** "We studied thousands of channels that took a break and found no correlation between break length and changes in views", though "it may take some time to 'warm up' your audience again"; and, asked whether posting more often helps, "No, we've done analyses over the years and found that growth in views across uploads is not correlated with time between uploads", with "Many creators have established reliable connections with their audience through quality over quantity" (answer 141805; PPC Land reports a German-language FAQ posted in the YouTube Help community on 2 September 2026, alongside Help Center pages on the Audience tab and on performance troubleshooting, [PPC Land, 2 Sep 2026](https://ppc.land/youtube-finds-no-link-between-upload-breaks-and-view-counts/); the English page's own date was not seen). YouTube also says "To grow regular viewers, publish content regularly and maintain a strong connection with your audience" ([YouTube Help, New, casual, & regular viewers tips, answer 13615784](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/13615784?hl=en)). Platform-official; no sample, period or method for the analyses.

**What the vendor data says.**

- vidIQ, 32,926,088 channels under 1,000 subscribers compared with themselves 360 days later: 0.20% of those that added no videos crossed 1,000, 1.86% of those that added 1 to 11, 8.65% of those that added 12 to 51 and 30.77% of those that added 52 or more; "This is an observational association, not proof that uploading caused subscriber growth", and the counts do not separate Shorts, long-form and livestreams ([vidIQ Research, Reaching 1,000 YouTube Subscribers, 11 Sep 2026](https://vidiq.com/research/youtube-uploads-1000-subscribers/)). Vendor data; correlational. PB1 row Y4.
- AIR Media-Tech, 18,000 English-language channels in the US, UK, Canada and Australia, primary analysis at 100,000 to 1 million monthly views: in Home and DIY, median subscribers were 83,050 at under 2 uploads a month (116 channels), 114,000 at 2 to 5 (178), 105,000 at 5 to 10 (130) and 90,700 at 10 to 20 (87); channels that "went dark for 14+ days three or more times a year" got fewer views per video, and "Channels that posted on a predictable schedule got more views per subscriber" than channels posting as many videos on random days; "This is correlational data, not causal" ([AIR Media-Tech, Does posting more on YouTube help?, 8 Jun 2026](https://air.io/en/audience-growth/does-posting-more-on-youtube-help-we-studied-18000-channels-to-find-out)). Vendor data; correlational; channels far larger than a new one; no effect size for the gap finding.
- Metricool: long-form at 2 to 4 posts a week gave the best results per post (about 10,552 views), and posting less than once a week gave the weakest monthly result (Metricool, 29 Jul 2026). Vendor data from Metricool's users; no account-size split for this finding.

**Weighing them.** The two kinds of source answer different questions. YouTube's analyses say that the gap between uploads does not predict how a channel's views change across them. The vendor data says that channels which publish more, and more regularly, have more subscribers and views, among channels that kept going and with no control for why they posted more. Both can hold at once: if each upload is another draw from a distribution where the top 1% take over half the first-week views (§4), a channel with more uploads has more chances at a rare large one, with no penalty attached to the gaps (this unit's inference; no source tests it). Nothing found shows that a fortnightly long-form channel is held back by its rate as such, and nothing shows that a weekly one would do better for the same person with the same hours.

Mara keeps one video every two weeks (Mara, fictional; PB1, PB3), for the reason PB4 §2 gave: PB1's stop rule and its base rates are counted in uploads per quarter, and 26 a year places her in vidIQ's 12–51 band. A weekly cadence would move her into the 52-plus band, whose 30.77% belongs to channels that may also have had more time, money or audience (PB1 §2), and PB3 priced it at 10 hours a week. When a week is lost, PB4's rules apply (§7, D1): no catch-up video, never two videos in one week.

## 7 · Titles and search for a small channel

**How search ranks.** YouTube names three elements, whose importance "may vary by search type": relevance, where it looks at "how well the title, tags, description, and video content match your search query"; engagement, such as "the watch time of a particular video for a particular query"; and quality, signals of "which channels demonstrate expertise, authoritativeness, and trustworthiness on a given topic". Results may also use the searcher's history, and "YouTube doesn't accept payment for better placement within organic search results" ([YouTube Help, How YouTube search works, answer 16090438](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/16090438?hl=en)). Platform-official. The page says nothing about channel size or upload date.

Three consequences for a small channel follow from the page itself. A video can be found by a stranger on its words alone, with no subscribers. The engagement signal is per query, so a video that holds the people who searched for its problem is the one that keeps the place. And the quality signal is about a channel's standing on a topic, which is one reason a channel that stays on one topic (PB2's positioning) is better placed in search than one that moves around (inference from the page; YouTube gives no weight). YouTube's creator page says the same thing in other words: "Viewers are naturally drawn to channels that have already demonstrated expertise or a clear niche", and for new channels "A key factor here is developing a critical mass of content" (answer 16559651).

**Tags.** "tags play a minimal role in your video's discovery", and are useful mainly where the video's subject "is commonly misspelled"; the title, thumbnail and description matter more ([YouTube Help, Add tags to videos, answer 146402](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/146402?hl=en)). Platform-official.

**What the title data says.** AIR Media-Tech coded the titles of 18,080 English-language channels at 100,000 to 100 million monthly views across 11 niches, measuring median engagement rate per video ((likes plus comments) divided by views) and the 75th-percentile views. It reports 30 to 50 characters as "the universal sweet spot", titles of 90 or more characters underperforming on views, with search-optimised content a partial exception, and advises putting the main keyword in the first 40% of the title for search-driven content and leading with the hook for browse; for Home and DIY, titles naming the finished project did better than titles describing the process ([AIR Media-Tech, How to write a YouTube title that gets clicked, 12 Jun 2026](https://air.io/en/audience-growth/how-to-write-a-youtube-title-that-gets-clicked-research-across-11-niches)). The authors say "This is correlational data" and that they "cannot prove the title alone caused the result"; titles were not separated from thumbnails, the period of the title data is not stated, and the outcome is engagement and views, not click-through rate. Vendor data; channels far larger than a new one.

**Your own search data.** Studio's reach report lists "what terms viewers searched when they found your content" (answer 9314355). For a new channel it is the only evidence of which words strangers use for its subject; no source found gives search volumes for YouTube queries that a creator can open without a paid tool.

**For Mara.** PB5 §7 set her title rule: it says what happens to which piece, every word true of the first 30 seconds. Her first title, "Restoring a teak side table with white water rings, with tools that fit in one box" (PB5), is 82 characters, under AIR's 90, and the piece's name starts at character 13, inside the first 40% (derived: 33 of 82). The search rule this unit adds (Mara, fictional; convention, from answer 16090438 and AIR's guidance): the piece and its fault, in the words a buyer of that piece would type, come before the method, and the description's first two sentences repeat them; from the Q2 review she reads the search terms report and carries any term strangers used into the next title of the same kind of piece.

## 9 · Common growth claims for which no source was found

Each of these was searched for on 12 November 2026; none has a source that meets this course's standard, and several have a platform statement against them.

1. **A "good" click-through rate of 4–6%, or any target.** YouTube publishes none; the most-cited figure has no stated sample (§3).
2. **YouTube shows a new video to subscribers first and judges it on them.** Beaupré said the opposite: within the first hour videos reach "people who've never watched the channel before" (§1).
3. **A break, or a slower cadence, is penalised.** YouTube says it found no correlation between break length and changes in views; AIR's gap finding is correlational (§6).
4. **Deleting or unlisting weak videos lifts the channel.** No source; YouTube says one video's underperformance "does not penalize a channel overall", and deleted or unlisted videos lose their qualified watch hours (§1, §4).
5. **A Shorts-to-long-form conversion rate of 0.04% to 0.5%.** Stated by a vendor with no method (§5).
6. **YouTube changed its ranking formula in September 2025 to "satisfied watch time per click".** An analyst's reading (Mario Joos, on X, 30 Nov 2025) reported by PPC Land, 2 Dec 2025; YouTube gave no explanation ([PPC Land, 2 Dec 2025](https://ppc.land/youtube-creators-report-drops-in-shorts-viewership-after-september-algorithm-change/)).
7. **Tags drive discovery.** YouTube says they play "a minimal role" (§7).
8. **Publishing at a particular hour lifts a video's views.** YouTube: "Publish time is not known to impact a video's long-term performance", although publishing when the audience is active "can be beneficial for early viewership" (answer 141805).
9. **A minimum length makes a video rank.** No source; Beaupré's remark that people watching on television "want longer videos" (PPC Land, 2 Sep 2026) gives no length and no ranking rule.
10. **Subscribers or regular viewers raise a video's reach.** YouTube: "Audience segments do not affect reach or monetization" ([YouTube Help, Understand new, casual, & regular viewers, answer 10246996](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/10246996?hl=en)); subscribers are one signal among many (§2).
