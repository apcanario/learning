# DD-SP2 · Deep dive: weight, body and who decides

This unit is information, not legal, medical or psychological advice. It teaches what a coach may do about weight and body: keep their own language clean, keep weighing out of the club's everyday life, run a decision so that the athlete makes it, and refer. It teaches no assessment, no diagnosis and no treatment, and it gives no weight-loss method, no calorie figure and no target weight, in the text, the examples or the scripts. When anything about eating, weight or body worries you (unless the athlete is acutely unwell, which calls for emergency help at once: section 17), your part ends when you have passed it, without comment to the athlete on their food or body, to the person your referral plan names for medical concerns (SP9); that person, and through them a doctor, a sports dietitian or a licensed or registered mental health professional, takes over. Where a legal duty may apply to you (consent for a minor, the records you keep), the unit puts it as a question and names the kind of body that can answer it.

Every technique and recommendation carries a grade in square brackets from SP1's grade key (section 9 of SP1, unchanged: five rules applied in order; "several" means three or more; the labels *direction* and *inference*). *Inference* marks evidence from adults, students or non-athletes applied to an athlete under 18, as SP1 words it. Where the only source opened is a consensus statement whose studies were not opened, the grade is [practice] with the note "the studies behind it were not opened", as in SP2 to DD-SP1. Age bands are SP2's: about 10 to 12, 13 to 15, 16 to 18 and 19 to 25. Where a claim rests on an abstract alone, the citation says "abstract read". Every federation page carries the date it was read: 19 Nov 2026 for all of them. Three statements this unit was asked to use could not be opened: the American Academy of Pediatrics' clinical report on weight control in young athletes (2017), the American College of Sports Medicine's consensus on weight loss in weight-category sports (2021) and the IOC's 2013 position statement on weight-sensitive sports. Their publishers' pages refused the request or were not returned by search, at writing and again at a second search on 19 Nov 2026, so nothing in this unit rests on them; section 6 says so where the question is asked.

The three case athletes return: Dani (fictional), 14, judo, at the top of the weight class she won a national title in at 13, after growing 9 cm in a year; her coach wants her to stay in that class one more season, and she has said once, quietly, that she wants to move up. She has started skipping the snack after school on training days; SP6 passed that concern to the club's person for medical concerns, who has not yet seen her. Milan (fictional), 17, plays football in an academy; Sara (fictional), 20, runs the 400 metres. They illustrate; they are never evidence.

## 1. Where weight or shape bears on the result

In some sports a body's mass or shape is part of the rules or part of the score. Researchers group them in three kinds, and the grouping matters because the pressures differ:

- **Weight-class sports**, where an athlete competes against others of the same body mass, checked at a weigh-in: judo, wrestling, boxing, taekwondo, weightlifting, lightweight rowing.
- **Aesthetic or judged sports**, where judges score how a movement looks: artistic and rhythmic gymnastics, figure skating, diving, artistic swimming, dance.
- **Endurance and gravitational sports**, where the athlete moves their own body over distance or against gravity: distance running, road cycling, cross-country skiing, ski jumping, climbing.

The sports named in each group are this unit's examples.

A Lithuanian survey of 1,003 competitive athletes aged 15 to 44 sorted sports in a similar way (aesthetic weight-sensitive; other weight-sensitive, which combined endurance, gravitational and weight-class sports; and less weight-sensitive sports such as ball games, fencing and shooting) and found that female athletes in aesthetic sports reported more body-related pressure from coaches than those in less weight-sensitive sports, and weighed themselves more often ([Baceviciene et al., 2023, cross-sectional, full text read](https://doi.org/10.3390/nu15122724)). In interviews with nine elite athletes aged 19 to 44, mostly from endurance sports, one put the belief plainly: "If you're light and you're thin, you're expected to be fast." (a participant in [Stoyel et al., 2021](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2021.633490)).

Whether athletes in these sports have more eating problems than anyone else is less certain than coaching culture assumes. A systematic review of 15 studies of young elite athletes, aged about 12 to 25, concluded that there is no certainty that pathogenic weight-control behaviour is more common in them than in non-athletes, except possibly in leanness sports, and that competitive pressure appears to be a risk factor for both sexes ([Werner et al., 2013, systematic review, full text read](https://doi.org/10.1186/2050-2974-1-18)). The review's own results and conclusions do not fully agree on the leanness-sport point. What the studies do agree on is that the pressure exists, that coaches are one of its sources (section 5), and that in weight-class sports a large share of young athletes cut weight before competing (section 6).

The other sports are not exempt. A basketball player told to "get stronger" or a rugby player told to "bulk up" is hearing a comment about their body too (section 16), and comments about appearance reach athletes in any sport.

## 2. Relative energy deficiency in sport, as the IOC defines it

The IOC's 2023 consensus statement describes Relative Energy Deficiency in Sport (REDs) as "a syndrome of deleterious health and performance outcomes" in female and male athletes, linked to low energy availability: too little energy taken in for what training uses. The statement reports more data on the effects of low energy availability in males, "further evidence of the interplay between mental health and REDs", and introduces "A new REDs Clinical Assessment Tool-Version 2" ([Mountjoy et al., 2023, consensus, abstract read on PubMed](https://doi.org/10.1136/bjsports-2023-106994)). The tool is for clinicians. Your part is to notice and pass on (section 17); you do not use it.

The abstract opened does not say what REDs does to growth, bone or mood in adolescents, so the unit takes that from a second consensus. The Female Athlete Triad Coalition's 2025 update, voted by a 16-person panel (a statement was kept only with more than 85% agreement and no vote against), introduces a model for adolescent girls. It says that "Energy deficiency in adolescent female athletes can result in delayed menarche or primary amenorrhea" (no periods by the expected age), that adolescent girls may be more severely affected because of possible disruption to the building of bone mineral during these years, and that eating disorders and disordered eating "may lead to growth restriction", particularly when intense training begins before puberty ([De Souza et al., 2026, consensus, abstract and sections 1 to 3.3 read](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-025-02333-z)). A narrative review of weight cycling in combat sports states that "weight cycling in youth and adolescents can negatively impact growth development" ([Lakicevic et al., 2021, narrative review, full text read](https://doi.org/10.1186/s13102-021-00381-2); a narrative review is outside the course's source types, and no grade rests on it).

What this means for a coach of 10- to 18-year-olds:

- **Growth and bone.** The consensus names delayed or absent periods and possible disruption of bone mineral accrual as consequences of energy deficiency in adolescent girls, and growth restriction as a possible consequence of disordered eating (De Souza et al., 2026). The evidence for boys of the same age was not opened for this unit.
- **Mood.** The IOC statement links REDs and mental health, without giving the direction in the abstract read (Mountjoy et al., 2023). SP9 already sends low mood and eating concerns by its route.
- **Performance.** The IOC defines REDs by its health *and* performance outcomes. An athlete who is underfuelled to hold a class may lose more than the class gains them.

What to do with it: treat any sign of under-eating, any change in periods an athlete or parent mentions, and any stress fracture as a medical matter for the person your plan names, the same day, without comment on food or body [practice; consensus; the studies behind it were not opened; the course's safety rule]. You do not ask about periods; if an athlete or parent tells you, you pass it on the same day, telling them that you are doing so and to whom; you do not promise to keep it to yourself.

## 3. Eating disorders and disordered eating, and the risk in adolescence

**Two terms, kept apart.** An eating disorder is a diagnosis a clinician makes. Disordered eating is the wider set of behaviours: a clinical review of eating disorders in athletes describes such behaviours (restricting food or bingeing, among others) occurring "even in the absence of a formal eating disorder diagnosis", and notes, in its abstract, that the risk is greater in sports where low body weight or leanness gives an advantage ([Joy et al., 2016, narrative review, full text read](https://doi.org/10.1136/bjsports-2015-095735)). Neither is yours to name. You notice behaviour; a clinician decides what it is.

**What the studies opened for this unit found in young athletes.** All are cross-sectional surveys: they count what athletes report at one time, they cannot show cause, and a questionnaire score above a cut-off means "at risk", not a diagnosis.

- In 999 French-Canadian athletes aged 14 to 17, 16.9% reported having used extreme weight-control behaviours during their sporting career: 19.75% of girls, 9.7% of boys, and 44% of those in weight-class sports ([Boudreault et al., 2022, cross-sectional, full text read](https://doi.org/10.1177/10126902211018672)). Bands 13 to 15 and 16 to 18.
- In 462 athletes aged 12 to 25 from school and local clubs in six European countries (59.7% under 18), 14.9% on one questionnaire and 19.9% on another were at potential risk of an eating disorder, more girls and women than boys and men on both; 44.6% were dissatisfied with their weight and 46.1% believed they needed to lose weight to perform better ([Kenđel Jovanović & Čulina, 2024, cross-sectional, full text read](https://doi.org/10.3389/fnut.2024.1398464)).
- In Greek female gymnasts aged 11 to 17, 16.3% of 147 competitive gymnasts and 7.4% of 122 recreational gymnasts scored at or above the questionnaire's cut-off for disordered eating ([Kontele et al., 2022, cross-sectional, abstract read](https://doi.org/10.3390/children9020254)). Ages 11 to 17 span bands 10 to 12, 13 to 15 and 16 to 18.
- In the Lithuanian survey, adolescent female athletes reported more of several disordered-eating behaviours than adult female athletes, while adult male athletes reported more dietary restraint than adolescent males (Baceviciene et al., 2023).
- SP9 reported a scoping review of elite youth athletes (mean age 12 to 17) in which the proportion above a level at which treatment is typically indicated ranged from 0% to 14% in boys and 11% to 41% in girls for disordered eating (SP9, section 1).

**Adolescence.** Across these studies girls aged about 13 to 18 report more disordered eating than boys of the same age, and adolescent girls report more of some behaviours than adult women. A systematic review of 31 studies found the same pattern by age in female athletes and the reverse in males, with adult men reporting more eating and body concerns than adolescent boys ([Li et al., 2024, systematic review, full text read](https://doi.org/10.3390/nu16162686)). For a club squad of 11- to 17-year-olds, both sexes are in scope; the girls' figures are higher, and the boys' are not zero.

What to do: watch for SP9's signs (skipped meals or snacks, talk about body shape or weight, extra training on top of the plan) and, at the first one, pass it on by your referral plan, with no comment on food, weight or body to the athlete [practice; SP9, section 4; the course's safety rule]. Dani's (fictional) skipped snack went this way in SP6. That is the whole of your part in it.

## 4. Body image, body dissatisfaction and body dysmorphic disorder

Three things share vocabulary and are different.

**Body image** is how a person sees, thinks and feels about their own body. **Body dissatisfaction** is the negative end of it: wanting a different weight, shape or size. In this course both are words for what studies measure with questionnaires; neither is a diagnosis (this unit's working description, not a clinical definition). Body dissatisfaction is common in adolescence and among athletes: 44.6% of the 12- to 25-year-old athletes above were dissatisfied with their current weight (Kenđel Jovanović & Čulina, 2024).

**Body dysmorphic disorder** is a clinical diagnosis. This unit defines it once, from the proposals of the World Health Organization's working group for the eleventh revision of the International Classification of Diseases (ICD-11), as a 2014 paper describing the working group's proposals reports them: a preoccupation with one or more perceived defects or flaws in appearance that are "either unnoticeable to others or only slightly noticeable", taking up a great deal of time, with repetitive behaviours (checking, comparing, seeking reassurance) or avoidance, and, as the same paper says of the diagnostic manuals, symptoms that are "either significantly distressing or interfering with one's life". The authors state that "A preoccupation predominantly focused on being "too fat" or overweight is not part of BDD": such a preoccupation belongs with the eating disorders ([Veale & Matsunaga, 2014, full text read at the publisher's page](https://doi.org/10.1590/1516-4446-2013-1238)). In young people, they write, the disorder sits on a continuum from ordinary adolescent self-consciousness. In the rest of this unit "body dysmorphic disorder" means only that clinical diagnosis.

**Why the difference matters to you.** A 15-year-old who dislikes her thighs in a leotard has body dissatisfaction, which is common; it is not a disorder and you do not call it one. Body dysmorphic disorder is diagnosed by a clinician and is never named by a coach. What you can see in either case is behaviour: avoiding the changing room, hiding in baggy kit, checking mirrors, asking repeatedly whether something looks wrong. That behaviour, if it is new or distressing against the athlete's baseline, goes by SP9's route like any other sign [practice; SP1's scope line].

**What the evidence says about body dissatisfaction and eating disorders.** The systematic review of 31 studies (11,184 participants, gymnasts, figure skaters, cheerleaders, wrestlers, rowers, rugby, basketball and martial arts among them) found several studies in which body dissatisfaction predicted eating disorders, others in which athletes satisfied with their bodies still showed disordered eating, and concluded that "the relationship between athletes' body image and eating disorders is complex and inconclusive" (Li et al., 2024). The studies were not pooled. SP9 quoted the IOC mental health consensus calling body-image dissatisfaction possibly "the strongest predictor of eating disorders in athletes" (SP9, section 4). The two are compatible: dissatisfaction is a risk factor in many studies, and its absence is no reassurance.

**Pubertal timing and body image.** The brief asks what maturation does to body image. SP2 could open no source on it, and the one systematic review found for this unit (on body dissatisfaction and puberty in adolescence, 2026) failed at its publisher's site. That part is unsourced here.

## 5. What the research found about coaches' comments on weight and shape

This is the section where your behaviour is the variable. The studies are cross-sectional or qualitative: they show that coach weight pressure goes with disordered eating, not that it causes it.

- **Weight-related maltreatment.** In the 999 French-Canadian 14- to 17-year-olds, 7.4% reported at least one form of weight-related maltreatment from a coach or parent; 4.1% reported weight-related psychological violence from a coach and 3.9% weight-related neglect by a coach. In a logistic regression, athletes who reported coach psychological violence about weight had 8.04 times the odds of reporting extreme weight-control behaviours, and those who reported coach neglect about weight 18.35 times (Boudreault et al., 2022). Three in four of those who reported weight-related maltreatment also reported extreme weight-control behaviours. The authors measured both at once: an athlete already restricting may draw more comments, or the comments may push the restriction.
- **Pressure and being told to lose weight.** In the 462 young European athletes, 30.7% reported weight or shape pressure from coaches (36.9% of girls and women, 24.0% of boys and men), and 27.7% had been told to lose weight by someone who was not a health professional (Kenđel Jovanović & Čulina, 2024).
- **In judged and aesthetic sports.** In competitive female gymnasts aged 11 to 17, weight pressures from sport and coaches, appearance and performance pressures and body mass index together explained 30.3% of the variation in disordered-eating scores (Kontele et al., 2022, abstract read). In 646 elite Spanish athletes from 33 sports (mean age 16.7), perceived coach pressure went with eating disorder symptoms in female athletes, particularly in aesthetic sports ([Teixidor-Batlle et al., 2021, cross-sectional, full text read](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2020.559832)).
- **Abuse more broadly.** In 794 current and recently retired Canadian national-team athletes over 16 (mean age 27.85), reported psychological abuse correlated with eating disorder indicators (r = .311), and the abstract reports that athlete satisfaction's interaction with psychological abuse was marginally significant in predicting eating disorder indicators in aesthetic and weight-based sport athletes ([Willson et al., 2025, cross-sectional, full text read](https://doi.org/10.3389/fspor.2024.1406775)). For athletes under 18 this is the unit's inference.
- **What athletes remember.** In interviews in Hungarian rhythmic gymnastics, retired gymnasts (mean age 20.25), coaches and mothers all linked a lean body to success, and gymnasts described demeaning remarks about their bodies ([Kovács et al., 2022, qualitative, full text read](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2022.955232)). In the UK interviews with adults aged 19 to 44, one athlete linked her coach's ambitions for her performance to the way she looked, and the authors' discussion cites earlier studies in which coaches strengthened the link between performance and disordered eating (Stoyel et al., 2021). For athletes under 18, both studies in this bullet are the unit's inference.
- **Both ways.** The Female Athlete Triad Coalition's consensus states that coaches and teammates can act as risk factors and as protective factors for eating pathology (De Souza et al., 2026, section 3.2.1).

The bibliography's best-known study of critical comments about shape and weight (Muscat and Long, 2008) failed at its publisher's site, so nothing is taken from it.

**The grade.** That coach pressure about weight and shape goes with disordered eating in athletes: [weak, association; several cross-sectional surveys and qualitative studies in athletes, no meta-analysis or controlled study opened (rules 2 and 5); a correlation, so nothing about cause]. That you therefore make no comment on any athlete's weight, shape or food: [practice; the course's safety rule; no study opened tests a coach's silence on weight against anything else]. The weak grade measures how much the studies can prove; it says nothing about how large the risk is. The rest of this unit works that one rule out.

## 6. Rapid weight loss: how common, how early, and what the positions say for minors

**Rapid weight loss** (RWL) is losing body mass quickly before a weigh-in, to compete in a lower class than the athlete trains at, usually followed by regaining it after the weigh-in. The unit describes no method; where a federation rule below names dehydration, it is named only as the risk that rule guards against.

**How common, in the young.** A systematic review of seven observational studies of combat athletes aged 19 or under (3,135 participants; wrestling, judo and boxing; studies from the United States, Israel, Belgium, Croatia and Brazil, and a boxing study across twelve European countries, 2004 to 2020) found rapid weight loss in 42% to 77% of young wrestlers across three studies, 80% of young judokas (one of its three judo studies) and 25% of young boxers (its one boxing study); its abstract gives 25% to 94% overall, the top of that range from older athletes ([Lakicevic et al., 2022, systematic review, full text read](https://doi.org/10.1186/s40337-022-00595-w)). The authors concluded that the patterns in young athletes are similar to those in adults. In the French-Canadian survey, 44% of 14- to 17-year-olds in weight-class sports reported extreme weight-control behaviours (Boudreault et al., 2022).

**How early.** In the same review, judokas in Israel and Brazil reported first cutting weight as young as four and nine years old respectively (Lakicevic et al., 2022). The narrative review reports many combat athletes first doing so as early as 12 (Lakicevic et al., 2021). Band 10 to 12 is not too young for the question to arise in your squad.

**Who drives it.** The systematic review found rapid weight loss mainly encouraged by coaches, with very few athletes seeking advice from health professionals (Lakicevic et al., 2022). In a Mexican survey of 160 wrestlers and taekwondo athletes aged 7 to 24 (mean 13.3), coaches were rated the most influential people in adopting it; the paper's table gives 66% rating their coach's influence as some or very high, and its discussion gives a different figure ([Castor-Praga et al., 2021, cross-sectional, full text read](https://doi.org/10.3389/fsoc.2021.637671)). Both studies rest on what athletes report.

**What the positions say for minors.** Three statements were listed for this question, and none could be read at its publisher's page, so this unit reports nothing from them: the American Academy of Pediatrics' clinical report on weight control in young athletes ([Carl et al., 2017, consensus, not opened](https://doi.org/10.1542/peds.2017-1871)), the American College of Sports Medicine's consensus on weight loss in weight-category sports ([Burke et al., 2021, consensus, not opened](https://doi.org/10.1249/JSR.0000000000000831)) and the IOC Medical Commission working group's position statement on weight-sensitive sports ([Sundgot-Borgen et al., 2013, consensus, not opened](https://doi.org/10.1136/bjsports-2013-092966)).

Searches on 19 Nov 2026 returned no publisher's page for the first two. For the third, the publisher's abstract link was refused and its metrics page showed only the title (see the opening note). No grade in this unit rests on any of them. What was opened:

- The systematic review's authors write that in young combat athletes "RWL needs to be stringently regulated and ideally banned in this population", recommend education for coaches, athletes and parents, and suggest that for athletes aged 13 and under there be less focus on weight categories, with opponents matched by weight on arrival (Lakicevic et al., 2022). This is the authors' recommendation, not a consensus.
- The narrative review recommends moving weigh-ins closer to the start of competition and rules that make rapid weight loss impractical (Lakicevic et al., 2021).
- Federations have written such rules into their own procedures (section 18): smaller allowances for younger athletes at a second weigh-in, so that they are not tempted to dehydrate; no lightweight category below Under 23 in rowing; a rule that cadets above a set zone over their class compete in the class above.

The grade for "no rapid weight loss for any athlete under 18, and no coach encouraging it": [practice; a systematic review's authors' recommendation and federation rules; no study opened tests a ban; no consensus statement on it was opened]. The evidence that it is common and starts young is descriptive, from athletes' reports, and needs no grade.

## 7. Growth: what holding a class through puberty asks of a growing body

Dani (fictional) grew 9 cm in twelve months and is now at the top of her class. Her coach wants her to stay in it one more season.

The arithmetic is simple, and it is this unit's, not a study's: a body that is growing in height gains mass as it does, so holding a fixed class through a growth spurt means a body that may not weigh more while it grows. Whether any particular athlete can do that without restricting energy is a medical question about that athlete. What the opened sources add:

- Adolescent girls may be more severely affected by energy deficiency because of possible disruption to bone mineral accrual, and energy deficiency can delay or stop periods (De Souza et al., 2026); eating disorders and disordered eating may restrict growth, particularly when intense training starts before puberty (same source, section 3.3).
- A review of the growth of artistic gymnasts concluded that adult or near-adult height was not compromised by gymnastics training and that training did not appear to blunt pubertal growth, while noting that "it has not been possible thus far to establish cause–effect relationships", that inadequate energy intake with training has been suggested as inhibitory, and that the data on hormones were inadequate. The gymnasts who stayed in the sport were a select, shorter sample to begin with ([Malina et al., 2013, narrative review, full text read to the section on diet](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-013-0058-5); a narrative review is outside the course's source types, and no grade rests on it). The review is about training; it does not say that restricting food to hold a weight is safe.
- SP2 set out the three clocks (chronological, biological and training age) and the IOC statement's description of maturation as running through roughly the first two decades of life (SP2, section 1).

So the question "can she stay in the class?" is never answered by the coach. It goes to the medical contact, and the athlete decides with her parents after that answer (section 11). Until a medical contact has said that staying needs no restriction, a coach does not plan a season on it [practice; consensus; the studies behind it were not opened; the course's safety rule].

## 8. Predicting adult build, or the class that "suits" a body

Coaches say a 13-year-old "will be a heavyweight" or "is built for the 400", and plan on it. The evidence opened says such predictions are unreliable for the individual.

- A narrative systematic review of 15 studies of 4,707 male academy footballers aged under 9 to under 18 compared the non-invasive methods clubs use to estimate maturation (equations that predict the age of peak height velocity, and estimates of the percentage of predicted adult height reached). It found that "none of the estimated ages at PHV were equivalent to actual ages at PHV", that the methods agreed poorly with one another, and that individual timing varies widely within an age group ([Sullivan et al., 2023, narrative systematic review, full text read](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0286768)). Its studies were boys in one sport.
- SP2's meta-analysis reported differences by maturity status in height and body mass in girls (SMD 0.96 to 1.19), and its authors say more research is needed on girls ([Albaladejo-Saura et al., 2021, meta-analysis, abstract read](https://doi.org/10.3390/ijerph18010328)). The bibliography's review of maturation assessment in youth athletes (Malina et al., 2015) could not be found at its publisher's address.
- No study opened for this unit tests whether anyone can predict an adolescent's adult body mass, or the weight class or event that will "suit" them as an adult.

The grade for "predictions of an adolescent's adult build are unreliable for the individual": [weak; one narrative systematic review, in male footballers, with no pooled estimate (rule 5); untested in girls and in weight-class, judged and endurance sports: the unit's extrapolation]. The grade for "do not choose, or steer an athlete towards, a class, event or position on a prediction of their adult body": [practice; no study opened tests it]. The review's abstract does report three methods estimating adult *height* to within 1 cm of actual adult height, while noting discrepancies between predicted and actual adult height and age at peak height velocity, but height is not body mass, and no source opened tests a prediction of adult body mass, class or event.

## 9. The class in which an athlete wins, and the body the athlete lives in

A weight class carries a conflict of interest, and the coach is on one side of it.

The class in which an athlete is most likely to win now is often the lowest one they can make. Winning in it serves the athlete, and it also serves the coach: results, selection for the club, a coach's own standing. The body the athlete lives in, now and for the rest of their life, serves only the athlete. When those two point different ways, the person whose interest is in the results is the wrong person to decide. The studies above show who has usually decided: coaches were the main source of encouragement for rapid weight loss in the young combat athletes reviewed (Lakicevic et al., 2022) and the most influential people in its adoption in the Mexican survey (Castor-Praga et al., 2021).

DD-SP1 starts from the same fact about power: a young athlete who depends on you for selection and attention has reasons not to object to what you want (DD-SP1, section 1). A 14-year-old who looks to her coach after every exchange, as Dani (fictional) does, will hear "I'd like you to stay in the class" as more than a preference. That is why Dani's coach does not argue for a class (section 11), and why the reasoning in this section is this unit's, built on DD-SP1's account of power [practice; no study opened tests it].

## 10. A class as an identity

Athletes in weight-class sports often speak of themselves by their class, and a title won in a class can become part of who an athlete thinks they are. Moving up can then feel like losing the title, the rankings and the self that went with them, even when the move is what the body needs.

The evidence on this is thin. The Mexican survey's authors cite earlier reports linking rapid weight loss to a sense of "sporting identity", but they did not measure it (Castor-Praga et al., 2021), and no study opened for this unit measures attachment to a weight class. SP2's section on identity from 16 to 25 covers athletic identity in general. So this paragraph is description, not finding, and what follows from it is [practice; no study opened tests it]: when an athlete's class comes up, talk about what they want from the season and from the sport, and let the class follow from that. A national title at 13 is a fact about one competition at 13 (SP2, section 10), and no reason to keep a class.

## 11. Who decides, and what the coach's part is

The decision about an athlete's weight class, and any decision that touches their weight or body, belongs to the athlete. For an athlete under 18 it is made with a parent or guardian. Where any restriction would be needed (eating less, drinking less or training extra to make or hold a weight), it waits for medical input. Where a concern about eating, weight or body is already open, only a move to a higher class may be recorded before the medical contact has seen the athlete, and the medical contact is told of it; any other choice waits for them. The coach supplies competition facts and records the decision; the coach does not make it.

This is the course's scope line applied to weight (SP1, section 2), not a finding. The sources opened point the same way without testing it: the Female Athlete Triad consensus puts nutrition and health decisions with an athlete care team of physicians, athletic trainers and sports dietitians (De Souza et al., 2026, section 3.1); the systematic review of youth combat sports found that very few athletes sought health professionals' advice and recommends education of coaches, athletes and parents (Lakicevic et al., 2022); British Taekwondo's weigh-in procedure states that it is never its recommendation that athletes, "particularly very young athletes, be encouraged to lose weight" ([British Taekwondo, SOP031, version 5a, June 2024, section 6.4, read 19 Nov 2026](https://www.britishtaekwondo.org.uk/wp-content/uploads/2024/08/Weigh-In-Policy.pdf)); and the Irish Judo Association's policy sends weight questions to a coach or a nutrition professional and to its Nutrition Lead (section 18). SP3 set out the autonomy-supportive and controlling behaviours this rests on.

## 12. Where persuasion becomes pressure and then harassment

**Why weight stays outside the comment test card.** DD-SP1's comment test card puts any comment on body or weight outside the card: not rewritten, not said (DD-SP1, section 5 and Deliverable A, comment 11). This unit keeps weight outside the card, for one reason: card question 2 asks whether a comment is about something the athlete can change, and weight is partly changeable by eating and training. Run through question 2, "You've put on weight" could be rewritten as a "behavioural" comment about food or extra sessions, which is exactly the comment the studies in section 5 associate with disordered eating. So no comment on weight, body or food is rewritten into another comment on weight, body or food; it is dropped, and the technical point that was available, if there was one, is made instead [practice; the course's safety rule]. ## 13. Weighing at the club: private, optional, and who sees the number

**What the sources say.** The opened evidence on weighing in clubs is thin and indirect:

- Former rhythmic gymnasts described public weight checks as humiliating and kept weighing themselves at home after retiring (Kovács et al., 2022; qualitative, 12 people; inference for under-18s).
- The Spanish study's authors list public weigh-ins among the coach behaviours that can contribute to disordered eating; they draw this from earlier literature, and did not measure it (Teixidor-Batlle et al., 2021).
- Female athletes in aesthetic sports weighed themselves more often than those in less weight-sensitive sports (Baceviciene et al., 2023; cross-sectional, so either could lead to the other).
- A systematic review of the markers used to diagnose REDs reports that the authors of the IOC consensus statement pointed out that too much focus on body measurements and composition can intensify the pressure on athletes, especially adolescents under 18 (the IOC statement itself was read here only in its abstract, which does not say this) ([Dvořáková et al., 2024, systematic review, full text read](https://doi.org/10.3389/fspor.2024.1375740)).
- In the clinical setting, the eating-disorder review lists a "blinded weight" (the review does not define it; read here as a weight taken without showing the athlete the number) among the measures in a physician's follow-up examination (Joy et al., 2016). That is a clinician's tool for a patient in treatment, named here only to show that even clinicians handle the number with care; it is not a club procedure.

**What follows** [practice; no study opened tests any weighing policy against another]: a club does not need to know any athlete's weight to coach them. For entering a competition you need the class the athlete has chosen, not the number. For pairing in contact training you can pair by the class list athletes chose, or by size by eye, without scales. Deliverable D turns this into a policy: no weighing of athletes under 18 in training; scales out of shared spaces; a weight check only at the athlete's or parent's request, by the medical contact, in private, the number told to the athlete (and parent) and to no one else, and not written in club records.

**Official weigh-ins are different.** A competition's weigh-in is run by the organiser under the federation's rules, and the athlete cannot opt out of it. British Taekwondo's procedure shows what privacy can look like at one: two officials of the same gender as the athlete, officials over 18 with safeguarding checks, athletes under 18 never weighed nude, each accompanied by an accredited coach or club welfare officer of the same gender or by a parent, and no other children's parents, non-accredited coaches, or other teams' players or coaches admitted (British Taekwondo, SOP031, sections 6.1 and 6.2, read 19 Nov 2026). Your federation's rules may differ; section 18 gives others.

## 14. Competition-day weigh-ins: what you say before and after

SP8 placed the weigh-in in Dani's competition-day protocol as a fixed time on the timetable, with no comment on weight before or after it (SP8, Deliverable A). This section fills that in [practice; no study opened tests what a coach says around a weigh-in].

**Before.** Logistics only: where, when, what to bring (identification, the right kit), who goes in with them if the rules require an accompanying adult. No "Are you on weight?", no "Have you made it?", no "How much are you?" The athlete's preparation for the weigh-in is between them, their parents and, where there is any concern, the medical contact (section 11). If an athlete tells you they are worried about making weight, you do not advise; you tell them that their health comes before today's weigh-in and that they may withdraw today, that the class can be changed at the next review, and you pass on the worry the same day by SP9's route; if they seem unwell, the competition's medical staff see them now.

**At it.** If your federation's rules have you accompany an athlete into the weigh-in, follow them exactly, including any rule on same-gender accompaniment. If they do not, you are not in the room.

**After.** No question about the number, and no comment on it, whether they made the weight or not. The next thing you say is about the next thing that happens: "Done. Warm-up is at [time], mat [number]." If the athlete did not make the weight, it is a fact about one weigh-in. Respond to their state (SP7's table) and say nothing about their body; the class question goes to the next review.

**Eating and drinking after a weigh-in** is the athlete's and their family's, guided by whoever advises them on nutrition. You make sure there is time and a place for it on the timetable; you do not comment on what or how much.

Deliverable C5 gives the words.

## 15. The equivalents in judged sports: appearance, costume, "lines"

In judged sports the body is seen and scored, and the vocabulary of coaching slides easily from movement to shape: "lines", "a long look", "presentation", "she looks heavy in the air".

The opened studies describe the pressure without testing what to do about it. In Hungarian rhythmic gymnastics, gymnasts, coaches and mothers all linked a lean body to success, and coaches who also judged said they were not always free to give the score a gymnast deserved (Kovács et al., 2022). In the UK interviews, competition costumes intensified athletes' comparison of their bodies, and the authors' introduction, drawing on earlier studies, describes a pressure in lean sports to be thin for performance and for appearance to judges and others (Stoyel et al., 2021; the interviews were with nine adults aged 19 to 44, so for under-18s this is the unit's inference). Competitive Greek gymnasts aged 11 to 17 reported more appearance- and performance-related weight pressure than recreational gymnasts (Kontele et al., 2022, abstract read).

What to do [practice; the course's safety rule; no study opened tests these steps]:

- **Coach the movement and leave the shape alone.** A line is a position of joints: "Extend through the toes on the landing", "Shoulders down and back in the spiral", "Reach longer through the fingertips". It is never "you'd have better lines if you were lighter". The same goes for a diver's entry, a figure skater's spiral and a dancer's arabesque.
- **Costume is the athlete's choice within the rules.** Where a rule allows a choice (leotard or unitard, sleeve length, shorts over a leotard), the athlete chooses, and nobody comments on how their body looks in it. Ask your federation which options its rules allow, and date the answer.
- **Judges' preferences are not a reason to change a child's body.** If you believe a judging panel rewards a body type, that is a matter to raise with the federation, not with the athlete.

Sara's (fictional) sport is judged by the clock, but the same rule holds there: DD-SP1's comment 11, "You'd fly if you lost a bit", to a 16-year-old in her coach's other group, is replaced by the technical point that was available, "Your arms cross on the bend. Drive them straight back." (DD-SP1, Deliverable A).

## 16. Boys, men and muscularity

Most of the research on athletes' eating and body image is on girls and women. Boys appear in it with lower rates, not zero ones: 9.7% of boys aged 14 to 17 in the French-Canadian survey reported extreme weight-control behaviours, against 19.75% of girls (Boudreault et al., 2022). In the systematic review, adult male athletes reported more eating and body concerns than adolescent boys, the reverse of the pattern in females, and male wrestlers reported high body satisfaction while using unhealthy weight-loss methods (Li et al., 2024). The Lithuanian survey measured drive for muscularity alongside thinness and found adult males reporting more dietary restraint than adolescent males (Baceviciene et al., 2023).

For boys and men the pressure often runs the other way, towards size. A review of eating disorders in men notes that research on male eating disorders and body image "is increasingly focusing on muscularity-oriented manifestations" ([Lavender et al., 2017, review, abstract read](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11920-017-0787-5)). **Muscle dysmorphia** is described in a 2025 study as a specifier of body dysmorphic disorder "characterized by the pathological pursuit of muscularity" ([Ganson et al., 2025, cross-sectional, full text read](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pmen.0000217)); the ICD-11 working group did not propose a separate specifier for it (Veale & Matsunaga, 2014). In that Canadian online survey of 2,731 people aged 16 to 30 (not athletes; a non-probability sample), 26.2% of boys and men and 11.8% of girls and women scored at or above a questionnaire's clinical cut-off for muscle dysmorphia symptoms, and muscle-building supplement use went with higher scores (Ganson et al., 2025). A cut-off score is not a diagnosis, and the sample was not athletes; for athletes under 18 this is the unit's inference.

What this means for a coach of boys and young men [practice; the course's safety rule]:

- **"Bulk up", "get bigger", "you're too skinny for this level" are body comments.** They fall under the same rule as "lose a bit". For a 17-year-old midfielder like Milan (fictional), with an academy decision at the end of the season, a coach's "you need to fill out" is heard as a selection criterion. (An illustration, not an event in the case.)
- **Strength training is one programme, set by stage, for everyone.** A young athlete's strength work follows SP2's age-and-stage table and whoever plans conditioning, and no athlete gets a programme aimed at changing their body on a coach's comment.
- **Supplements are not yours to suggest.** Questions about them go to the medical contact.
- **Signs are the same signs.** Extra training on top of the plan, rigid eating, distress at a missed session, avoiding being seen without a shirt: SP9's route, first sign, no comment.

## 17. Red flags and referral

SP9 owns the red flags and the route, and this unit changes neither. Two rows of SP9's red-flag table cover this unit's ground:

- **Dramatic weight changes:** a red flag on the IOC recognition tool's list; at once, privately, in behaviour words, with the contact offered, and no comment on weight, body or food (SP9, Deliverable A).
- **Eating, weight or body (skipped meals, body talk, extra training):** to the person for medical concerns at the first sign, with no waiting and no comment (SP9, Deliverable A).

An athlete who faints, collapses, is confused or seems acutely unwell around a weigh-in, a session or a competition gets the competition's medical staff, the club's first-aider or emergency services at once; the referral rows come after that. What you might see otherwise in the sports this unit covers, each going by one of those two rows: an athlete who seems unusually tired around weigh-ins, who is distressed or tearful around weigh-ins, who disappears from team meals, whose periods have stopped (if the athlete or a parent tells you), who has a stress fracture, who avoids changing in front of others or wearing competition kit, who keeps asking whether they look heavier. Your part ends at the referral, the same day. While the athlete is under referral for anything touching eating, weight or energy, ask the medical contact that week what training is safe until the appointment and add no load meanwhile; keep their place in the squad and their selection independent of the referral (SP9, section 8). ## 18. Federation rules on weigh-ins and on weight control for minors: dated examples

Rules change. These are examples, each read on the federation's own page on 19 Nov 2026, to show the range; your own federation's current rules are the ones that apply to you. No weight figure, tolerance figure or category limit is reproduced here.

- **Wrestling: United World Wrestling, *International Wrestling Rules* (version January 2026).** Age categories U15 (14 to 15 years, from 13 in the year with medical and parental authorisation), U17 and U20; each wrestler enters one category, the one corresponding to their weight at the official weigh-in (Art. 7). The weigh-in is held on the morning of each competition day of the category, after a medical examination, in the competition singlet: "The weigh-in and the medical control lasts 30 minutes." (Art. 8 and 11). No rule specific to minors' weight control appears in the part of the rules read ([UWW, read 19 Nov 2026](https://cdn.uww.org/2025-12/wrestling_rules_1.pdf)). A same-morning weigh-in leaves less time between weigh-in and bout than a weigh-in the day before, the direction the narrative review recommends (section 6).
- **Taekwondo: British Taekwondo, *Weigh-in Standard Operating Procedure* (SOP031, version 5a, June 2024).** The privacy rules in section 13 above; a second-attempt margin at the weigh-in that is smaller for younger categories, "to protect the athlete from attempting excessive weight loss by dehydration" (6.4); random weigh-ins with the list published in advance (6.6 to 6.7); and the statement that British Taekwondo never recommends that athletes, particularly very young ones, be encouraged to lose weight ([British Taekwondo, read 19 Nov 2026](https://www.britishtaekwondo.org.uk/wp-content/uploads/2024/08/Weigh-In-Policy.pdf)).
- **Judo: Irish Judo Association, *IJA Weight Management Policy* (version 1, updated 1 Feb 2022; its next review was due on 1 Feb 2025).** Pre-Cadet and Cadet athletes whose training weight is more than a set zone above their category "must compete in the weight category above" until their natural training weight is back within it; players outside the zone are referred to a coach or nutrition professional and, nearer selection competitions, to the programme's Nutrition Lead, and may be deselected for their health ([Irish Judo Association, read 19 Nov 2026](https://irishjudoassociation.ie/wp-content/uploads/2024/05/IJA-Weight-Management-Policy-2022.pdf)). The policy applies to its national performance programme; whether it has been reviewed since 2025 was not found. The international federation's own Sport and Organisation Rules could not be opened from a search result for this unit, so no international judo rule is stated.
- **Rowing: World Rowing, *Rules of Racing* (version March 2025; the later version on World Rowing's site, last updated 1 July 2025, has the same Rules 19 and 26 and Bye-Law to Rule 23, checked 19 Nov 2026).** A lightweight category is recognised "for Seniors and Under 23" only (Rule 19), with no Under 19 lightweight event (Rule 26): junior rowers do not race in weight categories at World Rowing's events (ask your national federation about its own junior events). Lightweight crews are weighed together in racing uniform between one and two hours before their first race of each lightweight event on each day of racing (Bye-Law to Rule 23) ([World Rowing, read 19 Nov 2026](https://worldrowing.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/04/2025-World-Rowing-Rules-of-Racing-Overall-Classic-rowing-Related-byelaws_March2025.pdf)).

Three ways of protecting young athletes show across these four: no weight categories below a given age at World Rowing's events (rowing), smaller margins for younger athletes so that dehydration does not pay (taekwondo), and an obligation to move up when the body has moved on (Irish judo). For your own sport, ask your national federation: What are our weigh-in rules for each youth category, and when do they take place? Is there a rule on moving up for growing athletes? Who may accompany a minor into the weigh-in? Write the answers, with the date and the document's version, into Deliverable D.

### C. Scripts for the weight-class conversation

Each script is said in private, at normal volume (the change Dani's coach is working on, SP3 and SP8). Words in square brackets are filled for your setting.

**C1. Opening the review** (coach to Dani and her parents, in the meeting room).

> "Thanks for coming. This is about which class Dani competes in this season. It's Dani's decision, with you two. My part is to give you the facts and to write down what you decide. Here's what I know: [the classes in her age category], the next competitions are [dates], entries close on [date], and [what the selection rules say]. Whatever class you choose, I'll coach you the same, and your place in this squad doesn't change. Dani, can I ask you some questions? What do you want from this season?"

**C2. The athlete chooses the class in which she will probably win less** (Dani has just said she wants to move up).

> "Then that's the class I'll enter you in. Thank you for telling me straight."
>
> (If she asks, "Are you disappointed?") "No. You've made a decision about your own body and your own season, and that's the right person to make it. There'll be new opponents up there and we'll learn them together."
>
> (After her first loss in the new class, the same week, at her side.) "The first exchange went to her. The sleeve was there in the second one, and you took it. Same plan next time: sleeve first."

What the coach does not say, then or later: "I told you so"; "You'd have won that a class down"; "Maybe next season we'll think about going back." The class is reopened only by the triggers in A. [practice; SP3 for how a coach responds after a loss; SP8's debrief card]

**C3. The athlete wants to stay in, or drop to, a class that would need restriction** (a template case; an illustration, not an event in the case).

> "Thank you for telling me. Before I can enter you in that class, [the medical contact] needs to see you and tell you and your parents whether that's safe for you. That's not me saying no, and it's not me saying yes: it's not my decision and it's not my knowledge. Until then I'll enter you in the class above, which asks nothing of your eating or training, if you want to compete, or you can wait. Which would you like?"

**C4. The athlete asks, "Should I lose weight?" or "Am I too heavy?"** (any age; in Dani's squad, an 11- to 17-year-old).

> "That's not something I judge, and I won't. If it's on your mind, [the medical contact] is the right person, and I can help set that up, with your parents if you'd like them there. Is something worrying you about it?"
>
> (Then listen. If what they say suggests restriction, extra training or distress: SP9's route, the same day, with their knowledge. If not: nothing further, and no follow-up comment about their body.)

**C5. Competition-day weigh-in** (Dani's regional competition, SP8).

> *The evening before, to Dani and her parents, by the message SP8 already sends:* "Meet at 7:30 at the club. Weigh-in is at [time] at the venue. Bring your ID card and both judogi."
>
> *On arrival:* "Weigh-in is through there. [If the rules require an accompanying adult: Your mum is going in with you / The club's welfare officer is going in with you.] I'll be at the warm-up area."
>
> *After, whatever happened:* "Done. Warm-up is at [time], mat [number]. Have you got what you need?"
>
> *If she missed the weight:* "That's today's weigh-in, that's all. Let's sort out what happens now with the organiser and your parents. We'll look at your class at the next review, not today."

If at any point she seems faint, confused or unwell: the competition's medical staff, at once (section 14). Never, before or after: the number, "close one", "made it?", "were you light?", any comment on what she eats or drinks afterwards [practice; SP8; section 14].

**C6. A parent pushes for the lower class** (an illustration, not an event: a parent at the edge of the mat after a session).

> "I understand you want her to win. The class is Dani's choice, with you, at the review, and anything that would need her to restrict goes to [the medical contact] first. I won't push her either way. Can we put it on the agenda for the review on [date]?" (If the parent describes restricting her food or drink, watching what she eats or weighing her at home, that goes the same day to the welfare officer by DD-SP1's route, and the eating side to the person for medical concerns by SP9's.)

### D. Club weighing policy

**Built for Dani's (fictional) club**: one judo squad of 18 athletes aged 11 to 17, a coach, a second coach who can be accredited for competitions (SP8), a person for medical concerns and a welfare or safeguarding officer (SP1). Until now there was no written policy, and a set of scales stood in the corridor by the changing rooms for athletes to check themselves before competitions (*added*).

> **[Club] weighing policy.** Adopted [date] by the committee. Reviewed each season with the who-decides reviews.
>
> 1. **No weighing in training.** Coaches and volunteers do not weigh athletes under 18 at training, before or after sessions, or at camps. Nobody keeps a list of athletes' weights.
> 2. **No scales in shared spaces.** The corridor scales have been removed (*added*). Scales are kept only where [the person for medical concerns] uses them.
> 3. **A weight check only on request, in private.** If an athlete, or the parent of an athlete under 18, asks for a weight check, it is done by a health professional reached through [the person for medical concerns], or by the athlete's own doctor, in a private room, with the athlete's parent or a second adult of the athlete's choosing present for an athlete under 18. The number is told to the athlete (and their parent) and to no one else. It is not written in club records.
> 4. **The coach needs the class; the number stays with the athlete.** Coaches enter athletes in the class the athlete has chosen at their who-decides review. They do not ask an athlete's weight.
> 5. **No weights in public.** No weights, weight changes or classes-to-make on boards, lists, group messages or social media.
> 6. **No targets.** Nobody at the club sets, suggests or monitors a weight for any athlete. A change of class goes through the who-decides review.
> 7. **Competition weigh-ins.** These are run by the organiser under [the federation's] rules, read on [date] (section 18). The club's part is logistics: getting the athlete there on time with identification and kit, and an accompanying adult where the rules require one ([a parent, or the welfare officer or second coach of the same gender as the athlete], *added*). Coaches say nothing about the number before or after (scripts C5).
> 8. **Concerns.** An athlete who faints, collapses, is confused or seems acutely unwell gets the club's first-aider, the competition's medical staff or emergency services at once (section 17). Otherwise, anyone who notices a sign about eating, weight or body tells [the person for medical concerns] the same day, without comment to the athlete (SP9). Anyone who sees another adult or athlete comment on a young athlete's weight, watch what they eat or weigh them tells [the welfare officer] (DD-SP1).
> 9. **Questions the club has asked.** May we keep any health information about a minor, and for how long? Asked of [the federation's safeguarding officer and the data-protection authority] on [date]; answer: [their words]. Who must consent to a weight check for an athlete under 16, and under 18? Asked of [the federation's medical officer] on [date]; answer: [their words].
