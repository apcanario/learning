## 1. Why a skill learned calm can fail in competition

SP4 set out what competition adds to training: evaluation and consequences. A lost ball in a drill decides nothing; a lost ball in the first fifteen minutes of an academy match may, in the player's mind, decide his future. SP4's two accounts of choking say what that does to attention: worry pulls attention away from the task (the distraction account), or the athlete starts to watch and steer a movement that normally runs on its own (the self-focus account). Either way, the skill the athlete practised is being run under conditions it was never practised in.

The idea behind pressure training follows from that. If an athlete only ever practises a skill, or a psychological skill from SP5, when nothing rides on it, the first time it meets worry, a watching selector or a single chance is in competition. Pressure training moves that first meeting into practice. One early pair of experiments shows the shape of the idea: 17 expert basketball players practised free throws for five weeks with or without induced anxiety, and 17 expert dart players threw from high or low on a climbing wall, with or without anxiety; in both, performance held up on the anxious post-test only in the group that had trained with anxiety, and the authors suggest the performers had become used to what anxiety does ([Oudejans & Pijpers, 2009, abstract read](https://doi.org/10.1080/17470210802557702)). The abstract gives no ages, no randomisation method and no numbers. The mechanism, getting used to the processes anxiety brings, is the authors' explanation, not something the study measured directly.

This is the third of SP5's four stages of teaching a skill: explain, practise calm, practise under pressure, use in competition. SP5's eight-week block used the pressure stage lightly, in weeks 2, 4, 6 and 8 (a scored game, a timed rep, a teammate watching), and left this unit to build it. Section 9 and the deliverable build it: a ladder of six levels, a rule for stepping an athlete down, and the debrief.

## 2. What the meta-analysis of pressure training found

**What it pooled.** A 2021 meta-analysis defined pressure training as physically practising domain-specific skills under simulated pressure. It included randomised or non-randomised studies comparing an experimental group with a control group, in which controls trained under low pressure and the outcome was quantitative performance on a high-pressure post-test; peer-reviewed English-language articles; no restriction on experience; case studies excluded ([Low et al., 2021, accepted manuscript read in full on the University of Essex repository](https://doi.org/10.1037/spy0000202)). The control groups, in the authors' words, "physically practiced as much as" the pressure groups: the comparison is pressure against the same amount of practice without it.

**On whom.** Fourteen studies, 394 participants (the abstract's figure; Table 1's per-study numbers sum to 399): ten in sport (badminton, golf, cricket, Australian football, rock climbing, basketball and darts) and four in law enforcement, all of them shooting tasks by police officers or a specialist police team. Participants ranged from novices to experts; one sample is described as elite youth cricketers. The paper gives no mean ages. The police samples are adults and one sample is elite youth cricketers of unstated ages; for the other sport samples the meta-analysis gives no ages.

**What it found.** Effects are Hedges' *g*. Across all 14 studies, *g* = 0.85 (95% CI 0.37 to 1.34) with high heterogeneity (I² = 78.4%). One outlying study drove most of the heterogeneity; without it, *g* = 0.67, which the authors call medium, with I² = 17.1%. The interval for that estimate is given as 0.41 to 0.94 in the results and as 0.43 to 1.12 in the abstract; this unit uses the results' figure and flags the conflict. In the sport studies alone, *g* = 0.72 (0.45 to 1.00; 10 studies); in law enforcement, 0.63 (−0.14 to 1.39; 3 studies). Novices *g* = 0.77 (5 studies) and experienced performers 0.61 (8 studies); one session 0.73 (6 studies), two to five sessions 0.72 (4 studies), more than five 0.42 with an interval crossing zero (3 studies). The authors conclude that none of these moderators changed the effect significantly. The funnel plot was described as fairly symmetrical and Egger's test was not significant (P = 0.12). Risk of bias was rated unclear in most domains, with one study high on several.

**What it did not test.** Every effect size comes from a post-test under simulated pressure, not from competition; only one study ran a retention test, and it was not pooled. The meta-analysis reports that one study measured competition performance and found the pressure group better overall, without isolating pressure moments; that study could not be opened for this unit. The authors list their own limits: pressure manipulations could not be compared one by one because studies combined them, few studies ran long programmes, and nothing tested transfer to other skills ([Low et al., 2021](https://doi.org/10.1037/spy0000202)).

**Two studies with younger athletes.** Neither tests pressure training against none, and neither is in the meta-analysis.

- In 82 male players aged 11 to 18 (mean 14.1) at one professional football academy, randomly allocated, both groups did the same pressure training three times over 18 weeks (a two-against-two task with crowd noise, a public leaderboard, rewards for the best and consequences decided by a dice roll for the worst, and sprints beforehand for the 15- to 18-year-olds to add fatigue); one group also had three 30-minute cognitive-behavioural workshops (15 minutes for the 17- and 18-year-olds) and reflective diaries. Coach-rated decision-making was higher in that group (χ² = 7.15, p = .028); skill execution did not differ. Only 68 players were retested at six weeks and 26 at twelve, and the authors call the support "partial" ([Kent et al., 2021, accepted manuscript read in full on the University of Gloucestershire repository](https://doi.org/10.1080/10413200.2020.1868618)). Their ethical recommendations include protecting players from harm and making clear that results will not inform contract decisions.
- In 19 players (18 completed) at an elite women's basketball academy (group means 18.4 and 17.8 years), a switching-replications design without random allocation gave each group a resilience workshop and eight 30-minute pressure sessions over three weeks: a simulated last two minutes of a game against a points deficit with a referee, and free throws with the team watching, a cap on attempts in some sessions and team sprints after misses, with a coach-led group reflection after every session. Individual resilience scores did not change; team vulnerabilities fell in one group (p = .013 for the interaction). Players reported more awareness and better communication; they also reported a lot of stress at first, frustration when they fell behind and had to run, a sense of unfairness, and some panic at the free-throw line, and the coaches noted that younger players reacted more to consequences that fell on teammates ([Kegelaers et al., 2021, full text read on a CORE repository copy](https://doi.org/10.1080/10413200.2019.1630864)). The authors' introduction describes facilitative environments as needing "a careful balance between sufficient challenge and support".

Teach coping skills alongside pressure training: [weak, positive; one randomised study in academy footballers aged 11 to 18, decision-making only, heavy attrition, rule 5; for 19 to 25 outside the sampled ages]. The basketball study is the course's only opened account of how athletes of about 18 experienced pressure sessions; its stress and panic reports are the reason section 4's rules exist.

## 3. Ways of making pressure in practice, and what is known about each

**Pressure and difficulty are different things.** Interviews with 11 elite coaches about how they built pressure into training found they manipulated stressors such as the task, forfeits and judgement, organised around the *demands* of training and its *consequences*, adjusted to the individual; the authors define pressure as "the perception that it is important to perform exceptionally" ([Stoker et al., 2016, abstract read at the journal's page](https://doi.org/10.7352/IJSP.2016.47.262)). A demand makes a drill harder: less time, more fatigue, more noise. A consequence makes the outcome matter: something follows from success or failure.

**The one experiment that separated them.** In a within-subject experiment, six elite shooters with disabilities (five rifle, one pistol, aged 20 to 41) each shot under seven conditions in random order, each adding one stressor to a baseline: a shorter time limit (task demand), a cognitive pre-fatigue task (performer demand), a repeating loud beep (environmental demand), a staged five-minute media interview if they missed a required score (forfeit), a cash prize for the top scorer (reward), and the performance director watching and rating them (judgement). The required score was each athlete's mean from their last three competitions. Perceived pressure rose above baseline only under the forfeit and under judgement; cognitive anxiety likewise; shooting scores fell below baseline under judgement and under the time limit; the reward changed none of these significantly. Mean perceived pressure on a 1-to-7 scale was 1.83 at baseline, 2.8 under the performer demand, 4.5 under judgement and 4.9 under the forfeit; the other conditions' means were not reported in the part read ([Stoker et al., 2019, manuscript read in full on the Liverpool John Moores University repository; citation from the University of Essex repository](https://doi.org/10.1123/tsp.2017-0045)). Six athletes, adults, one sport: the authors name the sample size as a limitation.

A practitioner article by four of the same research group draws the working rule from this and from a netball study (15 elite international players aged 19 to 32, within-subject: perceived pressure rose above control when consequences were added, judgement, a forfeit and a reward together, but not when demands alone were; performance fell under the demands; [Stoker et al., 2017, accepted manuscript read in full on the University of Essex repository; abstract at the publisher's page](https://doi.org/10.1080/10413200.2017.1298166)): added demands such as crowd noise or fatigue make a drill harder without reliably creating pressure; consequences create it; rewards are not recommended because they did not create pressure; and pressure is best raised gradually from a baseline, by analogy with graded exposure in clinical work ([Low et al., 2023, full text read on the Liverpool John Moores University repository](https://doi.org/10.1080/21520704.2022.2164098); a guidance article in a peer-reviewed practitioner journal, outside the source types the course lists, so it supports only [practice] grades here). The same article lists forfeits such as singing in front of teammates, deselection from the next competition and teammates doing sprints or push-ups, sets few limits on them beyond warning that a consequence must not read as punishment tied to the athlete's self-worth, and notes that "Few ethical consequences are likely to be strong enough" to create pressure alone. Section 4 rules most of those forfeits out for this course; the cost is pressure that is milder than it could be.

## 4. Which ways are acceptable with minors, and which are not

No study opened tested what pressure does to the wellbeing of athletes under 18, or which consequences harm them. The football academy study used a public leaderboard, dice-roll consequences and pre-drill sprints with players aged 11 to 18 and did not measure harm; the basketball academy study reported stress, frustration and panic in players of about 18 without measuring harm either. So the rules below are this unit's own. Each is graded [practice; no study opened tests it], and each gives its reason. The course applies them to every age band, 19 to 25 included: nothing opened shows that the forbidden consequences are needed, and an adult squad often trains alongside minors.

1. **No humiliation.** No forfeit that makes an athlete perform, sing, speak or be laughed at in front of others, and no list of who failed. Reason: SP3's ego-involving climate went with more fear of failure in young athletes (an association), the practitioner article warns that a consequence must not read as a judgement of the athlete's worth ([Low et al., 2023](https://doi.org/10.1080/21520704.2022.2164098)), and DD-SP1 draws the line between demanding coaching and harm.
2. **No physical punishment.** No sprints, push-ups, laps or extra conditioning as a forfeit, for the athlete or for teammates. Fatigue may be a demand, from the sport's own work, planned in the session load; never a penalty. Reason: SP3 graded exercise as punishment [practice] from adults' accounts of it as arbitrary and unsafe; the basketball players reported frustration when they fell behind and had to run (Kegelaers et al., 2021); and extra work is physical load added outside the plan (section 8).
3. **No threat to an athlete's place.** Nothing that happens in a pressure drill affects selection, playing time, squad membership, a contract or a grading, and the athletes are told so before the first session. Reason: SP3's rule never to use selection as a threat; the football academy study's own recommendation that results do not inform contract decisions ([Kent et al., 2021](https://doi.org/10.1080/10413200.2020.1868618)); and a drill that can cost a place has become the competition the athlete is preparing for.
4. **A way to step down, always, at no cost.** Any athlete may say "step down" before or during any drill, without giving a reason, and the coach steps them down with no comment, no record against them and no effect on anything in rule 3. Section 9 gives the rule. Reason: the practitioner article draws from graded exposure the principle of raising pressure in steps so that athletes are not overwhelmed; an athlete who cannot stop a drill has no way to keep the steps small.
5. **Consequences stay inside the sport.** The consequence of a missed standard is a sporting one: a point, possession, the starting position in the next round, going first or last, the choice of the next drill. Reason: these are the consequences of competition itself, so practising with them rehearses what the athlete will meet.
6. **No athlete pays for a teammate's miss.** A team may lose a point in a team game; no individual forfeit falls on others. Reason: the basketball coaches saw younger players react more to consequences that fell on teammates (Kegelaers et al., 2021), and a forfeit carried by teammates makes the squad the enforcer.
7. **Known in advance.** The level, the standard and the consequence are said before the drill starts; nothing is sprung. Reason: SP3's autonomy-supportive behaviours (explain the reason, offer choice) [moderate, positive, association, as SP3 grades them; ages not given in the meta-analysis's abstract, so for athletes under 18 an inference].
8. **Nothing about weight, food or the body is ever a pressure or a consequence.** No weigh-in drill, no comment on body or shape, no food as a reward or a forfeit. Reason: the course's safety rule; DD-SP2.

## 5. Pre-performance routines

**What a routine is.** The meta-analysis below adopts a definition of a pre-performance routine as "a sequence of task-relevant thoughts and actions" carried out before a sport skill. It distinguishes extensive routines, combining two or more preparatory elements (tested in 33 samples), from stand-alone routines of a single element (27 samples), among them a left-hand dynamic handgrip (squeezing with the left hand; 7 samples) and quiet-eye training (a steady final fixation on the target; 3 samples) ([Rupprecht et al., 2024, abstract and parts of the text read at the publisher's page](https://doi.org/10.1080/1750984X.2021.1944271)).

**What it found.** From 33 articles, 61 intervention samples and 112 effect sizes, in 15 sports (golf in 15 samples, basketball 13, tennis 6), with participants aged 11 to 40: in experiments against controls, *g* = 0.64 (95% CI 0.45 to 0.83) under low pressure and *g* = 0.70 (0.24 to 1.16) under pressure; in pre-post designs a small effect, standardised mean change 0.31 (0.18 to 0.44). Routine type, age, gender, skill level and intervention characteristics did not moderate the effect, and the authors suggest routines "can already be applied by young individuals". Most samples were small: 16 had fewer than 10 participants and 32 had 10 to 19. In the pre-post data smaller studies reported larger effects; the authors' further analyses found no evidence that publication bias produced the overall effect. No primary study included senior athletes ([Rupprecht et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.1080/1750984X.2021.1944271)).

**Grade.** Teach a pre-performance routine and practise it under pressure: [moderate, positive; a meta-analysis in athletes aged 11 to 40 with mostly small samples and a small-study effect in the pre-post data, rule 4; for 10-year-olds outside the sampled ages]. This agrees with SP1's worked example of the moderate grade, which uses the same paper. Extensive or single-element routine: either [moderate, positive; type did not moderate the effect].

**How to build one.** The routine is the athlete's, built from skills they have already practised calm (SP5). Each element is one SP5 skill or one physical action, in a fixed order, timed from the moment of performance backwards, with a reset for when something interrupts it [practice; the order and the reset are this unit's; no study opened tests a routine's structure]. Keep it short where the sport gives little time (a tennis serve, a volleyball serve, a free throw) and give it stages where it gives a lot (a high jumper's long approach, a swimmer's call room, a 400 m runner's hour before the race). The deliverable's routine builder sets this out.

## 6. What the review of choking interventions found

A systematic review of 47 empirical studies with athletes, published up to April 2017 (experimental, quasi-experimental and single-case designs), grouped 13 interventions by the choking account they rest on (distraction or self-focus) and found a third group, acclimatisation, meaning training under pressure. Interventions from both accounts and acclimatisation generally improved performance under pressure. Pre-performance routines, quiet-eye training, left-hand contractions and acclimatisation training were the most often reported effective. Using a secondary task to keep attention off the movement helped under pressure but was harmful when used in training. Evidence for analogy learning (teaching a movement by one image, such as "swing like a pendulum") was mixed. Goal setting, neurofeedback training and reappraisal cues showed null effects ([Gröpel & Mesagno, 2019, abstract read on the German sport science index LIDA, not the publisher's page](https://doi.org/10.1080/1750984X.2017.1408134)). SP4 read more of the same review (SP4's reading, not re-read for this unit): 62% of its studies used experienced athletes, mean ages were 20 to 40 in 68% of studies, golf was the most common sport, only four studies analysed real competition, and most experiments were rated of medium quality.

## 7. Rehearsing the competition day itself

A pressure drill rehearses one moment. A competition day adds the moments around it: travel, the warm-up at a set time, waiting, the call room or the dressing room, the coach's last words, the first action, a bad call, the time between rounds or heats. No study opened tests rehearsing a whole competition day. The nearest is the basketball package, which rehearsed the last two minutes of a game with a referee, a deficit and limited time-outs ([Kegelaers et al., 2021](https://doi.org/10.1080/10413200.2019.1630864)), and its players said training pressure could never fully match competition.

So, as practice: the top level of the ladder (level 6) is a rehearsal of the competition's timeline, with the athlete's routine, one attempt, a consequence inside the sport, and the demands of the day added (waiting, noise, a bad call), run on the timings of a real event. A judoka is called, waits and bows in; a swimmer reports to the call room, waits and walks out; a footballer warms up, goes in, comes out and starts with a kick-off against a side told to press. Run it once or twice a season, well before an important competition, never as the last session before one (section 8) [practice; no study opened tests it]. SP8 builds the competition-day protocol; a rehearsal is where its timings are tried.

## 8. Psychological load, and when not to train under pressure

**Psychological load is load.** The IOC's consensus statement on load in sport and risk of injury counts "psychological load and travel" as load, alongside changes in training and competition load and calendar congestion, and gives guidelines for monitoring training, competition and psychological load, athlete well-being and injury ([Soligard et al., 2016, consensus, abstract read on Loughborough University's repository](https://doi.org/10.1136/bjsports-2016-096581)). A 2024 consensus statement of seven sport injury psychology researchers states that "Stress responses are the strongest psychological risk factor for acute injuries", citing a meta-analysis this unit did not open ([Tranaeus et al., 2024, consensus, full text read at the publisher's page](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-024-02045-w)). Neither tests pressure training. Count a pressure slot as part of the week's load: put it where it replaces part of a session, never on top of the hardest physical session of the week, and keep it to two slots a week of about 15 minutes [practice; IOC and injury-psychology consensus; the studies behind them were not opened; the slot length and frequency are this unit's]. The practitioner article warns that pressure once a month is too rare to develop coping, and that not every session needs it ([Low et al., 2023](https://doi.org/10.1080/21520704.2022.2164098)) [practice].

**Add up what the athlete already carries.** Exams, selection decisions, a return from injury, a family change and short sleep are load too. In the case register: Dani's end-of-year exams in June; Milan's final-year exams in the same weeks as the academy decision; Sara's university exam periods in January and June, and her six weeks of about five hours' sleep a night. In a week like that, the athlete trains one level lower, or at level 1 [practice; the IOC consensus counts psychological load; the step is the unit's].

**When not to train under pressure** [practice; no study opened tests these rules]:

- **An athlete in distress.** Any sign on SP4's normal-or-refer table: stop pressure for that athlete, hold the first conversation, use the table and the scope card. A red flag (SP4 rows 9 and 10) stops the session for that athlete and goes by the urgent route the same day. Pressure training is training for a squad. Treating an anxious athlete belongs to a clinician. SP4 noted that exposure is a clinician's treatment; the practitioner article borrows graded exposure only as an analogy for raising pressure gradually.
- **An athlete whose referral is under way.** The athlete trains at level 3 at most until the person they were referred to has seen them; the coach then asks that person, through the route on the scope card and with the athlete's agreement, whether there is any reason not to train at higher levels, asks for no clinical details, and follows the advice. The athlete climbs only if they ask to. Reason: section 2's two youth studies report stress and panic in some players, the coach cannot tell which athlete pressure will overwhelm, and the referral exists because the coach has already seen a sign beyond ordinary nerves.
- **The days before a competition.** No new level in the last session before a competition; that session runs one level below the athlete's current level, and the day before a competition carries no pressure drill. Reason: the practitioner article notes that stopping pressure training just before competition may protect confidence if athletes struggle, and leaves the trade-off to coaches ([Low et al., 2023](https://doi.org/10.1080/21520704.2022.2164098)); this unit takes the cautious side, and SP5's rule already bans anything new from the night before.
- **An athlete new to the squad.** Level 1 only, for four weeks, and only after the athlete has practised calm the skill the drill uses. Reason: an athlete who does not yet know the coach, the squad or the climate cannot judge what a consequence means, and rule 4's step-down depends on trust that has not formed.
- **A skill not yet learned.** New techniques are practised calm first; pressure comes after (SP5's stages; the practitioner article says the same) [practice].

## 9. The pressure ladder and the step-down rule

The ladder adds one source of pressure at each level, in the order section 3's evidence suggests: first a score, then a standard, then judgement, then a single chance, then a consequence, then the whole competition. Each level keeps what the levels below it added. The ladder is untested: no study opened tests this order, these levels or this rate of climbing, and its grade is [practice] throughout. Its parts take the grades in section 3.

| Level | Name | What is added | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Scored | The drill counts: a score, a time, a points total, recorded by the athlete. | A volleyball player counts serves in the target zone out of ten. |
| 2 | Standard | A standard set from the athlete's own record (their recent mean or a small step above it), told before; the result is told to the coach. | A swimmer's relay take-over must be inside her own recent mean reaction time. |
| 3 | Watched | Someone whose view matters watches and rates the athlete against the standard: the coach, a second coach, the squad. | A gymnast's beam routine watched and scored by the coach, in the order of a competition. |
| 4 | One chance | One attempt only; no repeat. | One penalty kick; one jump at the height; one 300 m rep on the clock. |
| 5 | Consequence | A sporting consequence of missing the standard, within section 4's rules: a point lost, possession lost, going first in the next round, losing the choice of the next drill. | A tennis player who misses her first serve standard starts the next practice game at 0–15. |
| 6 | Rehearsal | A competition rehearsal on the day's timeline (section 7), with waiting, noise and a bad call added. | A judo round with a referee, a call, a wait and golden score on the clock. |

**Readiness to climb.** An athlete moves up one level, never more, when all three hold at their current level in their last full pressure session [practice; the unit's own]: they used their skill (the routine, the cue, the process goal) as planned in most attempts, by their own account; they rated the pressure 6 or below on the debrief's 0-to-10 question; and they say yes when asked whether they want to move up. Meeting the standard is not a condition: an athlete who misses it while using their skill is learning what the ladder is for. At most one climb a week.

**The step-down rule.** The athlete steps down one level, or straight to level 1, when any of these happens, and the coach says nothing about it beyond "fine, level X" [practice; the unit's own]:

1. **They ask.** No reason needed, at any moment.
2. **Distress that does not settle.** Tears, shaking, or an athlete who cannot restart the drill after the normal reset between attempts: down to level 1 for the rest of the session, and the first conversation afterwards.
3. **The skill breaks down.** By their own account, they could not use their skill in most attempts, twice running at the same level: down one level.
4. **The week is heavy.** An exam week, a selection week, illness, short sleep, a bereavement: down one level, or to level 1.
5. **A sign on SP4's table.** Stop pressure for that athlete; first conversation; SP4's table and the scope card. A red flag goes by the urgent route the same day.

Stepping down costs nothing: it is not recorded against the athlete, mentioned to others, or connected to selection (section 4, rule 3).

## 10. How to debrief a pressure session

**The evidence opened.** A meta-analysis of 46 samples (2,136 participants) of team and individual debriefs, in medical and non-medical settings, found that debriefs improved effectiveness over controls by about 25% (*d* = 0.67), with similar effects for teams and individuals, simulated and real settings and both control designs; alignment of the debrief with its purpose strengthened the effect, and facilitation and structure showed potential ([Tannenbaum & Cerasoli, 2013, abstract read on the Epistemonikos database, full text on a copy hosted by CEBMA](https://doi.org/10.1177/0018720812448394)). The abstract does not say whether any samples were athletes; the full text splits its samples into medical (28) and non-medical (18) settings, excludes respondents younger than 16 and describes none as from sport. In the basketball academy study, a coach-led group reflection followed every pressure session ([Kegelaers et al., 2021](https://doi.org/10.1080/10413200.2019.1630864)). The practitioner article suggests asking "What did you do that helped/hurt you in this drill?" and how well the athlete kept to their routine ([Low et al., 2023](https://doi.org/10.1080/21520704.2022.2164098)).

**Grade.** Debrief every pressure session: [weak, positive; one meta-analysis outside sport, rule 5; inference for under-18s]. The questions below: [practice].

**How.** Three to five minutes, straight after the drill, before the next part of the session. With a squad, in pairs or small groups, then two or three athletes report; with an individual, one to one. The athlete talks first. The coach records answers in the athlete's words and makes no judgement of the person. Results are not ranked aloud. The debrief questions:

1. "What did you plan to do?" (the skill, the routine, the cue)
2. "Did you do it? In how many attempts?"
3. "Where was your attention: on the task, on the result, on who was watching, or on how you were moving?" (SP4's two accounts)
4. "From 0 to 10, how much pressure did that feel like? Was it too little, right, or too much?"
5. "What will you keep, and what will you change next time?"
6. For the coach, afterwards: does anyone step down or climb next session (section 9)?

Question 4 measures how much pressure the drill created for this athlete, as the shooters' study measured it; it is not SP4's "how nervous" check-in, and neither question is a test of anything clinical.

## 11. The popular claims, regraded for pressure

**"Harsh coaching builds toughness."** SP1 found no peer-reviewed source for it and graded the opposite direction [weak, against]; SP3 re-examined it and kept [weak, against]. No opened study tested whether harshness adds anything. The opened pressure studies did use punishment: the basketball package made the team sprint after missed free throws, and players reported frustration at having to run ([Kegelaers et al., 2021](https://doi.org/10.1080/10413200.2019.1630864)); the forfeit that raised pressure most in the shooters was a staged media interview about missing the score, which section 4's rule 1 would itself rule out. They show that a consequence and being judged create pressure; none compared a harsh consequence with a sporting one, and the consequences in this unit's ladder are sporting ones. For the claim: no peer-reviewed source found.

**"Mental toughness is a trait you install."** No peer-reviewed source found for the claim. The one opened study that measured a related trait after pressure training, the basketball academy's individual resilience score, found no change after eight sessions, though team vulnerabilities fell in one group ([Kegelaers et al., 2021](https://doi.org/10.1080/10413200.2019.1630864)) [weak, no individual change shown; one non-randomised study]. The two-year study of mental toughness in elite young cricketers in the course's reading could not be opened, so nothing is said about it.
