# SP10 · The season plan

This unit is information, not legal, medical or psychological advice. Every technique and recommendation carries a grade in square brackets from SP1's grade key (section 9 of SP1: five rules applied in order; "several" means three or more; the labels *direction* and *inference*). *Inference* marks evidence from adults, students or non-athletes applied to an athlete under 18, as SP1 words it; where a study sampled younger athletes than the band it is applied to, the unit says "outside the sampled ages", as SP5, SP7 and SP8 do. Where the only source opened is a consensus statement whose studies were not opened, the grade is [practice] with the note "the studies behind it were not opened", as in SP2 to SP9. Debriefs after a result, resets between rounds and sharing time between athletes stay [practice], as SP8 graded them. Age bands are SP2's: about 10 to 12, 13 to 15, 16 to 18 and 19 to 25.

**How this unit is sourced.** It adds almost no new evidence. It assembles the tools of SP1 to SP9 into one plan, and every claim points to the unit and section that sourced it, in the form (SP5, section 4); the grade beside a claim is that unit's grade unless this unit says it regrades it. Six references are new, each opened for this unit because a flag on the course asked for it: two on athletic identity (section 9), two on how common mental health symptoms are in young athletes outside elite international sport (section 4) and two on coaches' own load (section 10). Each is marked where it is used, with its sample, its design and what was read.

The three case athletes return: Dani (fictional), 14, judo; Milan (fictional), 17, football; Sara (fictional), 20, 400 metres. They illustrate; they are never evidence.

## 1. What a season plan is, and what it is built from

A season plan puts on a calendar what the earlier units built as separate tools, so that each tool is used at the point in the season where it does its job and none is forgotten. It works at two levels. The **squad plan** is a calendar: which week carries which block, which session carries which slot, which competition carries which protocol, and when each review falls. The **athlete page**, the one-page formulation of section 2, says what this athlete's calendar looks like inside the squad's: their cap, their cue, their baseline, what has been referred. No study opened for this course tests a season plan for psychological skills against no plan [practice; no study opened tests it]. The plan's parts carry their own grades, and those grades travel with them.

What each unit hands to the plan:

| Unit | Tool | What the plan does with it |
|---|---|---|
| SP1 | Scope-of-practice card; grade key | Written before the season; every line of the plan carries a grade from the key. |
| SP2 | Age-and-stage table; development note | Development note rewritten each season and whenever something on it changes (SP2, Deliverable B), and before a known transition (this unit). |
| SP3 | Climate self-audit; parents' page | Audit repeated every six weeks (SP3, Deliverable A); parents' page issued before the season. |
| SP4 | Check-ins; anxiety profile; normal-or-refer table | Check-ins on fixed occasions; profile reviewed after six weeks of check-ins (SP4, Deliverable A). |
| SP5 | Eight-week skills block; skill cards | Placed early in the season; kept skills continue all season (SP5, section 4). |

## 2. The one-page formulation: a coach's working description

**What it is.** One page per athlete you are watching, assembled from the sheets you already keep: their stage (SP2's development note), their motivation and the climate they train in (SP3's audit and the term question in SP3, section 7), their anxiety profile if you have built one (SP4), the skills they have kept and the level they train at (SP5's cards; SP6's cap and routine), their baseline (SP7), their competition-day cue (SP8), and anything flagged or referred (SP9). It answers five questions on one page: what do I see, against this athlete's own baseline; what has the athlete told me, in their words; what am I doing, with what grade; what do I not know yet; and what has gone to someone else.

**What it is not.** In clinical work a formulation is, as this unit uses the word, a clinician's explanation of a person's difficulties (the unit's description; no defining source was opened). This page is a coach's working note and nothing more. It names no condition, guesses at no cause and carries no score, the same three things SP9's handover leaves out (SP9, section 8). A coach does not assess or diagnose (SP1, section 2), and a questionnaire score does not describe a disorder (SP4, section 9). Where something on the page touches mental health, eating, injury or abuse, it gets one line: what was passed on, to whom, on what date, and whether that person has seen the athlete. Nothing more goes on the page about it (SP2's development-note template asks the same of its "What I hold back" line).

**How to write it** [practice; no study opened tests a coach's written formulation; the rules are the earlier units' rules for their own sheets]:

- **Behaviour words and the athlete's words.** What you saw, dated; what the athlete said, in quotation marks, dated (SP7, section 2; SP4, Deliverable A).
- **Known and not known, apart.** A line headed "Not yet known" holds the questions you have not asked. Milan's anxiety profile does this: whether his nerves spread beyond match days is "not yet asked" (SP4, Deliverable A). An unknown written down gets asked; a guess written down hardens into a fact.
- **Every action with its grade,** copied from the unit that graded it.
- **Nothing you would not show the athlete.** The page is about them. An adult athlete can read theirs and add to it. For a minor, who may see it and how it is kept is a question: what may you record about an athlete, where may it be kept, for how long, and who may see it? Ask your club's welfare or safeguarding officer and your federation; the answer may depend on data-protection law where you coach, which this course does not state.
- **It is not the referral.** When you refer, you hand over what SP9 lists (what you saw, dates, the athlete's words with their agreement, what you changed in training), never the page (SP9, section 8).

**When** [practice; the earlier units' review dates]. Write it once the baseline is built, after four to six weeks of ordinary training (SP7, Deliverable A); rewrite it each term and whenever a line on it changes. SP3's audit, SP4's profile review and SP9's term check all fall on dates the season plan fixes, so the rewrite does too.

## 3. Planning for a squad, and planning for one athlete

**What the squad shares.** The climate (SP3), the skills block (SP5), the pressure block and its rules (SP6), the skeleton of the competition-day protocol (SP8), the parents' page (SP3, SP8) and the referral plan (SP9) are written once, for the whole squad. The evidence behind the squad-level tools comes from programmes run with groups: teaching psychological skills inside training is [moderate, positive; samples mostly adult where ages are reported, so for athletes under 18 an inference] (SP5, section 2), training under pressure is [moderate, positive; inference for under-18s] (SP6, section 2), and a pre-performance routine is [moderate, positive; ages 11 to 40] (SP6, section 5). Teach the skills yourself, inside your own sessions: in one meta-analysis of randomised trials in athletes, read in abstract, which gives no ages, there was some evidence that effects were greatest when coaches delivered the intervention [weak, positive] (SP5, section 2).

**What is individual.** Five things in the squad plan change for one athlete:

- **The ceiling.** Each athlete's pressure cap is the lowest that applies: the band cap (3 at 10 to 12, 4 at 13 to 15, 6 from 16), 3 while a referral is under way, 1 for four weeks when new to the squad (SP6, section 9) [practice].
- **The routine and the cue.** Built by the athlete from skills they have practised calm (SP6, Deliverable B; SP8, section 2) [moderate, positive, for the routine; practice, for the one-cue rule].
- **The baseline.** What ordinary looks like for this athlete (SP7) [practice].
- **The heavy weeks.** Exams, selection, injury, short sleep: one level lower that week, or level 1 (SP6, section 8) [practice].
- **The referral status.** Whether a referral is under way, whether the person has seen the athlete, and what they said about training (SP6, section 8; SP9, section 8) [practice].

**Why both levels.** The meta-analyses behind the squad tools pooled averages; individual athletes varied, and an athlete for whom a skill does nothing is common (SP5, section 5). A squad plan with no individual layer pushes every athlete through the same weeks at the same level. An individual plan with no squad layer costs more minutes than any coach has (section 10). The rule this unit uses: the squad plan sets the calendar; the athlete's page sets the ceiling and the exceptions [practice; the unit's own].

**A squad spans bands.** Dani's (fictional) club squad runs from 11 to 17: three bands, three pressure caps (SP6, Deliverable A) and three ways of teaching each skill (SP5, section 3). A swimming club's competition group of 12- to 18-year-olds, a volleyball academy's under-18s with a 15-year-old called up, and a gymnastics squad from 10 to 16 face the same layering. Write the squad's calendar once; write the band rows beneath it once; then the individual pages.

## 4. Placing the blocks across the season

A season here has three phases: pre-season, the competitive season (with any breaks inside it) and the off-season. The placing rules come from the units that built each block.

**The skills block (SP5).** Eight weeks, ten minutes in two sessions a week, one new skill a fortnight, each skill explained, practised calm, practised under light pressure and then used in a competition that matters less (SP5, sections 4 and 6; Deliverable A) [practice, for the sequence; moderate, positive, for skills training as a whole, with inference for under-18s]. Two placements fit those rules. Pre-season, where the competitions that matter less are friendlies, time trials or club meets: the first uses in the block's even weeks land there. Or a quiet phase of the competitive season, with no selection decision and no championship inside the eight weeks. Pre-season is the better fit where the sport's pre-season has competitions; a sport whose pre-season has none (a crew whose winter base phase has no racing, for example) uses the first quiet weeks of racing [practice; the unit's own reading of SP5's first-use rule].

How long a skill takes to learn has no direct source in this course. SP5 inferred weeks to months from how long the interventions it opened lasted, and from two dose findings: imagery effects larger after 100 days than after 20, and an eight-week self-talk programme that changed coach-rated performance where a one-week programme did not (SP5, section 4). That is SP5's inference, and this unit uses it as one. So the block introduces each skill; it does not finish it. Each skill an athlete keeps at the block's review continues for the rest of the season, a few minutes a week [weak, positive, for longer practice; moderator results and one randomised comparison] (SP5, section 4). The plan gives the kept skill five minutes a week [practice; the five minutes are this unit's number].

**The pressure block (SP6).** Four weeks, after the skills block, so that the skills it puts under pressure have been practised calm (SP6, section 12) [moderate, positive, for pressure training; inference for under-18s; practice, for the ladder and its rules]. SP6, section 8 sets the rules the plan obeys:

- Two slots a week of about 15 minutes, at most; each replaces part of a session, never on top of the hardest physical session of the week.
- No new level in the last session before a competition, which runs one level below the athlete's current level; no pressure drill the day before.
- In a heavy week (exams, selection, illness, short sleep), one level lower, or level 1.
- A rehearsal of the competition's timeline (level 6) once or twice a season, well before an important competition, never as the last session before one, and only for athletes whose cap reaches 6 (SP6, section 7).

The ladder is climbed one level a week at most (SP6, section 9), and a four-week block reaches level 5 at most for an athlete who starts at level 1 or 2. So the rehearsal belongs to a second block later in the season. Between blocks, the plan keeps one 15-minute slot a week at each athlete's current level, with no climbing [practice; the unit's own; SP6 sets two slots as the most, and its practitioner source warns that pressure once a month is too rare to develop coping].

**The competition-day protocol (SP8).** Run for every competition from the first, with the rules for that event checked and dated beforehand, and the debrief card at an agreed time afterwards, usually the next session (SP8, Deliverables A and B) [practice]. A competition over several days runs each day's protocol from the top (SP8, section 10) [practice].

**The referral plan (SP9).** Contacts checked before the season and at the start of each term; the plan reviewed after any incident (SP9, Deliverable B) [practice; consensus; the studies behind it were not opened]. SP9 gave the reason for writing it before the season: symptoms are common enough at every level studied that the route should exist before the first crisis (SP9, section 1). SP9 found figures only for elite adults and for elite youth athletes of mean age 12 to 17 who compete internationally, and nothing for club athletes or for the band 10 to 12. Two studies were opened for this unit to look at that gap; neither closes it:

- **Ages 6 to 18, mixed levels.** In 756 athletes aged 6 to 18 (mean 13.5) seen at one sports injury prevention centre, 8% reported having anxiety or depression diagnosed by a clinician, answered in a single self-report item that did not separate the two; the figure was 13% in athletes who played only individual sports and 7% in team-sport athletes, a difference that was no longer significant after adjustment. The authors note that participants were patients at the centre, not a random sample, and that the answers were not checked against medical records ([Pluhar et al., 2019, full text read at the publisher's page](https://www.jssm.org/jssm-18-490.xml-Fulltext)). One cross-sectional study of a clinic population, with no club-by-club or band-by-band breakdown.
- **Club under-20 players aged 18 to 20.** In 62 male under-20 footballers at three Brazilian clubs, measured once on a standard anxiety and depression questionnaire, 9 (14.5%) scored above its cut-off for anxiety symptoms and 4 (6.5%) above its cut-off for depression symptoms ([Rodrigues et al., 2023, full text read at the publisher's page](https://doi.org/10.1590/1517-8692202329012021_0385)). One small cross-sectional sample from one city; a score above a cut-off is a reason for a clinician to look, never a diagnosis (SP9, section 1).

Neither changes the plan: the route is written before the season whatever the figure for your squad [practice; SP9, section 1]. For club athletes aged 10 to 12, nothing was found here either.

**The off-season.** No pressure block and no new skill [practice; the unit's own]. It holds the end-of-season review (section 8), the rewriting of each development note (SP2, Deliverable B) and of each baseline sheet where an athlete has grown, moved or changed squad (SP7, Deliverable A), the conversations a transition needs (section 9), and the question SP2 asks once a season: what does the athlete want from sport next year, and from life outside it (SP2, section 7) [practice; ISSP consensus; the studies behind it were not opened]. For athletes of about 10 to 15, the off-season is where other sports fit: keep other sports and varied practice in their week and across their year [moderate, positive, for the association between early multi-sport practice and later world-class performance] (SP2, section 9); and for about 10 to 12, SP2's table holds back more than 8 months a year in one sport, the NSCA statement's limit [practice; NSCA consensus; the studies behind it were not opened] (SP2, Deliverable A).

**Three sports, one rule.** A swimmer whose season builds to one championship places the skills block in the autumn base phase, whose time trials are the lower-stakes first uses, and the pressure block before the winter gala series. A volleyball team with a league from autumn to spring places the skills block in pre-season, around its friendly tournaments. A gymnast whose competitions cluster in two short windows places each block in the quiet months before a window, with its timeline rehearsal six or more weeks before the first meet. Each is the same rule applied to a different calendar: skills before pressure, pressure before the competitions that matter, nothing new in the last days [practice; SP5, section 6; SP6, sections 7 and 8].

## 5. Parents across a season

SP3 graded a short, specific briefing for parents at the start of the season [weak, positive: programmes evaluated in a few small studies of mixed design; practice, for the page's format] (SP3, section 9), and built the page; SP8 added competition-day paragraphs to it [weak, for the behaviours young athletes said they wanted from parents, one qualitative study of 12- to 15-year-old girls in team sports; for boys, other ages and individual sports, inference; practice, for the format] (SP8, section 9). Both left the page's place in the season to this unit. The evidence is thin: SP3's review of parent-education programmes found 12 studies of mixed design and quality, and warned that they prevent firm conclusions (SP3, section 9). So the placing below is [practice; the unit's own]:

- **Before the season.** Issue SP3's page with SP8's competition-day paragraphs, in writing and in person, before the first competition. Where the first competition falls in the first week, issue it before the season starts.
- **Each term.** Reread it against the climate audit: if the three changes you are making have changed, the page's "What we aim for" paragraph changes too (SP3, Deliverable C).
- **Before a selection decision.** Remind parents where the criteria are written, who explains the decision and when, and that questions go to the coach in private (SP3, section 5 and Deliverable C).
- **After something on the day goes wrong.** A parent at the edge of the mat, a shout at an official, a car-ride post-mortem the athlete has told you about: say it once, privately, against the page. Dani's (fictional) father stands at the edge of the mat (case register); the competition-day note asks for a seat in the stands and gives the reason (SP8, Deliverable C).
- **For adult athletes,** the page goes to the athlete, who decides what to share (SP3, Deliverable C; SP8, section 9). Sara's (fictional) parents messaged her coach about her slower times; her coach replied only as she agreed (SP9, Deliverable C).

A concern about a parent's behaviour towards a child is not a page to reissue: it goes to the club's welfare or safeguarding officer (SP3, section 9) [practice; the course's scope line], and DD-SP1 deals with it.

## 6. Working with a sport psychologist

**Who you might be working with.** SP1 opened a guidance document hosted by the Association for Applied Sport Psychology that describes two routes into the work: a sport-science route whose graduates teach mental skills and usually do not become licensed psychologists, and a psychology route whose graduates most often become licensed psychologists and can work with clinical problems (SP1, section 1). Whether "sport psychologist" is a protected title where you coach, and who may assess or treat a minor, are questions for your national psychology regulator, your national health regulator and your federation's medical or safeguarding officer (SP1, section 1); SP1's scope card has a line for the answers (SP1, Deliverable A).

**What to ask for** [practice; no study opened tests any division of work between a coach and a practitioner]:

1. **A check of your plan.** The skills block, the pressure rules and the competition protocol, read by someone trained in the field before the season.
2. **Help teaching a skill,** with you in the session. Keep the skills inside your own sessions: the one moderator result opened found some evidence that effects were greatest when coaches delivered the intervention [weak, positive] (SP5, section 2).
3. **A view on the climate,** from a second audit by an observer (SP3, Deliverable A).
4. **Clinical work only by referral.** An athlete who needs assessment or treatment goes by the route on your referral plan to a licensed or registered practitioner (SP9, section 8). A practitioner trained to teach skills may not be one: a review SP5 opened states that treating athletes for mental illness is ethically beyond many sport psychology practitioners' professional competencies (SP5, section 1).

**How to brief them.** For the squad: one page with the squad's size and bands, the season calendar, what you teach and when (SP5's cards), the pressure rules and caps (SP6, sections 4 and 9), and your referral plan (SP9). For one athlete, only with the athlete's agreement, and for a minor as your plan says: what SP9 lists for any handover, which is what you saw, in behaviour words and with dates, the athlete's words if they agree, what you have changed in training, and how to reach you; no label, no cause, no score (SP9, section 8) [practice].

**What stays with you.** The climate, the session plan, selection and playing time, the athlete's place in the squad, your daily noticing against the baseline, and the referral route. None of these moves to the practitioner. Selection stays independent of anything the practitioner does, as it stays independent of a referral (SP9, section 8). What you do not receive is the content of their work with an athlete: you ask how the athlete is, not what was said, and you ask a practitioner about training in one question that describes the drill and not the athlete (SP9, section 8) [practice; consensus, for asking how they are, the studies behind it not opened; SP9's own and untested, for the one question].

**Where there is none.** The course is built for that case: every tool in the plan is one a coach runs alone, and the only part that needs a professional is the referral route, which leads to a doctor or a licensed or registered mental health professional; a sport psychologist is on that route only if licensed or registered for clinical work (SP1, section 1; SP9, section 8).

## 7. What you can track, and what you cannot conclude from it

You can keep a record of six things across a season without running a questionnaire or scoring anyone's mood.

| What | Where it is recorded | What it can show | What it cannot show |
|---|---|---|---|
| Attendance and leaving times | The register; the baseline sheet's "arrival" and "leaving" lines (SP7) | A change against this athlete's own pattern, as SP7's withdrawn row reads it | Why it changed |
| Enjoyment | The answers to the term question: what do you enjoy in training, and what would you change (SP3, section 7) | What your athletes say, term by term | Whether your plan raised it |
| Check-ins | The two questions, on fixed occasions, in the athlete's words (SP4, section 9) | One athlete's baseline and change against it | A disorder; a comparison between athletes |
| Whether routines and skills were used | SP6's debrief question 2; step 3 of SP8's debrief card | Whether the athlete did what they practised, by their own account | Whether it caused the result |
| Your own behaviours | SP3's self-audit, every six weeks; your touchline process goal (SP8, section 11) | Whether your counts moved | Whether the athletes changed because of it |
| Results | Competition records | Results | What the athlete controlled (SP8, section 7) |

Enjoyment is worth the term question because, in a meta-analysis of young athletes aged 5 to 19, it was the strongest correlate of intending to continue in sport [moderate, positive, for the association] (SP3, section 7). Your own behaviour is worth recounting because, in the one randomised trial of a coach education programme opened for the course, the gain in observed autonomy support faded within the season [weak, positive, for training your own behaviour deliberately; coaches of players aged 10 to 15, so for 16 to 25 outside the sampled ages] (SP3, section 3). SP3 also set a limit on the audit: its counts are for comparing yourself over time, never for comparing coaches (SP3, Deliverable A).

**What one squad cannot tell you.** Your squad is one group with no comparison group. SP1 set out what that design can and cannot show: without a control group, an improvement could come from practice, from growing up, from the season, or from the athletes expecting to improve; a pre-to-post comparison shows that people changed, not that the technique changed them (SP1, section 4). So if your squad's enjoyment answers rise from one term to the next, or Milan's (fictional) match-morning ratings fall, you have a description of change. You do not have evidence that the block caused it, and you cannot show the plan works by pointing to your results [practice; SP1, section 4's account of what a design without a control can show]. Most motivational-climate research has the same limit: measured once, it cannot tell whether a coach's behaviour caused an athlete's state, and an anxious athlete may also see the coach as harsher (SP3, the warning before section 1).

Two rules follow [practice]: record change in neutral words, without a claim of cause; and never let anything tracked here touch selection (SP6, section 4, rule 3; SP3, section 5).

## 8. Reviewing, and dropping what does not work

**Review points.** The plan has five kinds:

- **Weekly,** ten minutes, by the procedure in the deliverable (C).
- **At the end of each block:** SP5's week-8 question, "Which of these is yours?", recorded per athlete (SP5, Deliverable A); SP6's review of each athlete's level after the block's last week (SP6, Deliverable A).
- **Every six weeks:** the climate self-audit (SP3, Deliverable A).

**For the squad: when a tool does not earn its minutes.** Since section 7 rules out judging a tool by your squad's results, judge it on three things you can see [practice; the unit's own]:

1. **Is it used?** If no athlete keeps a skill at the block's review, or no one uses it in the debriefs that follow, take it out of the squad plan and leave it on the card for anyone who asks.
2. **Do the athletes say it helps?** Their answer to the review question and to the debrief, in their words.
3. **What does it cost?** Its minutes (section 10) against what the first two answers say.

## 9. The transitions a season contains

Some of the transitions in the brief can be dated in advance and some cannot. SP2 took the ISSP position stand's distinction between normative transitions, which are predictable (junior to senior, retirement), and non-normative ones, which are not (injury, deselection) (SP2, section 7). The plan writes the predictable ones into the calendar and keeps a page ready for the rest.

**Selection and deselection.** SP3 set the steps [practice; no study opened tests them]: say in advance what selection depends on and keep to it; never use selection as a threat; tell a deselected athlete the reason in private, with what they can work on (SP3, section 5). For athletes under about 15, SP2 adds two checks before a decision: judge size, speed and strength against how far through growth the athlete is [moderate, positive, for the maturity–performance association in boys; practice, for the selection rule] (SP2, section 2), and ask whether you would make the same call if the athlete were six months older [practice; the existence of relative age effects moderate] (SP2, section 3). A course flag asked this unit to open an interview study of coaches on how deselection is run (Neely et al., 2016); no search returned its publisher's page or its DOI address, so nothing from it is reported. The plan [practice; the unit's own] puts each selection date in the calendar, with the parents' reminder (section 5) the week before and a private conversation with each deselected athlete the same week. A deselected athlete whose distress lasts or spreads goes by SP4's table and the referral plan (SP4, Deliverable B; SP9).

**Moving up an age group.** Where age groups span two years or more, an athlete who was among the oldest in one group is among the youngest in the next (this unit's arithmetic, not a finding); SP2 reported a meta-analysis in which the relative age effect grew with the gap in relative age, and treats relative age and maturation as separate clocks (SP2, section 3). At each move, recheck three things in the plan: the athlete's band, which changes the pressure cap (SP6, section 9) and how each skill is taught (SP5, section 3); their place in the selection year (SP2, Deliverable B); and their baseline, which SP7 rewrites after a move (SP7, Deliverable A; a new squad and new coaches are this unit's reading of a move) [practice]. In a weight-class sport the move can raise a question about weight or class. Dani's (fictional) wish to move up a weight class is hers to raise with her parents and the club's medical contact, with no comment from her coach on weight, body or food (SP2, Deliverable B) [practice; the course's safety rule]; DD-SP2 deals with it.

**Junior to senior.** The ISSP stand describes this transition as lasting months to years (SP2, section 7). In the meta-analysis SP2 opened, higher-performing juniors had entered talent programmes younger, and higher-performing seniors had entered them older [strong, for the association; correlational] (SP2, section 10). Plan it as a transition that lasts months or years: the first senior season gets its own development note, a say in the season plan, and study or work kept in the plan [practice; ISSP; the studies behind it were not opened] (SP2, section 7 and Deliverable A). Sara (fictional) is in it now, with a return from injury and university at the same time (SP2, section 7).

**Injury.** An injury and the months back from it are a risk factor the sources SP9 opened name for mental health, and physical and psychological readiness to return do not necessarily coincide (SP9, sections 2 and 9). Return is decided by the doctor or physiotherapist managing the injury (SP8, section 10; SP9, section 9) [practice]. While an athlete is injured, the plan keeps them in the squad's life [practice; the unit's own], and their pressure cap stays where SP6 and SP9 put it until the person advising them has answered (SP9, section 9) [practice; consensus; the studies behind it were not opened]. A cyclist off the bike with a broken collarbone can still sit in on the skills block; a long jumper with a hamstring tear can still run their imagery scene.

**Leaving the sport.** The ISSP stand, as SP2 read it, reports a review of 126 studies finding that adjustment to the end of a career depends on athletic identity, education and finances, and is helped by planning, a new career or interest, and support (SP2, section 7). That review (Park et al., 2013) was not opened for SP2, and a course flag asked this unit to open it: no search returned its publisher's page or its DOI address, so it carries no claim here either. Athletic identity is how far a person defines themselves by sport (SP2, section 7). Two measurement studies of it were opened for this unit:

- **Ages 12 to 18.** In 259 male youth academy footballers aged 12 to 18, confirmatory factor analyses supported both a 7-item single-factor version of the Athletic Identity Measurement Scale and a three-factor version (social identity, exclusivity and negative affectivity), though not within the same analysis ([Mitchell et al., 2021, abstract read at the publisher's page](https://doi.org/10.25035/jade.03.03.04)).
- **Adults.** A Brazilian adaptation of the same scale was tested on 127 physical education students and compared with athletes. The original three factors were not confirmed (the items loaded on two), retest reliability was high (ICC 0.91), and comparing athletes with students gave what the authors call "good discriminant validity". The full text at the publisher's page, confirmed by the quality review on 19 Nov 2026, adds: a mean age of 21.6 for the students; 90 athletes in football, swimming and futsal, also used to check the clarity of the wording, whose ages are not given; a 14-day retest interval; and athletes scoring higher than students ([Silva et al., 2016, full text read at the publisher's page](https://doi.org/10.1590/S1980-65742016000100006)).

So a measure exists, and its structure differs between the two samples. Neither study measured athletic identity's link with adjustment after leaving sport, and neither asks a coach to score athletes. Do not run it on your squad [practice; the unit's own reading of the two studies]. Use SP2's question once a season, what the athlete wants from sport this year and from life outside it, and keep study, work and other interests in the plan [practice; ISSP consensus; the studies behind it were not opened] (SP2, section 7). Young athletes leave sport for many reasons, among them lack of enjoyment, failure to improve, pressure and conflict with the coach, as SP3's review of dropout from swimming reported from six studies of mixed design, in swimmers aged 5 to 30 (SP3, section 7); for other sports that is this unit's inference; the term question on enjoyment is where you hear it first.

**Why count.** Minutes are where a plan fails. Two sources were opened for this unit because SP9 found nothing on coaches outside elite sport (SP9, section 11); only the first is known to be about such coaches:

- In 688 unpaid Swedish youth football coaches, most of them coaching players aged 10 to 15, surveyed once with a two-item stress index the authors note was not a validated instrument, overall stress was low; among the strongest predictors of the stress coaches did report were the demands of their main job, difficulty catching up with family and not having enough time to plan activities ([Hertting et al., 2020, full text read at the publisher's page](https://doi.org/10.3390/sports8110146)). ### C. Ten-minute weekly review

Once a week, at the same time, with the week's sheets in front of you: the register, the baseline sheets, the check-in lines, the debrief answers, the ladder record and next week's calendar. The order puts what cannot wait first [practice; the unit's own; each step uses the unit named].
