## Preamble

Write one unit of a self-study course in the long-form report format of the Learning Project's markdown courses: a long-form report in Markdown. Length is whatever the topic needs (2,000–6,000 words); do not pad and do not cut to a target. Reader: a coach of athletes aged about 10 to 25, in any sport, individual or team, from club to national level, paid or volunteer; experienced in coaching the sport, with no training in psychology; trains athletes several times a week and stands beside them at competitions; some of those athletes already win at national level and some are years into a struggle with competition nerves; reads in order (each unit assumes the ones before it), reads on a phone, checks the citations, and wants genuine depth rather than a primer. Address the reader as 'you'. This course is written to be shared with anyone; it is an exception to the Learning Project's single-reader rule (decided 16 Nov 2026). Nothing about the Project's owner, his sport, his club or his athletes appears in it. Scope: the reader is a coach, not a clinician. The course teaches what a coach may do: shape the training climate, teach psychological skills, notice, respond and refer. No unit teaches assessment, diagnosis or treatment, and every unit that touches mental health, eating, injury or abuse says where the coach's part ends and who takes over. Any sport: state every principle so that it holds for any sport; draw the examples in each unit from at least three kinds of sport (among them an individual sport and a team sport and, where the topic calls for it, a weight-class, a judged or an endurance sport); no single sport supplies more than a third of a unit's examples. Ages: say which age band a claim holds for (about 10 to 12, 13 to 15, 16 to 18, 19 to 25) and what the study's sample was (children, adolescents, students, adult athletes, non-athletes); a finding from adults, students or non-athletes applied to younger athletes is labelled as the unit's inference. Evidence grades: every technique and every recommendation carries one grade from the key set in SP1: strong (consistent meta-analytic evidence in athletes), moderate (a meta-analysis of small or mixed studies, or several controlled studies in athletes), weak (single studies, or studies outside sport), contested (failed replications, or reviews that disagree), practice (professional consensus or coaching practice with no controlled test); give the effect size and the sample where the source gives them, in the source's own terms. Popular claims: where a claim is common in coaching culture and the evidence does not support it, name the claim and say what the evidence shows; the course's list of such claims is in the seed bibliography. Running case: three fictional athletes and their coaches: Dani, 14, judo; Milan, 17, football; Sara, 20, 400 metres. Their facts come only from courses/sport-psychology/notes/case-facts.md; label each '(fictional)' on first use in the unit; a fact the unit invents about them is listed in the notes so that it can be added to the register; the case is never evidence for a claim about the world. Deliverables are built twice: in full on the case athletes the brief names, then as a template the reader fills for their own athletes, each blank saying what goes in it. Safety rules that hold in every unit: no weight-loss method, calorie figure or target weight is given; nothing describes a method of self-harm; no law is stated as the reader's: a legal or reporting duty is put as a question with the body to consult, and the unit says it is information, not legal, medical or psychological advice; no real athlete, coach or club is named as an example of abuse, illness or failure unless a cited court, public inquiry or peer-reviewed source names them, and then only as that source states it. Tone: direct, no flattery, no filler, no motivational language, no 'not X but Y' constructions. Course goal: {GOAL} Sourcing standard: every claim that carries a number, a mechanism or a recommendation is sourced where it is made; acceptable sources are consensus and position statements of the IOC, ISSP, FEPSAC, AASP, AAP, ACSM and national bodies of the same kind (labelled as consensus), systematic reviews and meta-analyses, peer-reviewed primary studies, standard textbooks and handbooks cited by edition and chapter, and, for safeguarding and rules, the issuing body's own page (dated); videos, podcasts, social-media posts, blogs, coaching influencers, popular books and press reports of a study are not sources, and may be named only as the claim under examination; every reference is opened before it is cited: found by a search, read at its publisher's page, PubMed or its DOI address, and recorded in the unit's source log with the address, what was read (abstract or full text) and the date; a claim that rests on an abstract alone says so; a reference that could not be opened carries no claim; the seed bibliography (courses/sport-psychology/notes/bibliography.md) is a starting list whose entries were checked for existence only, never an authority on what they say; a study's finding is stated with its sample and design, and a correlation is called a correlation; no uncited figures; no invented names, studies, quotations or numbers; if a claim cannot be sourced, say so and leave it out of the deliverable. A unit that reads as advice without evidence is a failed unit. Links are given as links (the DOI address for a paper), so the course bibliography can collect them. Figures: where a section's content has a shape, declare it as a fenced viz block of a kind kit/viz.py draws (one to four a unit is a guide; none where nothing has a shape); a figure adds no fact, and its caption says what it shows and where its data comes from. Where the unit depends on current facts (safeguarding codes and reporting routes, federation rules, referral tools and services), search before writing and date the facts. End with the unit's deliverable, built out in full, not described.

## SP1

Unit SP1, What a coach can do, and how to tell evidence from noise. Cover: what sport psychology is and the professions in it (who may call themselves what, and who may treat, differs by country: put it as a question for the reader, with the kind of body to consult); the coach's part as the literature defines it (shaping the climate, teaching psychological skills, noticing, responding, referring) and where it ends (assessment, diagnosis, treatment); the kinds of evidence in this field (consensus statements, meta-analyses, controlled studies, correlational studies, case reports) and what each can show; how to read an effect size, a sample and a control group, in plain words; why most studies are small and on students or adults, and what that means for a coach of a 13-year-old; what the reviews of meta-analyses say about how far psychological variables and interventions relate to performance, with their limits; the replication record as cautionary cases, each from its replication study or meta-analysis (power posing, ego depletion, grit, the size of growth-mindset effects); the popular claims on the course's list, each stated as it circulates and then set against its evidence, saying 'no peer-reviewed source found' where none was; the course's five evidence grades, defined so that two readers would give the same grade; five questions to ask of any claim met in a video, a clinic or a book. Deliverable: a scope-of-practice card (what I do, what I refer, to whom; blanks for the reader's country, federation and club); a claim-check sheet of five questions, worked in full on three claims from the list; and the grade key every later unit uses, with one worked example per grade.

## SP2

Unit SP2, The developing athlete, 10 to 25. Cover: chronological age, biological maturation and training age as three clocks that run apart; the timing of puberty and what early and late maturation do to performance, selection and self-image; relative age effects; what is established about brain development through adolescence and into the twenties (protracted, different by region and by person, with no cutoff age), with the claim that the brain 'finishes at 25' examined against its sources; sensitivity to reward and to evaluation by peers, and what does not follow from a brain image; self-regulation and emotion regulation across the four age bands; identity and athletic identity from 16 to 25, the move from junior to senior, and the end of a competitive career in the twenties; sleep need by age and what the consensus statements say about sleep, mood, learning and injury; early specialisation against sampling, from the consensus statements and the studies of who becomes a champion; how weakly early success predicts adult success, and what that means for how a coach treats a champion of 13 and a late developer of 17. Deliverable: an age-and-stage table (four age bands by what is changing, what to ask for, what to hold back, what to watch), each cell graded; and a one-page development note per athlete (the three clocks, sleep, school load, what changed this year), built in full for Dani, Milan and Sara, then as a template.

## SP3

Unit SP3, Motivation and the climate you create. Cover: self-determination theory (autonomy, competence, relatedness; supporting a need against thwarting it) and achievement goal theory (task-involving and ego-involving climates), each with its evidence in youth sport and that evidence's design; what autonomy-supportive coaching behaviours are, one by one, and what controlling behaviours are, one by one, with what each has been found to go with (enjoyment, anxiety, effort, dropout, burnout) and whether the finding is correlational; what coach-training interventions have shown, with their samples; rewards, punishment and exercise used as punishment; selection, playing time and attention as the currencies a coach controls; high challenge together with high support, and what that model rests on; enjoyment and the reasons young athletes leave sport; burnout and overtraining as the consensus statements describe them; parents as part of the climate. Deliverable: a climate self-audit of twenty observable coach behaviours scored from one observed or filmed session; three session plans, each written twice (the same drill under controlling instructions and under autonomy-supportive ones) for an individual, a team and a judged or timed sport; and a one-page briefing for parents; all built on the three case squads, then as templates.

## SP4

Unit SP4, Anxiety, arousal and performing under pressure. Cover: the vocabulary (arousal, stress, anxiety; trait and state; cognitive and somatic), kept apart; what the meta-analytic data show about anxiety, confidence and performance, and the limits of the inverted-U as coaches are taught it; challenge and threat states; attention under pressure, with the distraction and the self-focus accounts of choking set side by side and what each predicts; what competition anxiety looks like at each age band, in training and in competition, and why an athlete can train well and compete badly; how competition anxiety is kept going over years (avoidance, reassurance, fear of evaluation, the meaning given to a result) and what coaches and parents do that feeds it without meaning to; competition anxiety that is ordinary against anxiety that needs a clinician (how long, how wide, how much it stops the athlete doing), as recognition only; short check-ins a coach may use, and what a coach must not conclude from a questionnaire. Deliverable: an athlete anxiety profile (when, where, the thoughts, the body, the behaviour, what has helped, what the coach does before and after), built in full for Milan; and a normal-or-refer table (sign, ordinary range, what to do, when to refer), then both as templates.

## SP5

Unit SP5, The cognitive-behavioural toolkit. Cover: the cognitive-behavioural model in plain words (situation, thought, feeling, body, behaviour) and the line between a coach teaching a skill and a clinician treating a problem; what the meta-analyses of psychological skills training show overall, and on whom; then each skill with what it is, its evidence, its grade and how it is taught to each age band: goal setting (outcome, performance and process goals, and what the sport meta-analysis found about each kind), self-talk (instructional and motivational, matched to the task), imagery, breathing and relaxation (saying what the breathing studies measured), attentional focus (external against internal), and mindfulness and acceptance approaches as the alternative to control, with what the reviews say about the quality of their evidence; how a skill is taught (explain, practise calm, practise under pressure, use in competition) and how long that takes; what to do when an athlete does not take to a skill; why a skill is never first handed over on competition day. Deliverable: an eight-week skills block that fits inside normal sessions (ten minutes, one new skill a fortnight, the words the coach says), built in full for Milan's squad and for Sara as an individual athlete; and one card per skill (what, when, script, grade, sample it was tested on, when not to use it), then the templates.

## SP6

Unit SP6, Training under pressure. Cover: why a skill learned calm can fail in competition; what the meta-analysis of pressure training found, in which domains and with what samples; the ways of making pressure in practice (consequences, being watched and judged, one attempt only, fatigue, noise, bad calls, waiting) and what is known about each; which of them are acceptable with minors and which are not, stated as rules of this unit with their reasons (no humiliation, no physical punishment, no threat to an athlete's place in the group, a way to step down); pre-performance routines, with their meta-analysis; what the review of choking interventions found worked and did not; rehearsing the competition day itself; how to debrief a pressure session; psychological load as load, added to the physical; when not to train under pressure (an athlete in distress, the days before a competition, an athlete new to the squad). Deliverable: a four-week pressure-training block for a squad (a ladder of six pressure levels, the session slot, the rule for stepping an athlete down, the debrief questions), built in full for Dani's and Milan's squads; and a pre-performance routine builder the athlete fills in, built for Sara; then the templates.

## SP7

Unit SP7, Reading and responding to an athlete. Cover: what the science of emotion supports about reading a feeling from a face or a posture, and what follows for a coach; observing behaviour instead of inferring feeling: an athlete's baseline, a deviation from it, and asking; emotion regulation strategies and how they develop across the four age bands; how one person regulates another's emotion, and what that means with younger athletes; the coach's own expressed emotion and what it has been found to do to athletes; what an athlete asks for (reassurance, to be left alone, to be told what to do, to be let off) against what serves their autonomy, competence and relatedness (SP3) and does not feed their anxiety (SP4), as a decision made each time, with the reasoning shown; the common states as a coach sees them (frozen, tearful, angry, flat, over-aroused, overconfident, withdrawn), each with what to ask, what to do, what not to do and the grade of that advice; feedback after an error; differences between athletes, between sexes and between cultures, only as far as a source supports them; athletes with a diagnosis that affects attention or emotion, put as questions for the athlete and the family, with nothing asserted that is not sourced; getting it wrong, and repair. Deliverable: a baseline sheet per athlete (what ordinary looks like in training, before and after competing); a state-response table of the seven states; and five asking scripts; built in full for Dani, Milan and Sara, then as templates.

## SP8

Unit SP8, Competition day. Cover: the day in phases (the evening before, travel and arrival, warm-up, waiting, performing, between rounds or periods, straight after, the days after) and what the coach controls in each; the last minute: how much instruction an athlete can use under pressure, and the case for one cue; the coach's behaviour and expressed emotion on the sideline and what it has been found to do; resets between rounds, periods or attempts; what a sport's rules allow a coach to say and when, put as a question for the reader's sport; several athletes competing on one day, and who gets the coach when; after a loss and after a win: when to talk, what to talk about, what the athlete can control and what they cannot, and how that differs at 12 and at 20; a first title and an early title, and what the days after them need; parents on the day; competitions over several days; the coach's own nerves and how to manage them, using the skills of SP5. Deliverable: a competition-day protocol (phase by coach action, the words, what not to do), built in full three times: for a sport of bouts (Dani), a team match (Milan) and a timed event (Sara); a debrief card for after a loss and after a win; and a note to parents; then the templates.

## SP9

Unit SP9, Wellbeing, red flags and referral. Search before writing. Cover: how common mental health symptoms are in elite and in young athletes, from the meta-analysis and reviews, with their limits; risk and protective factors; what the consensus and position statements ask of coaches and of clubs; recognising, never diagnosing: the signs a coach can see of low mood, of anxiety that has spread beyond competition, of burnout and overtraining, of disordered eating and low energy availability, of sleep trouble, of a bad response to injury, and of perfectionism turning harmful, each with how long and how much before it is a concern; the IOC's recognition tool for people who are not clinicians: what it is, who it is for and what it does not do, read on its own page and dated; the first conversation: where, the words, what not to promise, the limits of confidentiality with a minor, and when parents are told; urgent risk: what a coach does when an athlete talks about harming themselves or not wanting to live, as immediate steps and the local emergency and crisis services the reader looks up now, with nothing that describes a method; the referral route: to whom, how, what to hand over, and how to stay the athlete's coach afterwards; return and follow-up; the coach's own wellbeing. Deliverable: a red-flag table (sign, how long, what to do, who to call); a referral plan with blanks for the reader's local services, filled by the reader before they need it; and three conversation scripts (with the athlete, with a parent, with the club); built in full for Sara, then as templates.

## SP10

Unit SP10, The season plan. Cover: a one-page formulation of an athlete, assembled from the sheets of SP2 to SP9 (stage, motivation, anxiety profile, skills, baseline, flags), as a coach's working description and never a diagnosis; planning for a squad against planning for one athlete; placing the skills block (SP5), the pressure block (SP6) and the competition protocol (SP8) across pre-season, the competitive season and the off-season; parents across a season; working with a sport psychologist where one is available: what to ask for, how to brief them, what stays with the coach; what a coach can track (attendance, enjoyment, check-ins, whether routines are used, the coach's own behaviours from SP3) and what cannot be concluded from it (one squad, no control); reviewing, and dropping what does not work; the transitions a season contains: selection and deselection, moving up an age group, junior to senior, injury, and leaving the sport; what all of this costs in minutes a week, counted. Deliverable: two one-page formulations (Dani and Milan); a season plan (week by skill, session slot, competition, review point) for one squad; a ten-minute weekly review procedure; then the templates, ordered so that a coach can start in the week they finish the course.

## DD-SP1

Deep dive SP1, The coach's line with minors. Written after the ten core units. Search before writing. Cover: power in the coach-athlete relationship, and why a minor, or any athlete who depends on selection, has limited room to object; psychological, physical and sexual harassment and abuse, and neglect, as the IOC consensus statements define them, with the categories of emotional abuse from the research literature; how common each is, from the prevalence studies, with their samples and limits; the claim that harsh coaching improves performance: what the evidence on controlling and punitive coaching shows for performance and for wellbeing, why the athletes a coach can see are a selected sample, and any published critique of the challenge-and-support model; demanding against demeaning: a test for a single comment (about the behaviour or the person; about something the athlete can change; in private or in front of others; in proportion; sayable in front of a parent; whether the athlete can answer back; whether it is part of a pattern), with the reasoning for each question; what a comment made to one athlete does to the athletes who hear it; humour, nicknames and sarcasm; punishment, exercise as punishment, exclusion and the silent treatment; favouritism and attention made conditional on results; physical contact in coaching and in contact sports (its purpose, saying it first, where others can see); one-to-one situations, travel, changing rooms and lifts; messaging and social media with minors; gifts and special relationships; grooming as safeguarding bodies describe it, at the level of recognition; the duty to act when the concern is another coach, a parent or other athletes (bullying, initiation rites); reporting routes as questions with the bodies to consult, and the International Safeguards for Children in Sport as the frame; why a policy on paper is not enough; repair after the coach gets it wrong. Deliverable: the comment test as a card, with twelve comments a coach might make, drawn from three kinds of sport, each run through the test and rewritten; a personal conduct code (touch, one-to-one, messaging, travel) with blanks for the reader's federation and club rules; a report-or-not decision table; and a script for apologising to an athlete; built on the three case squads, then as templates.

## DD-SP2

Deep dive SP2, Weight, body and who decides. Written after DD-SP1. Search before writing. Cover: the sports where weight or shape bears on the result (weight classes, judged sports, endurance and gravitational sports); relative energy deficiency in sport as the IOC consensus statement defines it, and what it does to growth, bone, mood and performance; eating disorders and disordered eating in athletes, and the risk in adolescence; body image and body dissatisfaction, kept apart from body dysmorphic disorder, a clinical diagnosis defined here from a clinical source; what the research found about coaches' comments on weight and shape; rapid weight loss: how common, how early it starts, and what the consensus positions say about it for minors; growth: what holding a weight class through puberty requires of a growing body; why predicting an adolescent's adult build, or the class that 'suits' them, is unreliable, with the grade of that evidence; the conflict of interest between the class in which an athlete wins and the body the athlete lives in; a class as an identity; who decides (the athlete; with a parent for a minor; with medical input where there is any restriction) and what the coach's part in that decision is; where persuasion becomes pressure and then harassment (repeating it after a no, tying selection or attention to it, commenting in front of others, watching what an athlete eats), mapped to the definitions of DD-SP1; weighing at the club (private, optional, who sees the number); the equivalents in judged sports (appearance, costume, 'lines'); boys, men and muscularity; red flags and referral, pointing to SP9; and current federation rules on weigh-ins and on weight control for minors, as dated examples from at least three sports, each read on the federation's own page. No weight-loss method, calorie figure or target weight is given. Deliverable: a who-decides protocol (a once-a-season review: who is present, the questions asked, what the coach may say, how the decision is recorded and when it is reopened); a language card (never said, said instead); scripts for the weight-class conversation, including the one where the athlete chooses the class in which they will win less; and a club weighing policy; built in full for Dani, then as templates.

## DD-SP3

Deep dive SP3, Tuning out the noise. Written last. Cover: what the noise is, inside (worry, watching oneself, thoughts about the result) and outside (the crowd, the opponent, officials, parents, the coach); why telling oneself not to think of something tends to bring it back, in general and in sport, and the rule that follows, that every cue says what to do; cue words and self-talk in competition, and what is known about cues an athlete writes against cues a coach assigns; talking to oneself by name or as 'you'; reading arousal as readiness; if-then plans for the moments that go wrong; pre-performance routines and short resets after an error; breathing; attention placed outward; accepting the noise without arguing with it, for athletes whom control techniques make worse; music, headphones, phones and social media on the day, only as far as a source supports a claim; the opponent's talk and an official's decision; waiting; what makes a short phrase usable under pressure, graded, with no list of phrases presented as proven; for every technique the sample it was tested on and its grade, saying plainly where the evidence comes from outside sport or from adults; how each is trained (introduced in practice, tested under the pressure of SP6, kept only if it survives), by age band; and how to tell that a technique is making an athlete worse. Deliverable: twelve technique cards (what it is, when to use it, the script, how to train it in four sessions, its grade, the sample it was tested on, when not to use it); a cue-word builder the athlete fills in for themselves; a four-week training plan; and a competition-day pocket card of one page; built in full for Milan, then as templates.
