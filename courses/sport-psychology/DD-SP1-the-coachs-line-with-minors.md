# DD-SP1 · Deep dive: the coach's line with minors

This unit is information, not legal, medical or psychological advice. It teaches recognition and the route to the person who takes over. It teaches no assessment, no diagnosis, no treatment and no investigation of an allegation: when a concern about abuse arises, your part ends when you have passed it to the person your club or federation names, and that person, or the police or child-protection authority, takes over. Where a legal or reporting duty may apply to you, the unit puts it as a question and names the kind of body that can answer it. No law in this unit is stated as yours.

Every technique and recommendation carries a grade in square brackets from SP1's grade key (section 9 of SP1, unchanged: five rules applied in order; "several" means three or more; the labels *direction* and *inference*). Where the only source opened is a consensus statement or a safeguarding body's code whose studies were not opened, the grade is [practice] with the note "the studies behind it were not opened", as in SP2 to SP10. Age bands are SP2's: about 10 to 12, 13 to 15, 16 to 18 and 19 to 25. Where a claim rests on an abstract alone, the citation says "abstract read". Every page of an issuing body (a safeguarding body, a sport integrity body, the IOC's own copy of a consensus statement) carries the date it was read: 19 Nov 2026 for all of them.

The three case athletes return: Dani (fictional), 14, judo, in a club squad of 18 athletes aged 11 to 17; Milan (fictional), 17, football, in an academy squad of 20 players aged 16 to 18; Sara (fictional), 20, 400 metres, in a group of six whose coach also coaches two other groups, which include athletes under 18. They illustrate; they are never evidence. The case register already holds three facts this unit builds on: Dani's coach raises his voice in training and says shouting makes his judoka tougher (SP1); SP3's climate audit counted, in one session each, seven shouts, threats or belittling remarks by Dani's coach, one threat about selection by Milan's coach, and Sara's coach reading out the group's times in rank order three times.

## 1. Power, dependence and the room to object

Every rule in this unit starts from one fact about the relationship you have with an athlete: you hold more power in it than they do, and they need things only you can give.

The IOC's first consensus statement on harassment and abuse in sport defines psychological abuse as "A pattern of deliberate, prolonged, repeated non-contact behaviours within a power differentiated relationship" ([Mountjoy et al., 2016, consensus, Box 1, full text read](https://doi.org/10.1136/bjsports-2016-096121)). The power difference is part of the definition. The same statement says, of sexual harassment and abuse, that they "stem from abuses of power relations", and, of children, that "the concept of consent cannot apply to children" and that young athletes are more vulnerable because of "their need to be cared for, protected and supervised" (Mountjoy et al., 2016). The 2024 IOC consensus statement on interpersonal violence and safeguarding, produced by a 15-member expert panel from a scoping review and a RAND/UCLA appropriateness process with 80% agreement set in advance as consensus, says that "coaches sometimes wield immense power over athletes' careers" and that "athletes may feel pressured to tolerate IV in exchange for the opportunity" to compete and progress ([Tuakli-Wosornu et al., 2024, consensus, pp. 1322, 1324, 1328 to 1329, read on the IOC's copy](https://doi.org/10.1136/bjsports-2024-108766)). It adds that "hierarchical structures and administrative procedures" can make it hard for athletes to report (p. 1329).

What that power consists of, in a squad, is ordinary: you pick the team, set the training, decide who travels, write the reference, talk to the parents, and control the attention and approval that a young athlete reads as a verdict on their worth (SP3, section 5). An athlete who depends on you for selection has a reason not to object to anything you do, whatever their age. A former athlete in an Israeli study of retrospective accounts of coach violence put it in a sentence: "No one wanted to be punished or thrown off the team" (a participant in [Zach et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.3390/bs14060486)). In interviews with 20 former college athletes in the United States (mean age 26.0, nine sports), power and control was the frame through which they explained the behaviour of coaches they described as emotionally abusive ([Adams et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.3389/fspor.2024.1428682)).

Three consequences follow for a coach of 10- to 18-year-olds, and they hold for an adult like Sara (fictional) who depends on your selection or your goodwill:

- **Silence is not agreement.** An athlete who does not object may have no room to. The 2024 consensus found that "None of the reviewed articles focused primarily on promoting IV disclosure or reporting in sport" (Tuakli-Wosornu et al., 2024, p. 1335): how to make it easier for an athlete to speak up has not been studied.
- **A minor cannot consent their way out of your duty.** Whether a 16- or 17-year-old "agreed" to something does not move the line, as the IOC statement says for children (Mountjoy et al., 2016).
- **The line is yours to hold.** Because the athlete cannot reliably push back, the test of what you say and do has to be one you apply to yourself, before you speak. Section 5 gives it.

These three points are the unit's reading of the consensus statements [practice; consensus; the studies behind it were not opened].

## 2. What the consensus statements call harassment, abuse and neglect

Two IOC consensus statements define the forms of harm, eight years apart. The 2016 statement's definitions (Box 1, page 3 of the online-first version) and the 2024 statement's (Table 1, p. 1326) are set side by side below, each quoted under fifteen words or closely stated, with its page.

| Form | IOC 2016 (Mountjoy et al., Box 1) | IOC 2024 (Tuakli-Wosornu et al., Table 1, p. 1326) |
|---|---|---|
| **Umbrella term** | Harassment and abuse, "non-accidental violence" | "the intentional use of physical force or power against other persons" (interpersonal violence) |
| **Psychological** | "A pattern of deliberate, prolonged, repeated non-contact behaviours within a power differentiated relationship"; the box adds that this form is at the core of all other forms | "regular and deliberate use of a range of words and non-physical actions" |
| **Physical** | "Non-accidental trauma or physical injury caused by punching, beating, kicking, biting, burning" or otherwise harming an athlete; it could include forced or mandated inappropriate physical activity (age-inappropriate or physique-inappropriate training loads; training when injured or in pain), forced alcohol consumption and systematic doping | "an act attempting to cause, or resulting in, pain and/or physical injury" |
| **Sexual** | Harassment: "Any unwanted and unwelcome conduct of a sexual nature, whether verbal, non-verbal or physical". Abuse: "Any conduct of a sexual nature, whether non-contact, contact or penetrative", where consent is coerced or manipulated or cannot be given | "Any sexual act, attempt to obtain a sexual act, unwanted sexual comments or advances", among other acts |
| **Neglect** | "failure of parents or care givers to meet a child's physical and emotional needs" or "failure to protect a child from exposure to danger"; "This definition equally applies to coaches and athlete entourages" | A failure to provide a vulnerable athlete with an adequate standard of nutrition and medical care, among the provisions it lists (closely stated from the opening of the definition) |
| **Among peers** | Bullying: "unwanted, repeated and intentional, aggressive behaviour usually among peers", with a real or perceived power imbalance. Hazing: "An organised, usually team-based, form of bullying in sport", the degrading and hazardous initiation of new team members by veteran team members | Names hazing, bullying and grooming as forms of aggression related to interpersonal violence (p. 1326) |

Two features of these definitions decide how a coach uses them.

**Psychological abuse is defined as a pattern.** Both statements use the words "pattern", "repeated" or "regular". One sharp sentence is not, by these definitions, psychological abuse. That is why the comment test in section 5 asks, last, whether a comment is part of a pattern, and why the climate audit (SP3) counts behaviours across a session. A single comment still matters: it is the unit of which a pattern is made, and the athletes who hear it do not know whether it is the first or the fifth.

**Physical and sexual harm need no pattern.** One act of physical abuse or one unwanted sexual comment meets the definitions. So the comment test applies to the ordinary words of coaching; anything sexual, and any physical harm, is outside it and goes straight to the route in section 15.

**Emotional abuse, as athletes have described it.** The 2016 statement notes that some definitions use "emotional" and "psychological" interchangeably, and itself uses "psychological"; it lists the behaviours athletes report: "belittling, humiliating, shouting, scapegoating, rejecting, isolating and threatening behaviours", and "being ignored, or denied attention and support" (Mountjoy et al., 2016, citing athlete reports). The research literature sorts these into categories. The best-known categorisation, by Stirling and Kerr (2008), and an interview study of elite child athletes by Gervis and Dunn (2004), are in the course's reading; neither could be opened at a publisher's page for this unit, so nothing is taken from them. The two qualitative studies opened describe the same ground from athletes' accounts:

- In Adams et al. (2024), 20 former college athletes described hostile remarks, being called "dumb and stupid", yelling for its own sake, being humiliated, neglect of their development and wellbeing, and extra conditioning as punishment. They separated punishment tied to performance, which most accepted, from training that was unrelated to performance, unsafe, cruel or unexplained, which they called abusive. The authors conclude that emotional abuse cannot be identified from the coach's behaviour alone: the athlete's perception and the relationship matter too.
- In Zach et al. (2024), a mixed-methods study of 440 adults (mean age 27.6) who had trained at least a year in a youth department of an Israeli sports union, with 14 interviewed, about 30% reported being yelled at, insulted, humiliated or mocked by a coach, and about half reported excessive criticism, expulsion or suspension, or being ignored or treated with indifference. The interview themes included psychological and verbal violence and "Non-Proportional Punishing".

Both are retrospective accounts from adults, so for athletes under 18 they are the unit's inference. They show what athletes have experienced and named; they do not measure how common it is in any population.

**Where the coach's part ends.** You use these definitions to recognise, and to hold your own line. You do not decide whether what someone else did meets a definition: that is the job of the person your safeguarding route names (section 15) [practice; safeguarding guidance; the studies behind it were not opened].

## 3. How common it is, and what the figures cannot tell you

The prevalence studies ask athletes, usually adults looking back, whether they experienced each form. Their figures differ widely, and the reasons are in their samples and their questions.

- **The range across studies.** The 2024 consensus statement's scoping review gives the ranges of published estimates: psychological violence "from 21% to 79%", physical violence "from 4% to 66%", sexual violence between 0.5% and 78%, neglect "from 27% to 69%", overall interpersonal violence 44% to 86%, hazing 50% and bullying 17% to 61% (Tuakli-Wosornu et al., 2024, pp. 1326 to 1327). The statement warns that "interpreting and comparing current epidemiological estimates requires caution" (p. 1327).
- **Adults recalling childhood sport, the Netherlands and Belgium.** A retrospective online survey of over 4,000 adults prescreened for having taken part in organised sport before 18 found that 38% reported psychological violence in sport as children, 11% physical and 14% sexual; ethnic minority, lesbian, gay and bisexual, and disabled athletes, and those who competed internationally, reported more ([Vertommen et al., 2016, abstract read](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.chiabu.2015.10.006)).
- **Canadian national team athletes.** An anonymous online survey of 995 current and recently retired national team athletes (mean age 27.7; response rate 16%) found that 75% reported at least one experience of harm: neglect 68.8%, psychological 60.2%, sexual 20.5%, physical 14.3%. Coaches were the most frequent perpetrators of neglect, psychological and physical harm, and peers of sexual harm. Female athletes reported more harm of every kind ([Willson et al., 2022, full text read](https://doi.org/10.1177/08862605211045096)).
- **Adolescents now in sport, Canada.** A cross-sectional survey of 983 adolescents aged 14 to 18 in organised sport, a convenience sample from six secondary schools, found that 59.2% reported at least one type of interpersonal violence in sport, 23.8% physical and 10.6% sexual ([Parent et al., 2023, full text read](https://doi.org/10.3390/socsci12060336)). The paper's table gives 54.0% for psychological violence and neglect combined beside a count that does not match it, so that figure is not used here.

Each figure is from a survey: it counts reports, and it cannot say what caused what. The limits matter for how you read them:

- **Low response rates** (16% in the Canadian national team survey) can make a figure too high or too low: athletes with experiences to report may be more or less likely to answer.
- **Retrospective recall** in adults describes sport as it was years ago.
- **Definitions differ** between questionnaires, which is the main reason the ranges are wide.
- **Most samples are adults.** Only the Canadian adolescent survey here sampled athletes aged 14 to 18 while they were in sport; for 10- to 13-year-olds no prevalence study was opened.

What the figures show, on the samples opened that report them: psychological harm and neglect are reported far more often than physical or sexual harm, and coaches are among the most frequent perpetrators of the first two. That is the part of the line a coach is most likely to cross without meaning to, and it is the part this unit gives most space.

## 4. "Harsh coaching builds toughness": the claim regraded a third time

SP1 graded the popular claim [weak, against the claim]; SP3 kept that grade and asked this unit to open the three reviews on controlling coaching by Bartholomew and colleagues (2009, 2010, 2011) and to look for a published critique of the challenge-and-support model.

**The reviews.** None of the three could be opened. The 2009 review's publisher page and the 2010 scale paper's publisher page both refused the request; for the 2011 paper no publisher or DOI address was returned by any search; every other address found was a university repository, which this course does not count as opened. Nothing is taken from them, and they are on the unit's unsure list.

**What was opened, on wellbeing.** The studies SP3 opened stand: in surveys measured once, a controlling coaching style went with need thwarting and, through it, burnout in 103 male footballers aged 12 to 17, and with lower need satisfaction and, through it, fear of failure in 252 athletes aged 10 to 25 and over (SP3, section 2). This unit adds three:

- In 794 current and recently retired Canadian national team athletes over 16 (mean age 27.85), a cross-sectional survey found psychological abuse correlated with lower athlete satisfaction (r = −.32) and with indicators of eating disorders (r = .31) and of self-harm (r = .25). Citing earlier studies, the authors note that athletes and coaches often justify harsh coaching as necessary for results and that some athletes said it made them work harder; they suggest, without testing it, that abuse may be masked by success, and cite earlier studies in which athletes recognised it only after retirement ([Willson et al., 2025, full text read](https://doi.org/10.3389/fspor.2024.1406775)). Performance was not measured.
- In 301 Chinese university student-athletes surveyed three times, three weeks apart, coaches' abusive supervision measured at the first wave was associated with burnout at the third (r = 0.536) ([Li et al., 2025, full text read](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2025.1643356)). Performance was not measured.
- The 2024 consensus statement lists "Diminished sport and academic performance" among the outcomes of interpersonal violence reported in the literature, and notes that causality has not been shown for every outcome in its table (Tuakli-Wosornu et al., 2024, p. 1329). Its panel's view is that "Safe sporting environments and high-performance sporting success can go hand in hand" (p. 1337).

**What was opened, on performance.** No study opened for this unit, and none found by its searches, tests harsh or controlling coaching against a supportive alternative and measures performance. In the qualitative studies, athletes justify it in performance terms ("He wants you to succeed, he pushes you", a participant in Zach et al., 2024), and some said the yelling spurred them on while adding that they would not willingly go through it again (Zach et al., 2024). Those are accounts of belief; they do not measure an effect.

**Why the athletes you can see are a selected sample.** The athletes in front of you are the ones who stayed. Those for whom harshness did not work, or who were harmed by it, are more likely to have left, to have been deselected, or to say nothing while they are still competing. Two of the opened studies point the same way: some athletes recognised harm only after they stopped, as one of Zach et al.'s interviewees said and Willson et al. report from earlier studies (Zach et al., 2024; Willson et al., 2025). So a coach who sees tough, successful athletes under a harsh regime is seeing survivors, and cannot tell from them what the regime did to the others or what the survivors would have done under a different one. This is the unit's reasoning about the sample a coach sees, not a finding [practice].

**The challenge-and-support model.** The model in the course's reading holds that performance thrives where challenge and support are both high; the abstract and notes opened do not state it in those words. The practitioner article it was taken from was opened again at the publisher's page, in abstract and notes only, as in SP3: its notes warn that "extreme challenge-no support environments" have been created in the name of toughening performers up, and set their approach apart from "the unethical and inappropriate imposition of adversity"; the page reports no test of the programme's effects ([Fletcher & Sarkar, 2016, abstract and notes read](https://doi.org/10.1080/21520704.2016.1255496)). The searches made for this unit (for critiques of the challenge-and-support model, of challenge-support matrices in resilience training, and of mental toughness as a cover for abuse) found no published critique of the model that could be opened at a publisher's page; two candidate chapters and articles were found only on repository or index records and are not used. So: no published critique opened. The model's own authors set harshness without support outside it.

**Grade.** For the claim that harsh coaching improves performance or builds toughness: no peer-reviewed source found. Against it: [weak, against the claim; rule 5: correlational studies in athletes, mostly adults or older adolescents, no meta-analysis of controlling coaching opened, no controlled test; for athletes under 18 partly inference]. Do not use harshness as a method [weak, against]. The grade is unchanged from SP1 and SP3; what has changed is that it now rests on five correlational studies in athletes instead of three (SP3's three, two of them named above, and the two surveys this section adds), and on a consensus statement that names diminished performance among the reported outcomes.

## 5. Demanding against demeaning: a test for a single comment

Coaching is demanding. You set standards an athlete has not yet reached, tell them they have not reached them, and ask for more. None of that is harm. The line runs through how you say it, to whom, about what, and how often. No study opened for this unit tests any rule for a single comment, so the test below is the unit's own, built from the definitions in section 2 and the athletes' accounts in sections 2 and 4 [practice; no study opened tests it; consensus definitions, the studies behind them not opened].

Ask seven questions of anything you are about to say to an athlete, or have just said:

1. **Is it about the behaviour, or about the person?** "Your hands dropped on the last two" is about a behaviour. "You're lazy" is about the person. Reason: the behaviours athletes name as abuse are aimed at the person: belittling, humiliating, scapegoating, rejecting (Mountjoy et al., 2016). A comment about a behaviour tells the athlete what to change; a comment about the person tells them what they are.
2. **Is it about something the athlete can change?** Technique, effort, choices and preparation can change. Height, the timing of puberty, body shape, background and talent cannot be changed by trying (SP2 for maturation; DD-SP2 for weight and body). Reason: a comment on something fixed has no corrective use, so all that is left of it is the judgement.
3. **Is it said in private, or in front of others?** Correct the drill in front of the group when the whole group can learn from it; take anything about one athlete's attitude, worth or selection aside (SP3, section 5). Reason: humiliation needs an audience, and the 2016 statement names a "pervasive bystander effect" that "prevents disclosure and seeking of help" around abusive coaching (Mountjoy et al., 2016). Section 6 deals with the audience.
4. **Is it in proportion?** To the mistake, the age and the moment. A raised voice to be heard across a pool is in proportion; a raised voice an inch from a 12-year-old's face is not. Reason: athletes in the Israeli study named "Non-Proportional Punishing" as a form of the violence they experienced (Zach et al., 2024), and the former college athletes separated proportionate, performance-tied demands from arbitrary ones (Adams et al., 2024).
5. **Could you say it, in the same words and tone, in front of the athlete's parent?** For an adult athlete, in front of the welfare officer or a colleague you respect. Reason: it is a proxy for transparency. Safeguarding codes build the same idea into their rules: contact that is "observable and interruptible" ([U.S. Center for SafeSport, *Minor Athlete Abuse Prevention Policies*, effective 1 Jan 2025, Part III, read 19 Nov 2026](https://uscenterforsafesport.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/01/2025-MAAPP.pdf)); contact that never takes place "out of sight of others or in secret" ([NSPCC Sport, *Physical contact and young people in sport*, updated 7 Aug 2025, read 19 Nov 2026](https://sport.nspcc.org.uk/resource-library/best-practice/physical-contact-and-young-people-in-sport/)). A comment you would change in front of a parent is one you already know is wrong.
6. **Can the athlete answer back?** Could they say "I don't think that's fair" without paying for it in selection, attention or the next drill? Reason: the definitions put the power difference inside psychological abuse (Mountjoy et al., 2016), and athletes "may feel pressured to tolerate" what they would object to (Tuakli-Wosornu et al., 2024, pp. 1328 to 1329). A comment the athlete cannot answer is one they have to absorb.
7. **Is it part of a pattern?** Yours, this week, to this athlete or about this kind of thing. Reason: psychological abuse is defined as a pattern of repeated or regular behaviour (section 2). One comment that fails a question is a mistake to repair (section 17). The same failure repeated is moving towards the definition.

**How to use the answers.** Asked as the card in Deliverable A words them, any "no" to questions 1 to 6 means rewrite before you say it; a "no" to question 7 (it is part of a pattern) means repair and, for anyone's pattern, the welfare officer. A comment already said that failed is repaired (section 17). A pattern of failures, yours or anyone's, is a concern for the welfare officer (section 15). Anything sexual, any comment on an athlete's body or weight, and any physical harm are outside the test: they are not rewritten, they are not said or done, and a concern about them goes by the route in section 15 (DD-SP2 takes weight and body) [practice; the unit's own].

The test is for your own words first. It is also how DD-SP2 will run comments about weight and body, and how the deliverable's season-plan rows check the wording of every pressure drill (deliverable E). It does not tell you whether someone else's behaviour is abuse; it tells you whether a concern is worth passing on, and the answer to that is usually yes (section 14).

## 6. What a comment to one athlete does to the athletes who hear it

A comment made to one athlete in front of the squad is heard by everyone in it. No study opened for this unit measured, in a training group, what a coach's comment to one athlete does to the athletes who witness it. What the opened sources do say:

- The 2016 statement describes psychologically abusive coaching with a "pervasive bystander effect" that prevents disclosure, and lists "passive attitudes/non-intervention, denial or silence" among the mechanisms that let harassment and abuse continue (Mountjoy et al., 2016). The 2024 statement names "passive inactions such as bystanding and enabling" as part of interpersonal violence (Tuakli-Wosornu et al., 2024, p. 1327) and lists lack of bystander intervention among the factors that let harm continue (Table 4, p. 1330).
- In interviews with 11 people aged 17 to 27 who had been bullied in youth sport between the ages of 10 and 16 (basketball, football, swimming, athletics), teammates who saw it often did not step in, and some sided with the aggressor or "took it as a joke"; one participant linked this to bystanders' fear of being harmed if they helped. Coaches' responses, where there were any, rarely ended it ([Ríos et al., 2022, full text read](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2022.819981)). That study is about peers, not coaches.
- In the Israeli interviews, athletes described a coach's anger that the whole squad saw ("Everyone knows it") and kept away from (Zach et al., 2024).

What follows [practice; the unit's reading of these sources; no study opened measures the effect on witnesses]: a public comment that fails the test teaches the squad what you allow, tells each of them what might be said to them, and makes it less likely that any of them will tell you, or anyone, when something is wrong. A public correction that passes the test teaches the drill. A volleyball coach who tells a setter, in front of the squad, that she always panics on match point has told eleven other players what may be said about them; the same coach saying "Setters, on match point, look at the middle first" has taught all twelve something. (These illustrations are the unit's, not data.) SP3's ego-involving climate, in which comparison and attention to the best athletes went with more fear of failure in 16- and 17-year-olds, is the same mechanism at the scale of a squad (SP3, section 2).

## 7. Humour, nicknames and sarcasm

Humour holds squads together, and it is also where many coaches cross the line without noticing. No study of coaches' humour with young athletes was opened for this unit (the one study found, of banter in professional football, could not be opened at the publisher's page). So the comment test carries it [practice; no study opened tests it]:

- **A joke at an athlete's expense in front of the squad** fails questions 1, 3 and 6 at once: it is about the person, it has an audience, and the athlete cannot answer back without seeming unable to take a joke. The bullied athletes' teammates "took it as a joke" (Ríos et al., 2022); the athlete it was aimed at did not.
- **Sarcasm** says the opposite of what it means and leaves the athlete to work out the judgement. SP7's state-response table already lists it among the responses to avoid with an overconfident athlete (SP7, Deliverable B). With 10- to 12-year-olds in particular, a literal reading is likely, which is SP2's development note applied here as the unit's inference.
- **Nicknames.** A nickname the athlete chose, or plainly likes, is theirs. A nickname about the body, about a mistake, about where someone comes from or about how they speak fails question 2 and usually question 1. Ask the athlete, in private, what they want to be called, and use that. In a new squad, use names until they tell you otherwise. A hockey squad that calls its tallest 13-year-old by a name about his height is using a nickname he did not choose, about something he cannot change, in front of everyone (the unit's illustration).
- **Self-deprecating humour** from you, about your own mistakes, carries none of these risks and shows the squad that a mistake is survivable (SP7, section 11).

## 8. Punishment, exercise as punishment, exclusion, and the line between a sporting consequence and a punishment

**The definitions.** The 2016 statement includes forced or mandated inappropriate physical activity within physical abuse (Mountjoy et al., 2016, Box 1, closely stated). One safeguarding body states, for its own jurisdiction, that any physical punishment of children in sport is unlawful and counts as physical abuse (NSPCC Sport, *Physical contact and young people in sport*, 2025); that is the issuing body's statement, made without naming the jurisdiction it covers, and not a statement of the law where you coach. Whether physical punishment, or exercise as punishment, is permitted under your federation's code, and under the law where you coach: ask your federation's safeguarding lead.

**What athletes report.** In the Israeli study, about 28% reported that a coach had forced them, or asked them, to do extra high-intensity training until they were physically exhausted; one interviewee was made to skip for about two and a half hours after walking instead of running (Zach et al., 2024). In Adams et al. (2024), extra conditioning used as punishment included bear crawls "until somebody would throw up" and sprints as payback for a lost first half. In a qualitative study of 332 written reflections by students at one private high school in the United States, collected between 2019 and 2023, students described running as punishment as common, and some said it made them avoid taking risks and soured their relationship with exercise ([McCleery et al., 2025, full text read](https://doi.org/10.3390/youth5010012)); the authors say the findings do not generalise. These are accounts: none measures an outcome against a comparison group. SP3's rule stands: do not use exercise as punishment [practice; no study opened tests it; consensus definition includes forced inappropriate physical activity; the qualitative accounts are from adults and from high-school students, so for 10- to 13-year-olds inference].

**Exclusion and the silent treatment.** Leaving an athlete out of a drill, sending them to sit out, refusing to speak to them, or going cold after a poor performance: the 2016 statement lists "being ignored, or denied attention and support" among the behaviours athletes report as abuse, and about half the Israeli respondents reported being ignored or treated with indifference, among other items (Mountjoy et al., 2016; Zach et al., 2024). SP3's controlling-behaviour scale calls withholding attention and warmth negative conditional support and found it, with the other controlling behaviours, associated with need thwarting and burnout in 12- to 17-year-old boys (SP3, section 2). Do not use exclusion or silence as a sanction [practice; exclusion and silence as such untested; the association of controlling behaviour with need thwarting and burnout is SP3's, graded weak there]. Removing an athlete from a drill for safety, or because they asked, is not a sanction; say why, and bring them back.

**The line between a sporting consequence and a punishment (F78, F79, F82).** SP6 builds pressure into training with consequences, and set eight rules for doing it with minors (SP6, section 4). Its sources used consequences that those rules forbid. This unit draws the line between the two and tests the published consequences against it.

A consequence in a pressure drill is **sporting** when all six of these hold [practice; the unit's own; built from SP6's rules and the definitions in section 2]:

1. **The competition itself could bring it.** A point, possession, a starting position, going first or last, a re-start a goal down, the choice of the next drill. Competition never makes an athlete sing, run laps or lose their place for missing one drill.
2. **It falls on the drill, never on the athlete's body, worth or standing.** No physical cost beyond the load already planned; no public list of who failed; no loss of selection, playing time, squad place or warmth.
3. **It is known before the drill starts.** Nothing is sprung or decided after the result.
4. **It ends with the drill.** It is not carried into the next session, the changing room or the selection meeting.
5. **It falls on whoever missed, inside the game.** A team may lose a point in a team game; no individual forfeit falls on teammates.
6. **It passes the comment test in the words used to announce it and the words used after a miss** (section 5).

Anything that fails one of these is a **punishment**: something unpleasant added to an athlete because of a result. With minors, punishment is off the ladder at every level.

Tested against this line, as SP6 reports the sources (this unit could not open the two youth studies or the practitioner article at a publisher's page, and takes their content from SP6, section 3, which read them on repository copies; the rows are SP6's report, not this unit's finding, and carry no claim beyond it):

| Consequence in the source | Source, as SP6 reports it | Fails | Sporting alternative |
|---|---|---|---|
| Singing in front of teammates | Practitioner article (Low et al., 2024) | 2 (worth), comment test question 3 | Lose the choice of the next drill |
| Deselection from the next competition | Practitioner article | 2 (standing), 4 | Lose the point in this drill |
| Teammates doing sprints or push-ups | Practitioner article | 2 (body), 5 | The team loses a point in the game |

**Did any study measure harm?** SP6 found that neither youth study measured harm (F79, F82). The searches made for this unit, for studies of harm, wellbeing, distress or burnout from pressure training in athletes under 18, found none. A meta-analysis of 24 controlled trials of psychological interventions for anxiety in athletes aged 14 to 23 included one pressure-training study with footballers of about 15 to 16, and extracted only anxiety scores and performance; no harm outcome was reported for any intervention ([Li, Yang & Wang, 2025, full text read](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2025.1621635)). A narrative review of pressure training in music hypothesises that it can protect wellbeing and warns that it "must not be used as an excuse" to expose performers to any stressor, without measuring either ([de Bie et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2024.1501014)). So SP6's ladder, caps and step-down rule remain practice, untested for harm or for safety, and this unit's line is practice too. The cost SP6 named stands: milder pressure than the published forfeits produce. The benefit claimed for the line is that nothing in a pressure drill meets the consensus definitions of psychological or physical abuse; that is a judgement from the definitions, not a tested outcome.

## 9. Favouritism, and attention made conditional on results

You cannot give every athlete equal time on every day, and some days the athlete with a competition tomorrow gets most of it. Favouritism is different: attention, warmth, privileges or exemptions that follow an athlete's results or your liking for them, and that the squad can see.

- SP3's ego-involving climate, with the coach giving most attention to the best athletes, went with more fear of failure in 16- and 17-year-old handball players (an association, SP3, section 2), and its climate self-audit counts it (C7).
- Attention given after a good result and withdrawn after a bad one is negative conditional support, one of the four controlling behaviours (SP3, section 2).
- Special treatment also appears in the record of how abusers in sport operate: in a study of 120 cases of male coaches convicted of or reported for sexual offences against athletes in Canada from 1967 to 2020, coded from court judgments and media reports, making the athlete feel "like the chosen one" appeared in 21.7% of cases and gifts or special permissions unrelated to sport in 30.0% ([St-Pierre et al., 2022, full text read](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2022.856798)). Favouritism is not grooming, and most favouritism has nothing to do with abuse. The point is narrower: a squad in which special treatment is normal is one in which special treatment used for grooming is harder to see (section 13).

What to do [practice; SP3's climate rules; no study opened tests these steps]: count who you speak to in each session (SP3, S9); keep warmth constant after wins and losses; make any privilege (an extra session, a lift, a role, the end lane in a swimming squad) available by a rule the squad knows; and when one athlete needs more of your time for a reason, say so to the squad in general terms.

## 10. Physical contact in coaching and in contact sports

Physical contact is part of coaching many sports: spotting a gymnast, adjusting a rower's hands on the oar, demonstrating a hold in judo, treating an injury, celebrating a win. The question is how, never whether. No study opened tests any rule for contact, so the rules below come from safeguarding codes, each dated on the issuing body's page and none stated as yours [practice; safeguarding codes; the studies behind them were not opened].

- **Purpose.** NSPCC Sport, the UK's sport safeguarding unit, says contact should be "for the benefit of the child, rather than the adult involved", and lists legitimate reasons: treating an injury, developing a skill, preventing an accident, meeting the requirements of the sport, and comforting or celebrating with a child, while watching for repeated contact (NSPCC Sport, *Physical contact and young people in sport*, updated 7 Aug 2025).
- **Saying it first.** Outside emergencies, the same page says to "ask the child before making contact" and to "explain to the child the reason for this physical contact", and to invite the child to say whether it is comfortable.
- **Where others can see.** Contact must never take place "out of sight of others or in secret", and never involve the genital area, buttocks, breasts or any area likely to cause distress or embarrassment. Casual touches to the shoulder or arm can be misread and are best avoided.
- **In a contact sport.** The 2016 IOC statement notes that "Close proximity, frequent group interactions and rough-and-tumble handling are used by perpetrators" as a pretext (Mountjoy et al., 2016). In a sport like judo, wrestling or rugby, contact is the sport, which is why the rules above matter more there: demonstrate on a willing partner of similar age rather than by holding a minor yourself, where the technique allows it; when you must demonstrate on a minor, say what you will do, do it in the open, and stop if they say stop.
- **Treatment and massage.** The U.S. Center for SafeSport's policies, as one example, require another adult to be present during manual therapy of a minor, the minor to be clothed and the intimate areas covered, each step narrated with the athlete's assent, and recommend that coaches do not massage minor athletes (U.S. Center for SafeSport, MAAPP 2025, Part III, Manual Therapy). Leave treatment to the physiotherapist or doctor.

What contact does your federation's code permit in your sport, and what does your club's code add? Ask your club's welfare or safeguarding officer and your federation's safeguarding lead, and write the answers into the conduct code (deliverable B).

## 11. One-to-one situations, travel, changing rooms and lifts

Most coaching happens in groups. The moments of risk, for the athlete and for you, are the ones when it does not: a talk after training, an individual session, a lift home, an overnight trip. The codes opened agree on the principle that contact between an adult and a minor should be visible to others and open to interruption; they differ in detail [practice; safeguarding codes; the studies behind them were not opened]. Two codes as examples, each as its issuing body states it on the date read, neither stated as yours:

- **U.S. Center for SafeSport, *Minor Athlete Abuse Prevention Policies*, effective 1 Jan 2025.** One-on-one interactions and meetings with minor athletes "must be observable and interruptible", except in emergencies or with a documented exception; individual training sessions need written consent from a parent or guardian at least once a year, and parents must be allowed to watch. One-on-one transport needs advance written consent, and another adult or at least two other minors present unless an exception applies. Adults "cannot share a hotel room or otherwise sleep in the same room" with a minor athlete, outside listed exceptions; room checks need two adults. No device may be used to photograph or record in a locker room (Part III).
- **NSPCC Sport, *Safe use of changing facilities for children*, updated 6 Aug 2026.** Where adults and children must share a changing facility, at least two members of staff supervise, of the same sex as the children. SP9 dated the same body's page on away day trips (updated 24 Nov 2022) and the U.S. code's travel rule; the same body's *Overnight trips and international travel* page (updated 28 June 2022, read 19 Nov 2026) lists accommodation and emergency procedures among its topics and gives its guidance as a downloadable PDF, which was not opened; this unit adds nothing to SP9's ratio question (SP9, section 10).

What these mean in practice for you, as a coach of a squad with minors in it [practice; the codes above; the unit's own wording]:

- **Talks after training:** in view, door open, other adults nearby; SP9's first conversation already sits somewhere "private enough to talk and open enough to be seen" (SP9, section 6).
- **Individual sessions:** with the parents' knowledge and their written agreement where your code asks for it, in a place others use: a tennis lesson on a court beside other courts in use, not in an empty hall.
- **Lifts:** not alone with one minor, unless the parents have agreed in writing and your code allows it; pick up and drop off in an order that leaves you alone with nobody.
- **Changing rooms:** you do not change or shower with minors; supervision as your code sets it; no phones or cameras in use.
- **Overnight:** never in an athlete's room alone; room checks in pairs; the ratio, the rooms and the emergency procedure as your club's travel policy sets them (SP9, section 10).

## 12. Messaging and social media with minors

A message is a one-to-one conversation with no one else in the room. The codes treat it that way [practice; safeguarding codes; the studies behind them were not opened]:

- NSPCC Sport says online contact with young people should be "carried out on public pages/groups or via their parents and carers", that staff should not send private messages to a young person, and that an organisation's messaging should come from a business account managed and monitored by trained staff, with at least two staff having access ([NSPCC Sport, *Online safety in sport*, updated 14 Feb 2025, read 19 Nov 2026](https://sport.nspcc.org.uk/help-advice/topics/online-safety/)).
- The U.S. code requires one-on-one electronic communication with a minor athlete to be "Open and Transparent", which it defines as copying or including a parent or guardian, another adult family member or another adult participant, recommends sending messages only between 8:00 a.m. and 8:00 p.m. local time, and recommends against private social media connections with minor athletes (U.S. Center for SafeSport, MAAPP 2025, Part III, Electronic Communications).

In practice [practice; the codes above]: squad messages go to a group the parents are in, or to the parents; nothing to a minor alone (a gymnastics squad's training changes go to the parents' group, not to each gymnast's phone); nothing late at night; no private social-media connections with minors; nothing you would not want read aloud at the club. For an adult athlete like Sara, messages are between adults, and the comment test still applies to every word; her coach's other groups include athletes under 18, and those groups follow the rules for minors (case register, SP9).

## 13. Gifts, special relationships and grooming, at the level of recognition

This section is for recognition only. It does not teach you to investigate or to decide whether grooming is happening; a concern goes to the welfare officer (section 15).

**What grooming is, as the safeguarding bodies describe it.** The 2016 IOC statement says that "Sexual abuse is usually preceded by a 'grooming' process" in which the perpetrator prepares and desensitises the athlete, and that "psychologically abusive coaching practices can facilitate and mask sexual abuse grooming behaviours" (Mountjoy et al., 2016). NSPCC Sport describes grooming as the entrapment and preparation of a child, the adults around them and the setting, so that abuse can happen and stay hidden ([NSPCC Sport, *Protecting children from grooming and sexual abuse*, updated 16 June 2025, read 19 Nov 2026](https://sport.nspcc.org.uk/resource-library/best-practice/protecting-children-in-sport-from-grooming-and-sexual-abuse/)).

**What it can look like in sport.** Signs and tactics the NSPCC page describes: "unexplained gifts"; a child secretive about how they spend their time; social media and messaging used to manipulate a child; small rules broken to see whether anyone challenges it ("breaking small rules to see if they're challenged"); trust built with the whole family and with volunteers; an adult who makes themselves indispensable to the club. In the Canadian case study, the strategies coded most often included befriending the athlete and spending much time together (69.2% of cases), befriending the parents (38.3%), gifts or special permissions (30.0%), taking the athlete to an isolated place (36.2%), inviting them to the coach's home (35.3%) and spending a night together (30.2%); in 25% of cases a sport organisation knew of the abuse and did not report it (St-Pierre et al., 2022). That study coded detected cases from court judgments and media reports, which its authors call "the tip of the iceberg", and is descriptive: it shows what happened in those cases, not how often any behaviour leads to abuse.

**What follows for you** [practice; safeguarding guidance; the studies behind it were not opened]:

- **Gifts.** No personal gifts to a minor; anything given goes to the whole squad by a rule they know. The U.S. code recommends against personal gifts and allows gifts distributed equally to all athletes (MAAPP 2025, Part IV).
- **Special relationships.** No "special" athlete with private time, private messages, private jokes, lifts or privileges the rest do not have.
- **Your own boundaries are the protection for you as well.** A coach who keeps every rule in sections 10 to 12 cannot be mistaken for one who does not.
- **If you notice these signs in someone else's behaviour**, you do not decide what they mean. You pass on what you saw, in behaviour words, with dates (section 15).

**Relationships with older athletes.** Whether a sexual relationship between a coach and an athlete of 16 or 17, or an adult athlete they coach, is prohibited by law or by your federation's code differs between countries and codes. One example, as the issuing body states it for its own jurisdiction: NSPCC Sport says that since 28 June 2022 the law has been extended so that it is an offence for an adult in a position of trust in sport to engage in sexual activity with a 16- or 17-year-old in their care, whatever the young person says about consent ([NSPCC Sport, *Preventing abuse in positions of trust in sport and activity*, updated 2 Jan 2024, read 19 Nov 2026](https://sport.nspcc.org.uk/resource-library/best-practice/abuse-of-positions-of-trust-within-sport/)). Whatever the law where you coach, the rule this course keeps is: no sexual or romantic relationship with an athlete under 18 you coach, since the IOC statement says that "the concept of consent cannot apply to children" (Mountjoy et al., 2016) [practice; consensus; the studies behind it were not opened]. What does the law where you coach say, and what does your federation's code say about relationships with athletes of any age? Ask your federation's safeguarding lead and, for the law, a lawyer or the child-protection authority.

## 14. When the concern is someone else: another coach, a parent, other athletes

This unit's aim includes acting when someone else crosses the line. Acting means passing on what you saw, by the route; it never means investigating, confronting the person, or asking the child to prove anything [practice; safeguarding guidance; the studies behind it were not opened].

- **Another coach.** A colleague who shouts and belittles, uses exercise as punishment, keeps a minor behind alone, or messages one privately. If harm is happening in front of you in a session (shouting, belittling, punishment), you may stop the activity if it is safe ("Let's take a break"), without accusing anyone. About a minor kept behind alone or messaged privately, say nothing to the colleague. In every case write down what you saw and pass it to the welfare officer the same day. NSPCC Sport says that you do not need "to decide whether abuse has taken place" before reporting a concern ([NSPCC Sport, *Deal with a concern*, undated, read 19 Nov 2026](https://sport.nspcc.org.uk/help-advice/deal-with-a-concern/)).
- **A parent.** A parent who shouts at their child after a loss, or treats them in a way that worries you: SP3, section 9 and SP10, section 5 deal with parents' behaviour at competitions as part of the climate; a concern about how a parent treats a child goes to the welfare officer, not to the parent and not back to the child (SP3; SP9, Deliverable A).
- **Other athletes: bullying and initiation rites.** The 2016 statement defines hazing as bullying, an organised degrading initiation (section 2). The 2024 statement reports a hazing estimate of 50% and bullying estimates of 17% to 61% across the studies it reviewed (Tuakli-Wosornu et al., 2024, p. 1327). An initiation rite in your squad is your responsibility while the athletes are in your care: say before the season, in plain words, that there are none; stop one when you see it; and pass on what happened. Bullying between athletes that you see or are told about is handled the same way; in the bullied athletes' accounts, a coach telling the victim to "work harder" did nothing to stop it (Ríos et al., 2022).
- **When an athlete tells you.** Listen without leading questions, promise no secrecy, write down their words the same day, and pass it on by the route; do not investigate and do not confront anyone (SP9, section 6 and Deliverable A, red-flag table, unchanged). Never ask the child to report it themselves or to tell their parents; the burden of telling is yours (SP9, section 6).

## 15. The route: questions to ask before you need it, and the International Safeguards as the frame

**The frame.** The *International Safeguards for Children in Sport* (2014) are the course's frame for what an organisation should have in place. The document itself could not be opened for this unit. A coach's part sits in four of them: you follow the procedures (2), you reduce risks in your own sessions (4), you keep the guidelines for behaviour (5), and you take the training and pass on what you know (6). **How the national bodies' routes look, dated.** Four examples, each as its issuing body states it on its own page, read 19 Nov 2026, none stated as yours; they show that routes differ and change:

## 16. Why a policy on paper is not enough

A club can have a safeguarding policy in a folder and still be unsafe. The evidence opened points three ways:

- **Athletes do not know the policies exist.** The 2024 consensus statement found that "awareness that these policies exist and are safe to access is low among athletes", that "safeguarding infrastructures in youth sport have limited influence and are frequently under-resourced", and that "Articles proposing safeguarding strategies, while promising, had not been empirically assessed" (Tuakli-Wosornu et al., 2024, pp. 1334 to 1335).
- **Progress is self-reported.** In the survey of 50 organisations working towards the International Safeguards (from about 400 contacted), of the 40 that completed the self-audit, between 42.5% and 72.5% rated each safeguard fully in place, under half for two of the eight (Table 6); the data were self-reported, attrition was substantial, and some organisations reported no policy yet or weak recording (Wilson & Rhind, 2022). What happens in sessions was not observed.
- **Organisations that knew did not act.** In a quarter of the Canadian coach cases, a sport organisation knew of the abuse and did not report it (St-Pierre et al., 2022).

The 2016 statement put the requirement as "culturally specific policy and procedures, with implementation", with monitoring and evaluation (Mountjoy et al., 2016). For a coach, implementation is what you do in your own sessions [practice; consensus; the studies behind it were not opened]: tell your athletes, in words their age can use, who they can talk to if something is wrong, and repeat it each season; follow the conduct code where you can be seen following it; check the route's contacts each term (SP10); and run the climate audit with the line in it (deliverable E).

## 17. Repair after you get it wrong

You will fail the comment test sometimes: shout at a 13-year-old in front of the squad, make a joke that lands on the wrong athlete, use selection as a threat in a bad week. What then?

**The evidence.** Repair after a coach's harmful behaviour has almost no evidence. SP7 found that only 6 of 80 papers in a review of conflict among athletes and coaches studied interpersonal conflict directly, and that none of the three studies it opened tests a way of repairing (SP7, section 11). This unit's searches found no study that tests a coach's apology to an athlete. SP7's five repair steps are practice, and are not cited here as tested (F101).

**The steps** [practice; SP7, section 11; no study opened tests repair with athletes]:

1. **Name what you did**, in behaviour words, without softening it.
2. **Say it was wrong**, without an explanation that excuses it.
3. **Say what you will do differently**, and do it: the change is what the athlete will judge.
4. **Ask how it was for them**, and listen. They may not accept it at once, and do not have to.
5. **Do it soon, in private, and once**, in a place others can see (section 11). With a minor, ask the welfare officer whether the parents should be told. If the comment was public, correct it in public too, in one sentence, without making the athlete the centre of it again.

**What an apology must not do.** It must not ask the athlete to reassure you, to forgive you on the spot, or to keep it between you: that last request would put a secret between an adult and a minor, which is the pattern of section 13. If what you did meets one of the definitions in section 2, or is part of a pattern, an apology does not close it: tell the welfare officer yourself.

### D. Apology scripts

Three scripts, one for each case coach, each for a behaviour in the case register. The words are *added*. The steps are SP7's, untested [practice; SP7, section 11; F101].

**D1. Dani's coach, after shouting at Dani in front of the squad** (register: he raises his voice; SP3's audit counted seven shouts, threats or belittling remarks; SP8: he is working on normal volume). The shout is comment 1 in A. After the session, at the edge of the mat while the squad stretches:

> "Dani, can I have a word? In the second round today I shouted at you across the mat and said you were fighting like a beginner. That was wrong. You aren't, and shouting at you in front of everyone doesn't help you get the sleeve. From now on I'll tell you one thing after the round, at your side, at normal volume. How was that for you?"

Then he listens, and does not ask her to say it was fine. Because she is 14, he asks the club's welfare officer whether her parents should be told; he does not ask Dani to tell them. Because he shouted in front of the squad, at the start of the next session he says to the squad: "Last time I shouted at someone across the mat. I shouldn't have. Instructions from me come at normal volume." He does not name Dani.

**D2. Milan's coach, after using selection as a threat** (register: one threat about selection in SP3's audit). The threat is comment 5 in A. At the start of the next session, to the squad, because it was said to the squad:

> "Last week I said I'd remember who missed the standard when I picked the team. That was wrong, and it isn't how selection works. Nothing in a training drill counts for selection. The criteria are the ones you were given (SP8). If anyone wants to talk about that, find me after."

And to Milan, who asks before most matches whether he is starting (register), in private at Monday's session: "When I said that about selection, how did it land for you?" The coach listens. Milan is 17: the coach asks the academy's welfare officer whether his parents should be told.

**D3. Sara's coach, after reading out times in rank order** (register: three times in one session, SP3's audit). Sara is 20, so this is between adults; the rest of the group heard it too. To the group at the start of the next session:

> "I've been reading out your times fastest to slowest. That turns training into a ranking, and it isn't what the sessions are for. From now on you'll each get your own time against your own record, on your card."

To Sara, in private, by the steps: "When I read the times out in order, you were at the bottom of the list in front of everyone. I shouldn't have done that. What would be useful to you from me after a trial?" He listens. He does not connect it to her referral or her sleep, which stay with the student health service as her plan says (SP9).
