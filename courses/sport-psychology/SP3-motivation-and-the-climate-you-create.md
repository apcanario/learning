Every technique and recommendation carries a grade in square brackets from SP1's grade key (section 9 of SP1: five rules applied in order; "several" means three or more; the labels *direction* and *inference*). Where the only source opened is a consensus statement whose studies were not opened, the grade is [practice] with the note "the studies behind it were not opened". Age bands are SP2's: about 10 to 12, 13 to 15, 16 to 18 and 19 to 25.

The three case athletes return: Dani (fictional), 14, judo; Milan (fictional), 17, football; Sara (fictional), 20, 400 metres. They illustrate; they are never evidence.

One warning applies to almost every section. Most of the research on motivational climate asks athletes to fill in questionnaires about their coach and about themselves, once. That design shows which things go together. It cannot show that the coach's behaviour caused the athlete's state; an anxious athlete may also perceive the coach as harsher. ## 1. Two theories, and what their evidence in youth sport is made of

**Self-determination theory.** The theory holds that people have three basic psychological needs: autonomy (acting by one's own choice), competence (feeling effective) and relatedness (feeling connected to others). A coach can support a need or thwart it. Supporting is more than the absence of thwarting: a coach can give choices in one drill and belittle in the next. The theory also sorts motivation by how much it is one's own, from autonomous (doing it because it matters to you or you enjoy it) through introjected (to avoid guilt or to feel worthy) and external (for a reward or to avoid a punishment) to amotivation (no reason at all). This summary of the theory is the unit's own and carries no citation; the studies below measure parts of it: the four kinds of motivation in the meta-analysis that follows, and need thwarting, which the authors of the footballers' study in section 2 describe as stronger than the mere absence of need satisfaction ([Morales-Sánchez et al., 2020](https://doi.org/10.3390/ijerph17134909)).

The largest body of evidence opened for this unit is a meta-analysis of autonomy support in sport and exercise: 1,320 correlations from 131 independent samples, 38,844 participants. Perceived autonomy support was associated most strongly with autonomous motivation (ρ = .39), weakly with introjected regulation (ρ = .16), not at all with external regulation (ρ = −.01), and negatively with amotivation (ρ = −.19). The authors report strong positive associations with well-being and negative associations with distress, and associations with need satisfaction; culture and type of sport did not moderate the results ([Mossman et al., 2024, abstract read](https://doi.org/10.1080/1750984X.2022.2031252)). These are correlations. The abstract does not give the ages of the samples or how many were athletes and how many exercisers. [moderate, positive, for the association between autonomy support and autonomous motivation: one meta-analysis read in abstract, its sensitivity analyses and study quality not seen, rule 4, on the reading that its samples are mainly athletes, which the abstract implies (it reports athlete outcomes and no moderation by type of sport) but does not state; if they are mainly exercisers, rule 5 makes it weak; no causal claim.]

**Achievement goal theory.** The theory distinguishes two ways an athlete can judge whether they did well: against their own effort and improvement (task involvement) or against other people (ego involvement). A coach's climate leans one way or the other. One questionnaire, the Perceived Motivational Climate in Sport Questionnaire (PMCSQ-2), used in a study of teen handball players, asks athletes to rate items such as "the coach emphasizes always trying to do your best" for a task-involving climate, and "the coach gives most of his or her attention to the stars" for an ego-involving one ([Gómez-López et al., 2020](https://doi.org/10.3390/ijerph17020592)).

In that study, 479 handball players aged 16 and 17 (250 boys, 229 girls), selected for their regions' teams in Spanish championships, answered once, during the championship. In the correlation table at the publisher's page, an ego-involving climate correlated with more fear of failure (r = 0.43) and a task-involving climate with less (r = −0.31); in the authors' model the ego-involving climate's link ran through fear of failure to more somatic and cognitive anxiety, and the task-involving climate's direct path to fear of failure was not significant ([Gómez-López et al., 2020](https://doi.org/10.3390/ijerph17020592)). One survey of one team sport, a correlation. [weak, for the association between an ego-involving climate and fear of failure, rule 5.]

**What the evidence base looks like as a whole.** A 2026 meta-analysis of motivational climate and outcomes in adolescents, in physical education and physical activity settings, pooled 334 effect sizes from 177 studies; of the effect sizes, 313 came from cross-sectional designs, 12 from longitudinal and 9 from experimental. A task or mastery climate correlated r = 0.27 (95% CI 0.22 to 0.32 in the paper's table; its text gives 0.20 to 0.32) with the outcomes pooled; heterogeneity across all the effect sizes pooled was extreme (I² = 98.45%; the paper gives no I² for the task-climate estimate alone), and the authors describe their model as a pattern of associations and do not claim it as a causal mechanism. The text read did not say how undesirable outcomes such as anxiety were coded, so the direction of that pooled figure for anxiety or burnout cannot be stated here ([Gu & Cheng, 2026](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2025.1716745)). The design count is the point for a coach: 94% of these effect sizes come from studies that measured everything at once.

## 2. The behaviours, one by one, and what each has been found to go with

The lists below come from the instruments researchers use and the guidelines they tested. A behaviour on the list is something you can see or hear in a session, which is why the self-audit in the deliverable uses them.

**Autonomy-supportive and task-involving behaviours.** The authors of a study of young footballers describe autonomy-supportive coaches as giving more choice and taking the athlete's perspective, and recommend conveying the meaning of activities and involving athletes in decisions, citing earlier work ([Morales-Sánchez et al., 2020](https://doi.org/10.3390/ijerph17134909)). The meta-analysis in section 1 lists, beside autonomy support, competence support, relatedness support, structure and involvement as related supports ([Mossman et al., 2024, abstract](https://doi.org/10.1080/1750984X.2022.2031252)). The coach programme tested in section 3 adds a behavioural list: more positive reinforcement, encouragement immediately after a mistake, corrective instruction given positively, sound technical instruction, team rules set early, and success defined as maximum effort and becoming the best one can be ([Smoll et al., 2007](https://doi.org/10.5195/jyd.2007.358)).

**Controlling behaviours.** The Controlling Coach Behaviors Scale, used in the footballers' study, has four subscales: controlled use of rewards, negative conditional support (the paper's term: withholding attention and affection when the athlete does not do what the coach wants), intimidation, and excessive personal control ([Morales-Sánchez et al., 2020](https://doi.org/10.3390/ijerph17134909)). The scale was developed by Bartholomew and colleagues (2010); that paper was not opened. The coach programme in section 3 adds two behaviours to avoid: withholding reinforcement for effort, and punishing mistakes ([Smoll et al., 2007](https://doi.org/10.5195/jyd.2007.358)). An ego-involving climate adds attention given mostly to the best athletes ([Gómez-López et al., 2020](https://doi.org/10.3390/ijerph17020592)).

In practice the difference is often a single sentence. A swimming coach who says why a set is swum at a steady pace, and lets the swimmer choose the order of the strokes, is giving a reason and a choice. A gymnastics coach who asks the gymnast what she felt on the landing before correcting it is taking her perspective. A volleyball coach who praises a setter's improved footwork after a lost point is recognising improvement. A rowing coach who goes quiet towards a crew after a slow piece, and warm again after a fast one, is using negative conditional support. A tennis coach who tells a junior which friends to see at weekends is exercising personal control outside sport. These illustrations are this unit's; they are not data.

## 3. What coach-training experiments showed

Surveys show what goes with what. An experiment trains some coaches and not others, then compares. Two were opened.

**Coach and parent workshops in community basketball.** The coaches of 17 teams in one community league took a 75-minute coach workshop (the Mastery Approach to Coaching), and parents in that league were offered a 60-minute parent workshop; 17 teams in a separate, matched community league served as control (34 coaches in all; 82 intervention and 69 control athletes analysed). Athletes were 151 boys and girls aged 9 to 15 (mean 11.6). Between preseason and late season, about 12 weeks apart, total sport anxiety on the Sport Anxiety Scale-2 (total score range 15 to 60) fell in the intervention group (mean 25.44 to 23.98) and rose in the control group (24.12 to 27.01), with significant time-by-condition interactions for the total and for somatic anxiety, worry and concentration disruption. The design was quasi-experimental, not randomised; only athletes whose parents attended were counted in the intervention group; no data on what coaches or parents actually did were collected; the first author delivered the workshops and wrote the manuals; no effect sizes were reported; and coach and parent effects cannot be separated ([Smoll et al., 2007](https://doi.org/10.5195/jyd.2007.358)).

**A randomised trial in grassroots football.** Sixty-eight youth football coaches in England, France, Spain and Greece were randomly assigned to a coach education programme (Empowering Coaching, n = 36) or to control (n = 32), and their behaviour was filmed and coded with a validated observation system, the Multidimensional Motivational Climate Observation System (MMCOS), at three points in the season. Intervention coaches showed a significant reduction in disempowering, ego-involving behaviour, and a temporary increase in autonomy and relatedness support ([Tzioumakis et al., 2026, full text of the final version read](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2026.1563931)). The workshop lasted about six hours; the players were aged 10 to 15. The paper reports no standardised effect sizes and measured no athlete outcomes; the authors call the gains in support "short-term, but not sustained".

**Studies not opened.** The randomised test of the Mastery Approach to Coaching on athletes' performance anxiety (Smith, Smoll & Cumming, 2007) could not be opened; nothing is reported from it.

What the experiments add to the surveys: a coach education programme of about six hours changed observed coach behaviour in one randomised trial, and the change in autonomy support faded within the season; a combined coach and parent programme went with lower anxiety in 9- to 15-year-olds in one non-randomised trial. Train your own behaviour deliberately and check it more than once a season [weak, positive: two controlled studies in sport, rule 5; ages 9 to 15 in the anxiety study and 10 to 15 in the football trial, so for 16 to 25 it is inference]. The faded effect is the reason the self-audit in the deliverable is repeated.

## 4. Rewards, punishment and exercise as punishment

**Rewards.** In self-determination theory a reward used to get compliance is controlling; the same scale that measures intimidation measures it ([Morales-Sánchez et al., 2020](https://doi.org/10.3390/ijerph17134909)). The meta-analysis of reward experiments in the course's reading (Deci, Koestner & Ryan, 1999) could not be opened, and no other source on whether rewards lower intrinsic motivation in young athletes was opened; this unit states nothing about how big that effect is or whether it exists in sport. Two things are sourced. Autonomy support was unrelated to external regulation (ρ = −.01) in the meta-analysis in section 1. And the coach programme that went with lower anxiety told coaches to increase positive reinforcement while defining success as effort and improvement ([Smoll et al., 2007](https://doi.org/10.5195/jyd.2007.358)). So praise and recognition were part of a programme with a good result, tied to effort; a prize promised for compliance is on the controlling list. Tie recognition to effort and improvement; do not use a reward to make an athlete do what you want [weak, positive, for the programme as a package, rule 5; practice, for the reward rule alone, which no study opened tests, rule 2].

**Punishment for mistakes.** The same programme told coaches to avoid punishing mistakes and to encourage learning from them ([Smoll et al., 2007](https://doi.org/10.5195/jyd.2007.358)). Whether that guideline alone did anything is unknown, because the programme was tested as a whole. Do not punish a mistake made while trying [weak, positive, for the package; the guideline alone untested].

**Exercise as punishment.** The claim as it circulates: making athletes run laps or do push-ups for a mistake, lateness or a loss builds discipline. For the claim: no peer-reviewed source found. The one study opened is qualitative: 20 former college athletes (nine sports, mean age 26.0; the United States setting and the age range of 19 to 44 are from the full text, not verified at the publisher's page on 17 Nov 2026) described what they experienced as emotional abuse. Most accepted some physical punishment as appropriate; they separated conditioning tied to performance from conditioning used to punish, and described the second as arbitrary, unsafe or cruel, with accounts of injury and fear (these accounts from the full text, not verified at the publisher's page on 17 Nov 2026; [Adams et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.3389/fspor.2024.1428682)). It measures no outcome and its sample is adult. Do not use exercise as punishment [practice; no study opened tests it; the qualitative accounts are from adults, so for under-18s inference]. Where punishment becomes harm is DD-SP1's subject; a concern about another coach goes to your club's or federation's welfare or safeguarding officer, the route on your SP1 scope card.

## 5. Selection, playing time and attention: the currencies you control

You decide who is picked, who plays, who gets the last word at the end of training. Athletes read those decisions as information about their worth. The questionnaire item quoted in section 1, the coach giving most attention to the stars, is scored as ego-involving, and the ego-involving climate went with more fear of failure in the handball study ([Gómez-López et al., 2020](https://doi.org/10.3390/ijerph17020592)). The IOC consensus statement asks coaches to focus on "each athlete's personal assets and mastery orientation" ([Bergeron et al., 2015, consensus](https://doi.org/10.1136/bjsports-2015-094962)). No study opened for this unit tested how playing time or selection decisions are communicated.

What to do [practice; no study opened tests these steps, rule 2]:

- Say in advance, to the squad, what selection and playing time depend on, and keep to it.
- Give every athlete at least one individual piece of feedback in each session; count it in the self-audit.
- Never use selection or playing time as a threat in training.
- Tell a deselected or benched athlete the reason in private, with what they can work on.

**Public and private feedback.** A common rule among coaches is to praise in public and correct in private. No behavioural study of how peer or coach evaluation in front of others affects young athletes was opened for this unit, and SP2 forbids drawing such advice from brain imaging. Two adjacent studies were opened. In a laboratory experiment with 48 participants (described in the full text as non-disabled adults aged 18 to 40; that description and the retention result not verified at the publisher's page on 17 Nov 2026), telling learners they were faster than average did not improve retention of a motor sequence compared with no performance feedback, and perceived competence rose in that group but not in the group given plain time feedback ([Lewis et al., 2023](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2022.1005705)) [weak, no learning benefit shown; outside sport; inference for athletes under 18]. In a survey of 101 team-sport athletes (mean age 28.7, age range not given; 40% para-athletes), a disempowering climate created by teammates went with more frustration of autonomy and relatedness ([Haberstock et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.1007/s12662-024-00980-4)) [weak, association; cross-sectional, mostly adults]. Neither tests public against private feedback. So the rule stays practice: correct technique in a way the whole group can learn from when it is about the drill; take anything about a single athlete's attitude, selection or worth aside [practice; no behavioural study in young athletes opened]. One such study, of youth athletes' perceived and preferred coach feedback and team motivational climate (Stein, Bloom & Sabiston, 2012, Psychology of Sport and Exercise), was found only as a copy on an author's university site, not at its publisher's page, so nothing is reported from it and the grade stands. SP7 must look for that evidence again.

## 6. High challenge together with high support

The model as it circulates: performance thrives where challenge and support are both high; challenge without support breaks people, support without challenge lets them coast. The source in the course's reading is a practitioner article on developing resilience ([Fletcher & Sarkar, 2016, abstract and notes read](https://doi.org/10.1080/21520704.2016.1255496)). The page opened showed only the abstract and notes. The notes describe the training programme as built on the authors' research with "the world's best athletes and teams" and its application with athletes preparing for the 2012 and 2016 Olympic Games, and they warn that "extreme challenge-no support environments" have been created in the name of toughening athletes up, which the authors call "the unethical and inappropriate imposition of adversity". The page opened reports no test of the programme's effects. The main text was not visible; the abstract and notes do not name the model, so that this article sets it out is the course reading list's attribution and was not checked, and no published critique of the model was opened.

What it rests on, then: the authors' interview and conceptual research with elite adult performers, and their experience applying it. Raise challenge only with support in place: a demanding task is paired with the information, encouragement and relationship that let the athlete meet it [practice; the studies behind it were not opened; samples elite adults, so for under-18s inference]. The model gives no warrant for harshness: its own authors set harshness without support outside it.

## 7. Enjoyment, and why young athletes leave

A meta-analysis of predictors of staying in youth sport (21 studies, 1,199 participants aged 5 to 19, nine cross-sectional and twelve longitudinal; sports including football, basketball, volleyball, swimming, handball, judo and ice hockey) found enjoyment the strongest correlate of intending to continue (r = 0.45, 95% CI 0.42 to 0.49, k = 4), with basic psychological needs and coach support moderately correlated (coach support r = 0.43, 95% CI 0.08 to 0.69, k = 4), and parental support, peer support and perceived competence weakly correlated. For actually staying, coach support had a medium effect (SMD = 0.68, 95% CI 0.27 to 1.10, k = 5), and intention to continue predicted continuing (SMD = 1.13) ([Zhang, Wang & Shao, 2022](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2022.871936)). Too few longitudinal studies measured enjoyment to pool its link with actually staying. Associations, from small numbers of studies per estimate.

A systematic review of dropout from swimming (six studies, aged 5 to 30, four from Canada) found reasons including other things to do, failure to improve competence, lack of enjoyment, boredom, pressure from parents, coaches or others, and conflict with the coach; the authors conclude that the main reasons are "controllable by the athletes" ([Monteiro et al., 2017](https://doi.org/10.3390/sports5030050)). Two of the six studies are from 1982 and 1985, and the designs are mixed: cross-sectional, qualitative and one prospective. The broader review of dropout in the course's reading (Crane & Temple, 2015) could not be opened.

The IOC statement asks for "a challenging and enjoyable sporting climate" ([Bergeron et al., 2015, consensus](https://doi.org/10.1136/bjsports-2015-094962)). Ask your athletes, once a term, what they enjoy in training and what they would change, and act on one answer [moderate, positive, for the association of enjoyment and coach support with intending to stay, rule 4; practice, for the asking, which no study opened tests]. Applies to all four bands; the studies sampled 5- to 19-year-olds, so for 19 to 25 it is inference.

## 8. Burnout and overtraining, as the consensus statements describe them

The IOC consensus statement on youth athletic development describes athlete burnout and the dropout that follows it as "a recognised part of competitive youth sport", arising from inappropriate and unrealistic demands and expectations and the "psychological overload (self or coach/parent induced)" that follows; it links early specialisation to "overuse injury, overtraining and burnout", and names "systems that promote over-training" among threats to young athletes. It recommends coach education that emphasises "autonomy-supportive, mastery-oriented sporting climates" that result "in less stress and more intrinsic motivation" ([Bergeron et al., 2015, consensus](https://doi.org/10.1136/bjsports-2015-094962)). A review proposing an integrated model of athlete burnout describes it as multifaceted, notes that it has been described from both a process and a state perspective, and proposes a model that includes major antecedents, early signs, consequences, and factors related to personality, coping and the environment; it offers the model to stimulate research, without presenting it as definitive ([Gustafsson et al., 2011, abstract read](https://doi.org/10.1080/1750984X.2010.541927)). The footballers' study in section 2 measured burnout as exhaustion, sport devaluation and a reduced sense of accomplishment, using a standard questionnaire ([Morales-Sánchez et al., 2020](https://doi.org/10.3390/ijerph17134909)).

The American Academy of Pediatrics clinical report on overuse, overtraining and burnout (Brenner et al., 2024) and the European College of Sport Science and American College of Sports Medicine consensus on the overtraining syndrome (Meeusen et al., 2013) could not be opened, so this unit gives no definition, sign list or recovery time from them.

For a coach the line is the one in SP1. Build a climate low in controlling behaviour and keep loads and rest planned [practice; IOC consensus; the studies behind it were not opened]. Persistent exhaustion, loss of interest in the sport, and a performance slide that rest does not fix are things to notice against the athlete's own baseline and raise in a first conversation; deciding whether it is burnout, overtraining or something else is assessment, which belongs to a doctor or a licensed or registered mental health professional, by the route on your SP1 scope card; SP9 builds that route [practice; the course's scope line]. Sara (fictional) already shows a pattern on her SP1 card that calls for that referral.

## 9. Parents as part of the climate

The IOC statement names "maladaptive perfectionistic tendencies, prompted by elevated parental expectations and criticism" among sources of psychological stress, and reports that "parents reportedly welcome advice on how to become a better sporting parent", while noting that putting such advice into practice is hard ([Bergeron et al., 2015, consensus](https://doi.org/10.1136/bjsports-2015-094962)).

A systematic review of parents and young athletes' motivation (29 studies; 9,185 athletes and 2,191 parents; mostly aged 10 to 19; 26 quantitative and 3 qualitative studies; 82.8% of the 29 studies cross-sectional) found parental autonomy support associated with more self-determined motivation, parental pressure with more controlled motivation and amotivation (one study found the opposite), and parental support with more enjoyment; whether parents or coaches matter more differs between studies. The authors conclude that "the manner and extent of their involvement are key" ([Gao et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2023.1291711)) [weak, association: a systematic review of mostly cross-sectional studies, no pooled estimate, rule 5].

On changing parents' behaviour: a systematic review of parent-education programmes in youth sport (12 studies, children aged 5 to 18, mainly tennis and football, quality from 20% to 100% of criteria met) reports that all five quantitative studies found significant results, while warning that differing designs and measures prevent firm conclusions and that no sport-specific measure of parents' behaviour was used ([Burke et al., 2021, full text read](https://doi.org/10.1080/1750984X.2021.1992793)). The basketball trial in section 3 included a 60-minute parent workshop ([Smoll et al., 2007](https://doi.org/10.5195/jyd.2007.358)). Give parents a short, specific briefing at the start of the season, in writing and in person [weak, positive: programmes evaluated in a few small studies of mixed design, rule 5; practice, for the page's format]. The parents' page in the deliverable is that briefing; SP8 extends it to competition day and SP10 to the season.

Where a parent's behaviour raises a safeguarding concern, the route is your club's or federation's welfare or safeguarding officer; do not handle it alone [practice; the course's scope line]; DD-SP1 deals with it.

## 10. The popular claim "Harsh coaching builds toughness", regraded

SP1 graded this claim [weak, against] and asked this unit to open the reviews on controlling coaching. Of the three reviews: the practitioner article by Fletcher and Sarkar (2016) was opened in abstract and notes (section 6); the two reviews by Bartholomew and colleagues (2009, 2011) could not be opened at a publisher's page, PubMed or a DOI address, and nothing is reported from them.

The evidence opened now: two cross-sectional studies in athletes that agree on direction, one of them in 12- to 17-year-old boys (controlling style with need thwarting and, through it, burnout; [Morales-Sánchez et al., 2020](https://doi.org/10.3390/ijerph17134909)), one across 10 to over 25 (controlling style with lower need satisfaction and, through it, fear of failure; [Hu et al., 2023](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2023.1106916)); one survey linking an ego-involving climate with fear of failure and anxiety in 16- and 17-year-olds ([Gómez-López et al., 2020](https://doi.org/10.3390/ijerph17020592)); and the authors of the challenge-and-support model setting harshness without support outside their model ([Fletcher & Sarkar, 2016](https://doi.org/10.1080/21520704.2016.1255496)). For the claim that harshness builds toughness: no peer-reviewed source found. Grade: [weak, against the claim; rule 5: correlational studies in athletes, no meta-analysis of controlling coaching opened, no controlled test]. Do not use harshness as a method [weak, against].

### C. One-page briefing for parents

**Built on the case: Dani's (fictional) club squad, 18 athletes aged 11 to 17.**

> **What we are trying to build, and how you can help**
>
> *For parents of the competition squad. This page is information, not medical or psychological advice.*
>
> **What we aim for in training.** Athletes improve against their own previous level, try things, and learn from mistakes. We give reasons for what we ask, offer choices where we can, and correct by saying what to do. We are working to stop shouting at athletes and to speak with each athlete in every session.
>
> **Why.** In studies of athletes and exercisers, a coaching style that gives reasons and choices has gone with more motivation that comes from the athlete; in a study of 12- to 17-year-old footballers a controlling style went with burnout, and in a study of athletes aged 10 to over 25 with lower need satisfaction and, through it, fear of failure. These studies show association, not cause. In one study of 9- to 15-year-olds, a short workshop for coaches together with one for parents went with lower anxiety over a season (Smoll and colleagues, 2007). In studies of parents, support has gone with more enjoyment and pressure with less self-driven motivation.
>
> **What helps at competitions.** Cheer effort and the whole team. Leave technical instructions to the coach. If your child looks to you after a contest, a nod or a smile is enough.
>
> **After a competition.** Let your child speak first. Ask what they enjoyed and what they learned before asking about the result.
>
> **What we will not do, and ask you not to do.** Punish mistakes, or use exercise as punishment. Compare your child aloud with others.
>
> **Weight, eating, body.** Coaches in this club make no comments on an athlete's weight, body or food. If you are worried about any of these, speak to [the club's named medical contact]. A concern about anyone's behaviour towards a child goes to [the club's welfare or safeguarding officer].
