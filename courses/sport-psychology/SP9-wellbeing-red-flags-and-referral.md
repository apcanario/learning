# SP9 · Wellbeing, red flags and referral

This unit is information, not legal, medical or psychological advice. It teaches recognition and referral. It teaches no assessment, no diagnosis and no treatment, and it names no crisis line, emergency number or health service as yours: those are blanks you fill in the deliverable, for the place where you coach, before you need them. Where a legal or reporting duty may apply to you, the unit puts it as a question and names the kind of body that can answer it.

Every technique and recommendation carries a grade in square brackets from SP1's grade key (section 9 of SP1: five rules applied in order; "several" means three or more; the labels *direction* and *inference*). *Inference* marks evidence from adults, students or non-athletes applied to an athlete under 18, as SP1 words it; where a study sampled other ages than the band it is applied to, the unit says "outside the sampled ages", as SP5, SP7 and SP8 do. Where the only source opened is a consensus or position statement whose studies were not opened, the grade is [practice] with the note "the studies behind it were not opened", as in SP2 to SP8 (the course has not yet settled how SP1's key treats this case). Age bands are SP2's: about 10 to 12, 13 to 15, 16 to 18 and 19 to 25. Where a claim rests on an abstract alone, the citation says "abstract read". Every page of an issuing body (a federation, an anti-doping organisation, a safeguarding body) carries the date it was read: 18 Nov 2026 for all of them.

The three case athletes return: Dani (fictional), 14, judo; Milan (fictional), 17, football; Sara (fictional), 20, 400 metres. They illustrate; they are never evidence. Each already has a referral under way (SP6). The deliverable is built for Sara, whose pattern (six weeks of about five hours' sleep a night, flat in sessions, no longer staying to talk after training, slower in each race since a personal best) has sent her to referral since SP1.

## 1. How common mental health symptoms are, and what the figures cannot tell you

**Elite adults.** The International Olympic Committee (IOC) consensus statement on mental health in elite athletes reports, from the studies it reviews, that the prevalence of depressive symptoms in elite athletes ranges from 4% to 68% across studies, and says that the prevalence of depressive symptoms in elite athletes and in the general population appears to be similar. It reports generalised anxiety disorder at 6.0% where a clinician confirmed the diagnosis and 14.6% where athletes reported symptoms on a questionnaire, social anxiety at 14.7% and panic disorder at 4.5% by self-report, and significant insomnia symptoms in about 64% of Olympic athletes ([Reardon et al., 2019, consensus, full text read at the publisher's page, its tables not shown there](https://doi.org/10.1136/bjsports-2019-100715)). These are the consensus's figures from studies this unit did not open. The width of the depression range is itself the finding: a range from 4% to 68% cannot be narrowed to one number for your squad, and why the studies differ so much was not checked here, because they were not opened.

The systematic review and meta-analysis of the same year that pooled these studies (Gouttebarge et al., 2019, in the course's reading list) could not be opened in this session: the publisher's page was not returned by any search and the PubMed record returned an empty page. No figure in this unit comes from it.

**Young athletes.** A scoping review of the mental health and well-being of elite youth athletes, defined as athletes with a mean age of 12 to 17 who travel internationally to compete, found 33 studies with 5,826 athletes. Disordered eating was the most studied outcome (16 studies), then anxiety (7) and depression (5). The proportion above a level at which treatment is typically indicated ("caseness") ranged from 0% to 14% in boys and 11% to 41% in girls for disordered eating; two studies estimated it for depression (7% in a mixed sample; 14% of boys and 40% of girls in the other), and one for anxiety (8% of boys, 28% of girls) ([Gwyther et al., 2024, scoping review, abstract read on PubMed](https://doi.org/10.1136/bjsports-2024-108244)).

**The limits, for your squad.**

- **Symptoms are not disorders.** Most figures come from questionnaires. A questionnaire score above a cut-off is a reason for a clinician to look, never a diagnosis, which is why SP4 keeps screening with licensed or registered professionals.
- **The youth evidence is thin and narrow.** Two studies for depression and one for anxiety; athletes at international level; mean ages 12 to 17. It speaks to the bands 13 to 15 and 16 to 18 at elite youth level. For club athletes, and for the band 10 to 12, nothing was opened: applying these figures there is the unit's inference. For 19 to 25 the adult elite figures above are the nearest; the ages of their samples were not checked, because the studies behind them were not opened.
- **Girls carry the higher figures in every youth outcome reported.** That is a description of the studies, and you still cannot tell from it which girl, or which boy, is struggling. SP2 reported an association between early menarche and depression in girls and women outside sport, and sent it here: the response to it is the same as to any other sign, the route below, and never a guess from growth or age.
- **What the figures do mean.** Symptoms are common enough at every level studied that a coach of a squad of any size should have the route in the deliverable written before the season, not after the first crisis [practice; the unit's reading of the figures above].

## 2. Risk and protective factors

**What the reviews found.** A scoping review of risk and protective factors for mental health in elite athletes evaluated 9 reviews and 43 empirical studies, 84% of them quantitative, and grouped the factors as personal or sport-environmental; the qualitative studies highlighted a feeling of autonomy, positive relations in sport and in private life, and adequate recovery as protective ([Kuettel & Larsen, 2020, scoping review, abstract read at the publisher's page](https://doi.org/10.1080/1750984X.2019.1689574)). The abstract does not list the risk factors one by one. In the youth scoping review, the common risk factors were sex, being an athlete (compared with non-athletes), and social or relationship factors involving coaches, parents or peers; the evidence on competition level was contradictory ([Gwyther et al., 2024, abstract read](https://doi.org/10.1136/bjsports-2024-108244)).

**What the consensus and position statements add.** FEPSAC (the European Federation of Sport Psychology) states, in the summary of its position statement on its own page, that injuries, overtraining and career termination may increase the risk of mental health disorders in elite athletes ([FEPSAC, position statement 12, summary page read 18 Nov 2026](https://fepsac.com/position-statements/)). The IOC consensus says that injury "can potentially unmask or precipitate mental health disorders", and that there is "minimal prospective research in this area" ([Reardon et al., 2019, consensus](https://doi.org/10.1136/bjsports-2019-100715)). The consensus of the International Society of Sport Psychology's think tank puts the environment first: "The environment can nourish or malnourish athlete mental health" ([Henriksen et al., 2020, consensus, abstract and propositions read at the publisher's page](https://doi.org/10.1080/1612197X.2019.1570473)).

**What a coach does with risk factors.** Nothing that resembles a risk score. A risk factor tells you where to look and when to look more often: an injury and the months back from it, a selection decision, a transition to senior sport, exams, a move away from home (SP2's transitions). The protective factors in the reviews are ones a coach shapes every week: the athlete's say over their own training (SP3's autonomy support), the relationships in the squad, and planned recovery [weak, positive, for the association; scoping reviews of correlational and qualitative studies, abstracts read; the protective factors are Kuettel and Larsen's, whose abstract gives no ages, so for under-18s outside the sampled ages as far as is known; Gwyther et al.'s risk factors come from elite youth athletes of mean age 12 to 17]. Sara (fictional) carries one factor the sources opened name, an injury and the months back from it (eight months back from a hamstring injury), and two of SP2's transitions at once: her first senior season and university away from home. Listing them assesses nothing. They are the reason to ask sooner.

## 3. What the consensus and position statements ask of coaches and of clubs

**Of coaches.** A Delphi study on the coach's role in athlete mental health, with 15 experts (2 coaches, 3 athletes over 18, 8 licensed mental health professionals and 2 health educators) voting over two rounds, a mean rating of 7 or more out of 9 counting as consensus, sets the coach's part in three areas: team culture, identifying athletes at risk and connecting them with care, and ongoing support. It states that "Coaches should foster team cultures that support athlete mental health", gives the coach the task to "facilitate referral to appropriate support" from a licensed practitioner, and says that return to play "should be determined by a licensed mental health practitioner and not a coach". It describes maintaining athletes' privacy as part of supporting them. The experts doubted that coaches could moderate sport demands during high-stress periods, and questioned how coaches receive support for their own mental health ([Bissett et al., 2020, Delphi consensus, full text read at the publisher's page; its table of final statements was not in the text read](https://doi.org/10.1136/bmjsem-2019-000676)). The panel's athletes were adults; the article says recruitment started from key informants who were members of the NCAA Mental Health Task Force and USA-based authors of the IOC consensus, and does not say where the panellists were based.

The IOC consensus says that "Within sport, coaches are often the primary interpersonal interface with the athlete", that "Coaches can ensure that training is age and developmentally appropriate", that for younger elite athletes coaches can communicate the importance of mental healthcare to parents, and that "Coaches should feel comfortable discussing eating disorders and RED-S with athletes" ([Reardon et al., 2019, consensus](https://doi.org/10.1136/bjsports-2019-100715)). Discussing a concern is different from commenting on weight or body, which the course's safety rule still rules out (DD-SP2).

**Of clubs and organisations.** The IOC consensus calls it best practice "to develop and rehearse a mental health emergency action management plan", with "clarity on what constitutes an emergency, who should be contacted and when", and a review of procedures after any incident ([Reardon et al., 2019](https://doi.org/10.1136/bjsports-2019-100715)). The IOC's toolkit of 2021 says that "All sporting organisations should develop a Mental Health Emergency Action Plan (MHEAP)", usually written by the medical team with licensed mental health experts, and tells every member of the athlete's entourage to check whether their team or club has one and to understand their role in it; it asks organisations to name a lead for referral and make the plan known to everyone ([IOC, *Mental Health in Elite Athletes Toolkit*, 2021, pp. 39, 47, 50 to 51 and 62 to 63, read 18 Nov 2026](https://stillmed.olympics.com/media/Document%20Library/IOC/Athletes/Safe-Sport-Initiatives/IOC-Mental-Health-In-Elite-Athletes-Toolkit-2021.pdf)). The think tank asks organisations to make mental health one or a few people's assigned responsibility, because "Sometimes 'everybody's business' means 'nobody's responsibility'", and gives that person the job of "building and maintaining a network of service providers and setting up a system of referral" ([Henriksen et al., 2020](https://doi.org/10.1080/1612197X.2019.1570473)). FEPSAC's comparison of six European countries found that the competencies, certification and professional boundaries of service providers, and national health systems, differ so widely that a single gold standard is hard to define ([FEPSAC, summary page](https://fepsac.com/position-statements/)): one reason the route in this unit is a set of blanks you fill for your own country.

So, for a coach [practice; consensus; the studies behind it were not opened]: find out whether your club has an emergency plan and who leads referral; if it has neither, ask the club's welfare or safeguarding officer, or its committee, who will write them, and use the deliverable's plan for your own squad meanwhile.

**SP1's scope line, checked.** The three statements agree with SP1's line on the division of work: coaches recognise and refer, licensed practitioners assess and treat, and a licensed practitioner decides return. They go further than SP1's sentence "your part ends at the referral" in one respect: the Delphi's third area, ongoing support, and the IOC toolkit's follow-up within 24 hours (section 6) keep the coach in the athlete's life after the referral. This unit reads SP1's line as ending your part in assessment and treatment at the referral, while your part as their coach goes on (sections 8 and 9). The difference is raised as a flag on SP1 for the course's syllabus review.

## 4. Recognising, never diagnosing: the signs a coach can see

You see behaviour against an athlete's own baseline (SP7). You do not decide what it is. The IOC's toolkit puts the coach's position this way: coaches "are in a position to recognise differences in moods, performance, social interactions" and other changed behaviours that might indicate further support is needed ([IOC toolkit, 2021, p. 29](https://stillmed.olympics.com/media/Document%20Library/IOC/Athletes/Safe-Sport-Initiatives/IOC-Mental-Health-In-Elite-Athletes-Toolkit-2021.pdf)). Its description of the recognition tool asks whether experiences are "significant and/or persistent" (p. 41). No source opened gives a coach a number of days or weeks for any sign. The lists of what you can see under each heading are this unit's own, drawn from the sources named in each paragraph; no source opened gives a coach a tested list of signs, and they are not a checklist. The time points below are this unit's, graded [practice], and set so that you raise a concern early and refer without waiting for a clinician's duration, as SP4 did for anxiety.

Two rules hold across every area [practice; the unit's own]:

- **A first conversation when a change against baseline shows in most sessions for about two weeks**, or sooner if the athlete is distressed by it or it costs them something that matters to them. Two weeks is this unit's reading of SP4's "for weeks" (row 3); it is the point for asking, not for deciding.
- **Referral offered at the first conversation; made when the athlete agrees, or when the change continues at the follow-up you agreed, spreads beyond sport, or the athlete asks for help.** A red flag (section 5) goes at once, by section 7, without waiting for anything.

**Low mood.** What you can see: flat in sessions, little reaction to good or bad results, loss of enjoyment, tearfulness unlike their baseline, withdrawal from teammates (SP7's flat and withdrawn states): a rower who stops joking in the boathouse, a basketball player who no longer stays for shooting practice. What you can hear: talk of being useless, of nothing being worth it, of feeling hopeless. Hopelessness, or feeling so overwhelmed that they cannot function, is a red flag on the recognition tool's page, not a sign to watch ([ISSF, IOC Sport Mental Health Recognition Tool, read 18 Nov 2026](https://issf-sports.org/issf/health/ioc-sport-mental-health-recognition-tool)) [practice; IOC tool].

**Anxiety that has spread beyond competition.** SP4's normal-or-refer table already gives this: worry, nausea or poor sleep at school, at work, in training or on ordinary days is not ordinary competition nerves, and goes to referral (SP4, row 6) [practice; IOC consensus; the studies behind it were not opened]. A sudden episode of overwhelming fear with marked physical symptoms such as sweating or shortness of breath, new or unlike earlier ones, is a red flag that needs medical help at once: it could be a panic attack or another medical problem (ISSF page; SP4, row 9).

**Burnout and overtraining.** SP3 took burnout's three dimensions from a study of young footballers: persistent exhaustion, devaluing the sport, and a reduced sense of accomplishment. Add what you can see on the training log (the unit's own list; the overtraining consensus statements were not opened, F32): performance falling over weeks while training holds or rises, illness or niggles that keep returning. FEPSAC names overtraining as a factor that may raise the risk of mental health disorders (section 2). Telling burnout from overtraining, from an illness or from low mood is a doctor's work; your part is to report the pattern and adjust load as the doctor advises [practice; the course's scope line; SP3].

**Disordered eating and low energy availability.** The IOC's consensus on Relative Energy Deficiency in Sport (REDs) describes harm to health and performance in male and female athletes whose energy intake is too low for what their training uses, and reports growing evidence of an interplay between mental health and REDs; it introduces a clinical assessment tool for clinicians ([Mountjoy et al., 2023, consensus, abstract read on PubMed](https://doi.org/10.1136/bjsports-2023-106994)). The mental health consensus states that dissatisfaction with body image "may be the strongest predictor of eating disorders in athletes" ([Reardon et al., 2019](https://doi.org/10.1136/bjsports-2019-100715)). What you can see: skipped meals or snacks, talk about body shape or weight, extra training on top of the plan (a gymnast running before the session, a cyclist adding rides), unease at team meals, injuries that keep coming. Dramatic weight change is a red flag on the tool's page. For this area there is no two-week wait: raise the first concern with the person your route names for medical concerns, make no comment on the athlete's weight, body or food, and suggest no change to what they eat [practice; IOC consensus; the studies behind it were not opened; the course's safety rule]. Dani's (fictional) skipped after-school snack went this way in SP6. Which sports the youth disordered-eating studies sampled is not given in the abstract read (DD-SP2 takes this further).

**Sleep trouble.** The athlete sleep consensus gives a range of 7 to 9 hours as appropriate for healthy adults and 8 to 10 hours for teenagers, lists poor mental health, including traits of "maladaptive perfectionism and hyperarousal", among candidate risk factors for poor sleep in elite athletes, and tells practitioners to start with a questionnaire validated in athletes and to "refer athletes to a sleep specialist for clinical diagnosis, when necessary" ([Walsh et al., 2021, consensus, read at the publisher's page](https://doi.org/10.1136/bjsports-2020-102025)). The questionnaire belongs to whoever the athlete is referred to, not to you (SP4). The consensus also says two things about the night before competition that sit uneasily together: one of its boxed points, on banking extra sleep in the nights before, says athletes can be confident that "a poor night's sleep the night before competition should not affect their performance", while its text says the effect of partial sleep restriction over one to three nights, "a more real-world scenario, remains unclear" (Walsh et al., 2021). Neither statement is about weeks of short sleep on ordinary nights, which is what calls for referral. Milan's (fictional) bad sleep the night before matches is the night-before case they address. What you can see or ask: how long they usually sleep on ordinary nights (SP2), against the consensus ranges for their age [weak, for the ranges; consensus built on studies outside sport, as SP2 grades them; inference for under-18 athletes]. Short sleep on most ordinary nights for about two weeks is the point for a first conversation; with flatness or withdrawal, it is the point for referral [practice]. Sara's six weeks of about five hours a night met both long ago.

**A bad response to injury.** A consensus of seven sport injury psychology researchers, reviewing mostly Northern Hemisphere studies of able-bodied athletes and giving no age range, states that "injured athletes are at risk for a variety of mental health issues, including clinical anxiety", that re-injury concerns can delay or prevent a return, and that "physical and psychological readiness to RTS do not necessarily coincide" (RTS: return to sport) ([Tranaeus et al., 2024, consensus, full text read at the publisher's page](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-024-02045-w)). It does not say where an ordinary response ends. What you can see: withdrawal from the squad during rehabilitation (a volleyball player who stops coming to watch matches while injured), low mood that outlasts setbacks in rehabilitation, fear that stops a cleared athlete doing what their physiotherapist has cleared, or a hurry to return against the medical plan. The physical return is the medical team's decision (SP8); the psychological signs go by the same two rules as low mood [practice; consensus; the studies behind it were not opened]. Sara's return from a hamstring injury, a personal best and then slower races are on her coach's record for this reason.

**Perfectionism turning harmful.** A meta-analysis of 14 studies (3,496 athletes aged 11 to 55, from school and university athletes to junior elite, despite the title's "elite") found that socially prescribed perfectionism, the belief that others demand perfection, was positively associated with emotional and physical exhaustion (r = 0.150, 95% CI 0.025 to 0.271) and with devaluing the sport (r = 0.138, 0.009 to 0.261), while self-oriented perfectionism, setting oneself high standards, was negatively associated with devaluation (r = −0.246, −0.344 to −0.143). The studies were correlational and heterogeneity was very high (I² = 94% across all effect sizes pooled; 86% to 91% for these three associations) and unexplained ([Yang et al., 2023, meta-analysis, read at the publisher's page](https://doi.org/10.3390/healthcare11101417)) [moderate, for the association; a meta-analysis in athletes whose heterogeneity its authors could not explain, rule 4; a correlation, so it says nothing about cause]. High standards on their own are not a sign. What you can see when perfectionism turns: fear of what others will think of a mistake (a swimmer who will not race a relay leg in case she lets the others down), self-criticism after every session, training through pain to avoid falling short, distress at any result below the target, sleep lost to going over performances. Those go by the two rules above [practice]. Sara describes herself as a perfectionist and logs her times to the tenth of a second; her coach records her words as she says them, with the date, and does not label or sort them.

**Reading SP7's tables for red flags.** SP7's baseline sheet and state-response table already send three states here: flat for weeks, withdrawn against baseline for weeks, tearful unlike baseline and recurring. Each now goes by the deliverable's red-flag table (A).

## 5. The IOC's recognition tool, read on the pages that present it

**What it is.** The Sport Mental Health Recognition Tool 1 (SMHRT-1) was published by the IOC with an assessment tool, SMHAT-1, in a paper published online in September 2020. The recognition tool "was developed for athletes and their entourage (eg, friends, fellow athletes, family and coaches)"; the assessment tool is for sports medicine physicians and other licensed or registered health professionals, for elite athletes "aged 16 years and older" ([Gouttebarge et al., 2021, consensus, abstract read at the publisher's page](https://doi.org/10.1136/bjsports-2020-102411)). The International Shooting Sport Federation's page presenting the tool, read on 18 Nov 2026 (undated; footer © 2025), describes it as a list of athlete experiences (thoughts, feelings, behaviours, physical changes) that could indicate mental health problems, to be used "to recognise mental health problems but not to diagnose them" ([ISSF, read 18 Nov 2026](https://issf-sports.org/issf/health/ioc-sport-mental-health-recognition-tool)). The IOC's toolkit of 2021 says the tool was developed by the IOC Mental Health Working Group for athletes, coaches, family members and other members of the entourage, and that "the SMHRT-1 is not a diagnostic tool" ([IOC toolkit, 2021, p. 41](https://stillmed.olympics.com/media/Document%20Library/IOC/Athletes/Safe-Sport-Initiatives/IOC-Mental-Health-In-Elite-Athletes-Toolkit-2021.pdf)).

**Its red flags**, as the ISSF page lists them, for which it says to "seek immediate help": comments related to harming self or others; talking about feeling hopeless or so overwhelmed that you cannot function; dramatic weight changes; other highly uncharacteristic behaviours, emotions and appearances; and "An episode of overwhelming sudden onset of fear with marked physical symptoms such as sweating or shortness of breath that has never before been experienced or is different from prior episodes (could be a panic attack or another medical problem)" (ISSF page, read 18 Nov 2026).

**What it does not do.** It does not diagnose. It sets no score and no duration: the toolkit asks only whether the experiences are significant and/or persistent (p. 41). The paper that presents it concerns elite athletes, and its companion assessment tool is for those aged 16 and older; neither the paper's abstract nor the ISSF page gives an age for the recognition tool, so using it with a squad of 11- to 17-year-olds is this unit's inference. It does not replace your club's emergency plan or tell you who to call where you coach. Its own list of athlete experiences, which the ISSF page links as a PDF on the IOC's athlete site, was found by search but returned a server error when opened in this session, so this unit describes the tool from the ISSF page, the IOC toolkit and the paper's abstract, and reproduces none of it. The ISSF page says the tool may be freely copied in its current form and should not be revised without the approval of the IOC Mental Health Working Group: print it from the IOC's or your federation's page, not from this unit.

So [practice; IOC tool]: keep the tool's red flags in the deliverable's table word for word as the ISSF page gives them; read the tool itself when you fill your template; never use it as a questionnaire on your squad (SP4).

## 6. The first conversation

**Where and when.** Somewhere private enough to talk and open enough to be seen: the side of the pool, an office with the door open, a bench at the edge of the pitch while the squad cools down. For an athlete under 18, some safeguarding codes require that contact between an adult and a minor be in view of others. One example, as a code that issues it states it: the U.S. Center for SafeSport's Minor Athlete Abuse Prevention Policies require that "All one-on-one in-program contact between an Adult Participant and a Minor Athlete must be observable and interruptible", except in emergencies ([U.S. Center for SafeSport, *How it Works: MAAPP*, page last modified 23 Dec 2025, read 18 Nov 2026](https://maapp.uscenterforsafesport.org/how-it-works/)). That code applies to adults in the US Olympic and Paralympic movement; it is not stated here as yours. What does your club's or federation's code require for a conversation with a minor alone? Ask your club's welfare or safeguarding officer. Choose a time with room to talk: never just before competing, never straight after a loss (SP7, SP8), unless a red flag is in front of you: then section 7 at once, whatever the timetable [practice].

**The words.** In this order [practice; IOC toolkit for listening, offering contact and following up; no study opened tests any wording]:

1. **Say what you have noticed, in behaviour.** "I've noticed you've been leaving straight after sessions, and you've seemed tired for a few weeks." No interpretation, no label.
2. **Ask, and listen.** "How are things, in sport and outside it?" Let them talk at their own pace; do not lead. The NSPCC's sport safeguarding page advises allowing the person to talk "without leading or intrusive questions" ([NSPCC Sport, *How to respond to child abuse concerns*, updated 7 Jan 2026, read 18 Nov 2026](https://sport.nspcc.org.uk/news-and-blogs/2026-01-how-to-respond-to-child-abuse-concerns-in-your-sport/)); it is written for abuse concerns, and the same rule serves here. The IOC toolkit's words for this step are to "Listen to and validate the athlete's concerns" (p. 41).
3. **If they talk of hopelessness or not wanting to go on, ask directly whether they are thinking of harming themselves or ending their life.** The common belief that asking about suicide puts the idea into someone's head is not supported by the evidence opened here: an editorial reviewing 13 papers (2001 to 2013) in adolescents and adults, general and at-risk, found that none showed a statistically significant increase in suicidal ideation among people asked about suicidal thoughts ([Dazzi et al., 2014, editorial, abstract read at the publisher's page](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0033291714001299); an editorial's review of 13 studies, outside Part 7's source types, kept until a meta-analysis is opened) [weak, asking does not increase ideation; studies outside sport, rule 5; for athletes, inference]. Then go to section 7. Asking is not assessing: you do not judge how serious it is.
4. **Offer help, and say what happens next.** "I'd like you to talk to someone who knows about this. Can I help you set that up?" The IOC toolkit's non-emergency pathway is to "Offer to contact your club/team's sports medicine physician and/or licensed mental health provider" (p. 41).
5. **Agree the next step and a time to talk again.** The toolkit says to follow up within 24 hours "to determine if further action is needed" (p. 41).

**What not to promise.** Secrecy. The NSPCC's sport page tells adults not to say "I promise I won't tell anyone", because "You can't promise this" (NSPCC Sport, 2026). Say instead what you will do with what they tell you: "I won't tell the squad. If I'm worried about your safety, I'll need to tell [the person on the plan], and I'll tell you who and why, before I do it whenever I can." Promise only what you will keep [practice; NSPCC guidance; the studies behind it were not opened].

**Confidentiality with a minor, and when parents are told.** Who may consent to health care at 16 or 17, who must be told about a minor's mental health, and what a coach may keep confidential differ between countries and organisations, and this unit states none of them as yours. Put the questions to the right bodies before the season, and write the answers into the deliverable's plan:

- At what age can an athlete in your squad consent to their own health care, and when must a parent or guardian be told? Ask your club's or federation's medical officer, or your national health regulator.
- What must you pass on, to whom, when a minor tells you about a risk to their safety? Ask your club's welfare or safeguarding officer and your federation's safeguarding policy.
- Do you have any legal duty to report, and to whom? Ask your national safeguarding body for sport or the child-protection authority.

Two rules hold whatever the answers [practice; the course's safety rules]. The burden of telling never rests on the child: you do not ask a minor to tell their parents, or to report a concern, themselves; you pass it on by the route, and tell them that you are doing so. And for an adult athlete, the decision to seek help is theirs: the IOC toolkit tells the entourage to "Respect their right to make their own decisions related to seeking further help", and says that, aside from when an athlete poses an immediate danger to themselves or others, "you can only encourage and support an athlete in seeking further help" (pp. 39, 41). Their parents are told nothing without their agreement, unless the emergency route requires it.

**Write it down** the same day: the date, what you saw, the athlete's words, what you offered, what they agreed. Facts, no interpretation (SP7's baseline sheet takes the entry).

## 7. Urgent risk: when an athlete talks about harming themselves or not wanting to live

The IOC toolkit's emergency pathway asks one question first: is there "serious and immediate danger to the athlete or to others"? If yes: "Contact emergency services", "Stay with the athlete until help arrives (if it is safe for you)", and "Follow the reporting and notification procedures designated by your club/team/organisation's Mental Health Emergency Action Plan" (IOC toolkit, 2021, p. 41) [practice; IOC consensus; the studies behind it were not opened].

As immediate steps for a coach [practice; steps 3, 6 and 7, and in step 4 the offer of contact with the club's medical staff, from the IOC toolkit and consensus; steps 1, 2 and 5, the rest of step 4, "or you cannot tell" in step 3, and the order are the unit's own, and no source opened tests them]:

1. **Take it seriously, every time.** Never treat it as attention-seeking or a figure of speech.
2. **Do not leave the athlete alone.** Ask another adult to cover the squad; if there is none, stop the session and keep the squad together where you can see them until they are collected. With a minor, a second adult joins you if one is near.
3. **If there is immediate danger, or you cannot tell, call the emergency number** where you are, and stay with the athlete until help arrives, if it is safe for you (IOC toolkit, p. 41). A sudden episode of overwhelming fear with marked physical symptoms needs medical help at once, whatever you judge about danger (section 4).
4. **If there is no immediate danger, get help at once:** with the athlete, call the urgent contact on your plan (the club's medical contact, the athlete's doctor, the crisis line you have written down); for a minor, the parents or guardians as your plan and the club's welfare officer say, unless what the child has told you concerns how someone at home treats them: then the welfare officer, or the police or child-protection authority if the child is in danger, and not the parents, until the welfare officer advises. Stay until the athlete is with someone who can help. If the athlete refuses help or leaves while you fear for their safety, call the emergency number at once and tell them what was said and where the athlete may be going.
5. **Do not ask for details of how**, do not try to talk them out of it, do not promise to keep it secret, and do not judge how serious it is beyond step 3's one question: is there danger now, or can you not tell?
6. **Tell the person your club's plan names**, and write down what was said and done, the same day.
7. **Afterwards**, follow up with the athlete within 24 hours (p. 41), ask the club for the review of procedures the IOC consensus recommends after any incident ([Reardon et al., 2019](https://doi.org/10.1136/bjsports-2019-100715)), and talk to someone yourself (section 11).

The same steps apply when an athlete talks about harming someone else; then the safety of others comes first, and the emergency number is the first call if anyone is in danger. If an athlete has already hurt themselves, or says they have taken something to harm themselves, it is a medical emergency: the emergency number and first aid at once.

**The services you look up now.** This unit names none, because they differ by country and region and change. Before the season, find and write in the deliverable's plan: the emergency number where you train and where you compete; a crisis line for young people and one for adults in your country; the out-of-hours health service; your club's urgent contact and welfare officer; your federation's welfare or safeguarding contact; and, for a university athlete, the university's out-of-hours or crisis contact. Ring or open each one to check it works, and write the date you checked [practice].

## 8. The referral route: to whom, how, what to hand over, and staying their coach

**To whom.** By setting, as SP4 set it and the deliverable fills it [practice; the course's scope line; IOC toolkit]:

- **An academy or national programme:** its medical staff, who refer on; the IOC toolkit says a referral to specialist care "will usually be made by the team doctor" (p. 39).
- **A club athlete:** the athlete's own doctor, with the parents of a minor as the club's medical contact advises; the club's named person for medical concerns.
- **A university athlete:** the student health or counselling service, or the athlete's doctor.
- **A concern about how anyone is treating a child:** the club's welfare or safeguarding officer, by your federation's policy (DD-SP1).
- **Eating, weight or body:** the person for medical concerns, never a nutrition plan from you (DD-SP2).
- **Injury:** the physiotherapist or doctor managing it.

**How.** With an adult, with their agreement: you offer to make the contact, or help them make it, and give them the contact in writing. With a minor, by the answers you got to section 6's questions, and with the parents as they say. You make the referral; you do not ask a child to make it alone [practice].

**What to hand over**, with the athlete's agreement [practice; the unit's own; consistent with SP7's baseline sheet]: what you saw, in behaviour words, with dates and against their baseline; the athlete's own words, if they agree you pass them on; what you have changed in training; how to reach you. What you leave out: any label, any guess at a cause, any score.

**Staying their coach.** Keep the athlete in the squad and in ordinary training unless the person they were referred to advises otherwise. Where the concern is eating, low energy, overtraining or injury, ask the medical contact that week what training is safe until the appointment, and add no load meanwhile [practice; the unit's own]. Keep selection independent of the referral (SP3). Ask how they are, not what was said in an appointment. Tell the squad nothing unless the athlete chooses what to tell; the Delphi counts maintaining athletes' privacy as part of supporting them ([Bissett et al., 2020](https://doi.org/10.1136/bmjsem-2019-000676)) [practice; consensus; the studies behind it were not opened].

**When skill practice brings up distress (SP5's stop rule).** SP5's skill cards stop an exercise for an athlete in whom practice brings distress, images or thoughts they cannot set aside, or thoughts about more than sport. The route is the same: stop the exercise for that athlete without comment in front of others, hold the first conversation after the session, and refer by the route above for their setting; if the distress includes a red flag, section 7 applies at once. The skills block continues for the squad and resumes for that athlete only when they ask and the person they were referred to sees no reason against it [practice; the course's scope line; SP5].

**Asking whether an athlete may climb SP6's ladder.** SP6 keeps an athlete whose referral is under way at level 3 at most, until the coach has asked the person they were referred to whether there is any reason not to go higher. How to ask [practice; the unit's own; no study opened tests it]:

1. **Ask the athlete first**, and for a minor the parents as your plan says: "Can I ask [the person you're seeing] one question about training? I won't ask anything about what you talk about."
2. **Ask through the route**: the team doctor or medical contact, or the practitioner directly if the athlete agrees, in writing where you can.
3. **Ask one question, describing the training, not the athlete**: "[Name] has agreed that I contact you. In training I plan to add drills where one attempt is watched and scored and missing a standard has a sporting consequence, such as losing a point. Is there any reason, from your side, not to? I don't need any detail of your work with them; a yes or no, and anything I should avoid, is enough."
4. **Follow the answer**, record its date, and let the athlete climb only if they ask (SP6).

**Medication and anti-doping.** Some athletes take medication that anti-doping rules restrict. As one national anti-doping organisation's page states it, ADHD stimulant medications "are banned in-competition", are on the World Anti-Doping Agency's Prohibited List as S6 stimulants, and an athlete with diagnosed ADHD can seek a Therapeutic Use Exemption, in advance or retroactively depending on their level ([Sport Integrity Australia, web page on ADHD medication in sport, undated, read 18 Nov 2026](https://www.sportintegrity.gov.au/what-we-do/anti-doping/substance-education/responsible-use-of-adhd-medication-sport)). That is Australia's application of the rules, read on one day; SP7's 2019 statement of the same point is replaced by this dated one. What applies to your athlete, at their level, this year? Ask your national anti-doping organisation. Your part is to know that the question exists, to ask the athlete or family whether it affects them, and to leave medication decisions to their doctor [practice].

**Autism.** No source on autism in sport was opened at a publisher's page for this unit, so it says nothing about it, as SP7 did. The route for an autistic athlete is the same as for anyone.

## 9. Return and follow-up

**Follow-up.** Within 24 hours of the first conversation or referral, and then at the points you agreed (IOC toolkit, p. 41). Ask how they are and whether the contact happened; if it did not, help again [practice; IOC consensus; the studies behind it were not opened].

**Return.** The Delphi says return to play "should be determined by a licensed mental health practitioner and not a coach" ([Bissett et al., 2020](https://doi.org/10.1136/bmjsem-2019-000676)). After an injury, the injury consensus warns that physical and psychological readiness to return "do not necessarily coincide" ([Tranaeus et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-024-02045-w)). So [practice; consensus; the studies behind it were not opened]: agree with the athlete, and through the route with the person advising them, what training looks like during care and on return; keep SP6's cap until that person has answered; build back with the athlete's say over the pace; and review on dates written into the plan.

**If things get worse**, go back to the red-flag table. A referral under way does not stop the urgent route.

## 11. Your own wellbeing

A systematic review of 101 studies (2010 to 2024) of elite coaches, in professional leagues, national teams and national or international championships, found burnout the most studied problem (40 studies) and stress next (32); it describes stressors from performance pressure, workload, managing athletes and outside expectations linked with a higher risk of burnout, lower wellbeing and strained coach-athlete relationships, and found few interventions aimed at coaches, among them a six-week mindfulness programme with US college coaches and a six-week mental skills programme with five British elite coaches ([Kang & Lee, 2025, systematic review, read at the publisher's page](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2025.1666035)). Most studies were from Western countries and all were of elite coaches. For club, school and volunteer coaches, nothing was opened.

What follows [practice; the review describes stressors and tests nothing a coach can use; the Delphi's experts questioned how coaches are supported]:

- **You are not the route.** Your part ends where the plan hands over. A coach who tries to be the athlete's counsellor carries what a clinician is trained and supervised to carry.
- **After an urgent event, talk to someone**: the club's welfare officer, the person who leads referral, your own doctor. Ask for the review the IOC consensus recommends; it reviews the procedure.
- **Use the skills on yourself.** SP8 applied SP5's skills to your own nerves on competition day; the same breathing and planning serve after a hard conversation.
- **Watch your own baseline.** Sleep, flatness and withdrawal are signs in coaches too. The route for you is your own doctor or your employer's or club's support.

### C. Three conversation scripts

**Built for Sara.** Each is said in private, at a quiet moment after a session, unless it says otherwise [practice; no study opened tests any wording].

**C1. With the athlete: the referral, and staying her coach.**

> "Thanks for staying a minute. I want to check in about the last few weeks. You've been sleeping about five hours, you've seemed flat in sessions, and you haven't been staying to talk like you used to. Your races since the PB have been slower, and I'm not worried about the times. I'm asking how you are."
>
> (She talks. He listens and does not interpret.)
>
> "Thank you for telling me. I think this is worth talking to someone who knows about sleep and how people are coping, more than I do. You mentioned the student health service. Would you like me to help you book it, or would you rather do it yourself?"
>
> "Would it help if I told them what I've seen in training? Only what I've seen, and only if you're happy with that."
>
> "Nothing changes about your place in the group. I won't tell the group anything. If I were ever worried about your safety, I'd have to get help, and I'd tell you who and why, before I did it whenever I could. Can we talk again tomorrow after the session?"

The next day, and after her appointment (*added*: booked):

> "How did it go getting the appointment? You don't need to tell me anything about it. Is there anything in training that would help, or that I should change?"

Before her coach asks the level-3 question:

> "Before I add the harder drills, I'd like to ask the student health service one question: whether there's any reason not to. I won't ask anything about what you talk about there. Is that all right with you?"

**C2. With a parent: Sara's parents, who have messaged asking why her times are slower (*added*).**

Sara is an adult; her coach speaks to her first and replies only as she agrees.

> To Sara: "Your parents have messaged me asking about your times. What would you like me to say, if anything? I won't mention anything else unless you want me to."

If she asks him to reply only about training:

> "Thanks for your message. Sara's training is going on as planned. She knows you're asking, and she'll talk to you about it herself."

If she asks him to tell them more, he says only what she has agreed, in her words. He does not discuss her sleep, mood or appointment with them on his own initiative, unless the emergency route requires it [practice; IOC toolkit, p. 41: the athlete's right to decide].

**C3. With the club: the welfare officer, before the season and after a referral.**

> Before the season: "I'm writing a referral plan for my groups. Does the club have a mental health emergency action plan, and who leads referral? For the minors in my other groups, who must be told, and when, if I'm worried about one of them? And what must I pass on if an adult athlete tells me something that makes me fear for their safety?"
>
> After Sara's referral: "One of my adult athletes is being supported through the university's health service. I'm not giving her name or any details without her agreement. I've kept her in ordinary training and kept selection unchanged. I'm asking you to check the plan's contacts with me, and who I talk to if anything urgent happens at a competition."
