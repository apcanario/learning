# Managing an Athlete's Career · briefs

The shared preamble (`{GOAL}` is filled with the course goal) and one brief per unit. Drafted 21 Nov 2026 in the design chat (Fable 5.2). kit/pack.py reads every `## ` section; nothing above the first is read.

## Preamble

Write one unit of a self-study course in the long-form report format of the Learning Project's markdown courses: a long-form report in Markdown. Length is whatever the topic needs (2,000–6,000 words for the body, up to and including the Self-check; the deliverable is outside that guide); do not pad and do not cut to a target. Quality over quantity: a shorter unit whose every claim was opened and checked is better than a longer one. Reader: a coach of an athlete in an Olympic or federation sport (an individual or small-team sport with little money in it), at a level below the one at which an agency takes interest, who also stands in a second relationship to that athlete: sponsoring them through a business of the coach's own (a kit or equipment label, a gym, a shop), helping them unpaid as their representative to other sponsors and funders (finding money, answering offers, reading contracts, planning the seasons), or both. Takes no payment from the athlete for any of it. Experienced as a coach and, where they have one, as the owner of a small business; not a lawyer, an accountant or a licensed agent. The athlete may be a child. Reads in order (each unit assumes the ones before it), reads on a phone, checks the citations, and wants genuine depth rather than a primer. Address the reader as 'you'. A parent or an athlete doing the same work is a second reader: where their position differs from the coach's, say so in a sentence. This course is written to be shared with anyone; it is an exception to the Learning Project's single-reader rule (decided 21 Nov 2026). Nothing about the Project's owner, his sport, his club or his athletes appears in it. Frame: the reader wears up to three hats for the same athlete (coach, sponsor, representative), all of them meant to help; the course treats the overlap as a conflict of interest that good intentions do not remove, to be declared and managed in writing from AC2 on. Working unpaid removes one conflict (a commission) and leaves the others: the commercial interest of the coach's business in the athlete's image, the power a coach holds over an athlete who is asked to say yes, and fairness to the athletes who are not sponsored. Teach no coaching, psychology or audience-building method: the reader has their craft; Sport Psychology for Coaches holds the athlete's mind and the coach's line with minors, and the public personal-brand course holds how an audience is built on a platform; point to them by title and do not re-teach them. Teach what an athlete has to sell, who pays and why, how the money is found, asked for, contracted and kept, which rules bind it, when a professional takes over, and what the career is planned around. The sponsor's side is taught from the sponsor's seat: what a business buys and how it judges what it bought; in AC9 that seat is the reader's own. Rules: the course never states a rule as the reader's. A rule of a federation, an event, a school system, an anti-doping body or a state is given as that body's own text, read on its own page and dated, as one example of its kind, followed by the question the reader must put to their own federation, event or authority. Say which sport and which country a rule belongs to; never present one as universal. Portugal layer: each unit that treats rules, money or law (AC2 to AC5, AC8, AC9, AC11 to AC14) carries one section headed 'In Portugal', which works the same questions on Portuguese sources (the Diário da República text, the IPDJ, the Comité Olímpico de Portugal, the federation, the Autoridade Tributária, the Segurança Social, the CPCJ), each read on its issuing body's page and dated, labelled as one country's worked example and never as the general rule; DD-AC3 re-reads and assembles them. Money: no fee, rate, commission or 'typical deal' is stated unless a named source published it, with whose figure it is, the sample, the year and the currency; where no public figure exists the unit says so. Every case figure is fictional and marked. Not advice: the unit says once, near its top, that it is information and not legal, tax, financial or medical advice; a legal or tax point is put as a question with the body or professional to consult; nothing advises on investing money. Minors: where the athlete is under 18 the commercial question is a safeguarding question first; from AC5 on, every unit closes its body with a short section 'If the athlete is under 18' saying what changes, by reference to AC4; no unit proposes for a minor a sponsor in alcohol, gambling, supplements or weight management, a sexualised presentation, or the publication of where the child lives, studies or trains. Anti-doping: no supplement is recommended, and a supplement sponsor is treated as an anti-doping risk. Real people and companies: no real athlete, coach or family is named as an example of hardship, exploitation, bankruptcy or failure unless they said it themselves in a cited source or a court or public inquiry names them, and then only as that source states it; no real agent, agency or sponsor is assessed, ranked or recommended; a real company is named only to attribute a dated public statement or rule to it. Nothing teaches how to get around an eligibility or advertising rule: ambush marketing is described as a risk to the athlete, never as a method. Evidence grades: every claim that carries a number, a mechanism or a recommendation carries one grade from the key set in AC1: research (peer-reviewed work, with its sample and design), rule (a rule-maker's or legislature's own text, for what it says, dated), data (a funding body's, statistics office's or event organiser's own published figures; a survey with its sample stated), interested party (an agency, a sponsor, a platform, a vendor, an advocacy group, or an athlete speaking of their own case), practice (a textbook's or practitioner's account of how the work is done, with no measurement behind it). The research in this field is mostly about famous athletes and large sponsorships: a finding applied to an athlete nobody has heard of is labelled as the unit's inference. Common claims: where a claim is common in sport and its support is thin, name the claim and say what the evidence shows; the course's list of such claims is in the seed bibliography, each with the unit that tests it; say 'no independent source found' where none was. Sports: examples come from at least three kinds of sport (a combat or weight-class sport, track and field or swimming, a racket or tour sport, a small-team sport), and no single sport supplies more than a third of them. Running case: two fictional athletes and their coaches carry the course: Sara, 20, 400 metres, and Dani, 14, judo; Milan, 17, football, appears only where a unit needs the contrast with a sport that has licensed agents. They are the athletes of Sport Psychology for Coaches; their facts come only from courses/athlete-career/notes/case-facts.md, which agrees with that course's register; the fictional sponsors, clubs and agents come from the same file; label each '(fictional)' on first use in the unit; a fact the unit invents about them is listed in the notes so that it can be added to the register; the case is never evidence for a claim about the world. Deliverables are built twice: in full on the case the brief names, then as a template the reader fills for their own athlete, each blank saying what goes in it. Every unit ends its body, before the deliverable, with: 'Check before you act' (the bodies to ask and the documents to open in the reader's own sport and country, each as a question); then, from AC5 on, 'If the athlete is under 18'; then 'Self-check' (five to eight questions a sponsor, a federation officer or a lawyer would expect the reader to answer, each with a short answer drawn from the unit's sourced text). Tone: direct, no flattery, no filler, no motivational language, no 'not X but Y' constructions. Course goal: {GOAL} Sourcing standard: every claim that carries a number, a mechanism or a recommendation is sourced where it is made; acceptable sources are peer-reviewed research and systematic reviews; position and consensus statements of the ISSP, the IOC and bodies of the same kind, labelled as consensus; standard textbooks cited by edition and chapter; a rule-maker's own text on its own page (a federation, an event organiser, an Olympic or Paralympic committee, an anti-doping organisation), dated; legislation and official guidance at the issuing body's page (an official journal, a tax authority, a regulator), dated; a court's judgment or press release at the court's own page; funding bodies' and event organisers' own published figures; surveys with their sample and year; an agency's, sponsor's, platform's, vendor's or advocacy group's own page or report, labelled interested party and dated, as evidence of what that party says, offers or requires and never of how things are; an athlete's own published statement about their own case, labelled anecdote; a law firm's note or a press report is never the source for what a law, a rule or a judgment says: it may lead the writer to the text, which is then read and cited itself; videos, podcasts, social-media posts, unsigned blogs, recruiting-service and lead-generation sites, and press rewrites of a release are not sources, and may be named only as the claim under examination; every reference is opened before it is cited: found by a search, read at its publisher's page, its DOI address or the issuing body's own page, and recorded in the unit's source log with the address, what was read (abstract, summary or full text) and the date; a claim that rests on an abstract alone says so; a reference that could not be opened carries no claim; the seed bibliography (courses/athlete-career/notes/bibliography.md) is a starting list whose entries were checked for existence only, never an authority on what they say; a study's finding is stated with its sample, its setting and its design, and a correlation is called a correlation; a text in Portuguese is cited in Portuguese with the article number, and what it says is given in English as the unit's translation; no uncited figures; no invented names, companies, studies, quotations or numbers; if a claim cannot be sourced, say so and leave it out of the deliverable. A unit that reads as advice without evidence is a failed unit. Links are given as links (the DOI address for a paper), so the course bibliography can collect them. Figures: where a section's content has a shape, declare it as a fenced viz block of a kind kit/viz.py draws (one to four a unit is a guide; none where nothing has a shape); a figure adds no fact, and its caption says what it shows and where its data comes from. Where the unit depends on current facts (rule texts, laws, funding programmes and amounts, prize schedules, regulators' guidance, court rulings), search before writing and date the facts. End with the unit's deliverable, built out in full, not described.

## AC1

Unit AC1, The job, the map and the evidence. Search before writing. Cover: what managing a career consists of when no agency does it: the functions one by one (representation and negotiation, sponsorship and commercial work, funding and money administration, calendar and logistics, contracts and legal questions, media and public presence, welfare, education and the career after sport), from the sports-agent literature and from regulators' own definitions of an agent or representative, read and dated; who performs each function at the top of a sport and who performs it for an athlete nobody represents; the people around the athlete (coach, parent, club, federation, sponsor, agent, lawyer, accountant) and what each owes the athlete; the roles this course's reader holds beside coaching (sponsor, unpaid representative), as a map only (AC2 takes them up); the sources of an athlete's income, as categories only (AC5 takes them up); what an athlete in a sport with little money can and cannot expect, from published surveys of athletes' incomes, each with its sample and who ran it; the kinds of evidence in this field (peer-reviewed sport-management and marketing research, rule texts, funders' and organisers' figures, surveys, agency and platform material, athletes' own accounts, textbooks) and what each can and cannot show; why the stories of athletes who made it mislead; the five evidence grades, defined so that two readers would give the same grade, including how to grade a survey run by an advocacy group, a figure one source repeats from another, and a public authority's summary of a law it administers; the common claims on the course's list, each stated as it circulates, set against the evidence this unit opens, and marked with the unit that tests it. Deliverable: a function map (function, who does it now, who should, what it costs in time or money, what goes wrong if nobody does it), built in full for Sara and for Dani; the grade key every later unit uses, with one worked example per grade; a working glossary, which later units extend; then the function map as a template.

## AC2

Unit AC2, Three hats: coach, sponsor, representative. Search before writing. Cover: what a conflict of interest is, why good intentions and working unpaid do not remove one, and what the standard remedies are (declare it, step back from the decision, give the other person independent advice, put it in writing), from the literature on conflicts of interest and from sport bodies' own ethics and conflict rules, read on their pages and dated; the three roles and what each owes the athlete; coach and sponsor at once: where the interests of a coach's business and of the athlete part (the athlete's image and results working for the brand; exclusivity that keeps a larger sponsor away; the athlete who cannot comfortably refuse their coach; the athlete who wants to leave the coach, or the brand, and fears losing both; product given to some athletes in a squad and not to others; selection and attention that follow sponsorship, or appear to; the brand's presence in the club; what other coaches, parents and the federation see), each sourced or labelled as the unit's reasoning; coach and representative at once: speaking for someone you also direct, money that passes through you, your stake in the athlete staying; all three at once: dealing with other sponsors while your own business is one of them; what research and consensus statements say about power and dependence between coach and athlete, with a pointer to Sport Psychology for Coaches for the coach's line with minors; what rule-makers say, on at least three texts read on their own pages and dated (an international federation's or the IOC's rules on conflicts of interest, a coaching body's code of conduct, a federation's rules on representatives), given as examples of what to look for; the arrangements available (declaring the roles to the athlete, the family and the club; criteria published in advance for whom your business sponsors; someone other than you deciding, and someone other than you advising the athlete; no exclusivity, or exclusivity the athlete can leave; the relationships able to end separately), each with what it protects and what it costs; handling money and product (whose account, whose name on the paper, receipts, reporting to the athlete); what changes when the athlete is a child and the parents sign; what would change if you were ever paid; how each role ends without ending the others. In Portugal: who may represent an athlete under the current law on sports representation and intermediation, whether unpaid help with sponsorship falls inside it, and what a federation's and a club's own rules say about a coach's commercial interests, read at the texts, as questions. Deliverable: a roles declaration (each role you hold for this athlete, what you gain from it, what the athlete can refuse without consequence, who else decides, who advises the athlete, how each role ends), built in full for Sara and her coach, whose label supplies her kit, and for Dani's coach with Dani's parents; a written mandate for the unpaid representative work (what you may do in the athlete's name, what needs the athlete's or the parents' signature, how money is handled, the review date, how either side ends it); a conflict register (situation, whose interest, how it is handled, who was told); then all three as templates.

## AC15

Unit AC15, Capstone: the one-year career plan. Cover: what the plan is for and who reads it (the athlete, a parent, you, a sponsor in part); its parts, each drawn from an earlier deliverable named by code (the roles declaration, the mandate and the conflict register; the sponsorship policy and agreement where your own business sponsors the athlete; the rules register; the safeguard protocol where the athlete is a minor; the budget and funding log; the asset inventory and marketability plan; the sponsor pipeline and servicing calendar; the contract questions still open; the representation decision; the dual-career and exit plan); the year on one calendar: competition, study, funding deadlines, sponsor obligations and review dates; the decisions that are the athlete's alone; what the plan refuses; the difference between what is sourced and what is assumed; how the plan is reviewed, and by whom. Then write one plan in full for Sara, and one for Dani in which most commercial lines read 'not yet', each with its reason. The unit says plainly that the plan that matters is the reader's own, built on rules this course could only teach them to look up. Deliverable: two complete worked plans; the plan template, each blank naming the earlier deliverable that fills it; a one-page review checklist.

## DD-AC1

Deep dive AC1, Comparables: documented careers below the agency level. Written after the fifteen core units. Search before writing. Cover: what counts as a documented case here (the athlete's own published account together with an independent record: a results database, a funder's published list, a sponsor's own announcement) and why a press profile alone does not; six to eight athletes from at least four sports and three countries, among them at least one from Portugal and at least one who stopped for lack of money by their own published account; for each: the level, how the career was funded as documented, who did the representing, what is verifiable and what is the athlete's word, graded; what the cases share and what they cannot show (survivorship, selection by visibility); nothing is inferred about a named person beyond the sources. Deliverable: a comparables table (athlete, sport, country, level, income sources as documented, who represented, sources and grades, what transfers to the reader); and a page headed 'What these cases cannot tell you'.

## DD-AC2

Deep dive AC2, Hard numbers: what the money is. Written after the fifteen core units. Search before writing. Cover: prize money from organisers' own schedules in at least four sports, at national and international level; grant and scholarship amounts from the funders' own pages in at least three countries, Portugal among them; surveys of athletes' incomes, each with who ran it, the sample and the year; what is published about the value of sponsorships and at what scale, and the absence of public figures at this level; published rules on commission; the cost side, from federations' or researchers' published figures where they exist; every number with whose it is, the sample, the year, the currency and the date read; what cannot be known from public sources. Deliverable: a numbers sheet (figure, whose, sample, year, currency, grade, date read); an income scenario for Sara built from the sheet's sourced ranges and marked fictional; a list of the numbers the reader must get from their own federation and funder.

## DD-AC3

Deep dive AC3, Portugal, end to end. Written last. Search before writing. Cover: every 'In Portugal' section of AC2 to AC14, re-read at its source and brought up to date; the legal texts in force, read in the Diário da República's consolidated version (sports representation and intermediation; high-performance sport and post-career support; minors in advertising; the patronage regime after its 2026 change; the right to one's image); the bodies and what each decides (the IPDJ, the Comité Olímpico and the Comité Paralímpico de Portugal, the federation, the CPCJ, the Autoridade Tributária, the Segurança Social, the advertising regulator); the Olympic preparation programme: its levels, grants and obligations as published; one federation in an Olympic sport followed through its own regulations on advertising, national-team agreements and representatives; the open questions that a professional must answer, listed; every correction this deep dive makes to an earlier unit's Portugal section, raised as a correction under the carry-forward rule. Deliverable: a Portugal register in AC3's and AC5's forms (body, text, article, what it governs, date read, the question for a professional), built for a coach in Portugal who represents an athlete like Sara and a minor like Dani; a year calendar of Portuguese deadlines as published; a list of who to ask for each open question.
