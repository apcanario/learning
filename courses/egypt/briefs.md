# Egyptian history and mythology · briefs

The shared preamble (`{GOAL}` is filled with the course goal) and one brief per module. `kit/pack.py` reads every `## ` section; nothing above the first is read. A brief is used as written and never reworded by a writing chat.

## Preamble

Write one module of a self-study course in the long-form report format of Personal Mythology: a long-form report in Markdown, in the forms of `kit/AGENT-SPEC.md` (6 to 10 `## ` sections, dispute boxes opening `**Where sources disagree.**`, `## Self-test` with 10 questions and answers, `## Carry forward` with exactly 3 ideas). Length is whatever the topic needs (2,500 to 5,000 words is the guide); do not pad and do not cut to a target. Quality over quantity: a shorter module whose every claim was opened and checked is better than a longer one. Reader: an educated adult who wants genuine depth; self-directed study, built so that the gaps get filled in the right order and nothing is taken on trust; reads in order, each module assuming the ones before it, and this course assuming the Greek and Roman courses before it; one or two modules a sitting, on a phone; takes the Self-test before moving on; treats a module as a study text, not scripture: the citations are there so he can check them. Frame: Egypt is told from Egyptian evidence first and from Greek and Roman observers second; every statement says what kind of evidence carries it and how far that evidence stands from the event. Tone: plain, direct prose; British spelling; no em dashes; no preamble, no summary of what is about to be said, no closing pleasantries, no flattery, no motivational language. Course goal: {GOAL} Sourcing standard: every claim that carries a date, a number, a name, an attribution or an interpretation is sourced where it is made. Every reference is opened before it is cited: found by a search, read at its publisher's page, its DOI address, an open-access repository or the holding institution's own page, and logged in the source log with the address opened, what was read (full text, chapter, abstract or catalogue record) and the date; a reference that could not be opened carries no claim. An Egyptian text is cited by its standard designation (Pyramid Texts by utterance and section, Coffin Texts by spell, Book of the Dead by chapter, a papyrus, stela or ostracon by its name or its museum number) and by the published translation or edition that was opened; an object by its museum and accession number or its excavation number. A classical author is cited by book and chapter for what that author says, with the century he wrote in. Acceptable modern sources: peer-reviewed journal articles; monographs and edited volumes from academic presses; the UCLA Encyclopedia of Egyptology; excavation reports and the pages of the mission or institute that dug; museum collection records; peer-reviewed scientific studies, with their sample and method. Never sources: videos, documentaries, podcasts, social-media posts, unsigned web pages, general encyclopaedias and wikis, travel and tour sites, press reports of a discovery, popular books by non-specialists, and any translation by E. A. Wallis Budge; one may be named only as the claim under examination. A discovery known only from a ministry or mission announcement is graded announced and dated. A modern scholar is named for a view only from that scholar's own opened text, or from a peer-reviewed review or encyclopaedia article that reports it, named as the reporter. Every claim carries a grade from the key that module EG1 sets (attested, later tradition, consensus, contested, scientific, announced, conjecture). Every date BC before 664 BC is given as approximate and names the chronology it follows. Every number names whose figure it is. A claim that cannot be sourced is said to be unsourced or left out. Nothing is quoted that was not read; a quotation is under 15 words and names its translator. Where the module depends on current facts (the state of an excavation, a scan, a museum display, a restitution claim), search before writing and date the facts. Pointers: another module of this course is "module EG12"; the Greek course is "Greek module 6.1" and the Roman course "Rome module 5.5"; open the target before pointing and do not retell it. End with the module's deliverable, built out in full, not described.

## EG1

Module EG1, How we know anything: the sources and the evidence key. Cover: the Egyptian record by kind: royal annals and king lists (the Palermo Stone and its fragments, the Abydos list of Seti I, the Saqqara tablet, the Turin Canon), what each includes and leaves out, and why; monumental inscriptions and the rhetoric of kingship, read as claims and not as reports; administrative papyri and ostraca; private tomb autobiographies; the archaeological record and its biases (stone tombs and temples at the desert edge against mud-brick towns under the floodplain, the Delta against the Valley); Manetho: who he was, when he wrote, that his work survives only in later excerpts and epitomes (Josephus, Africanus, Eusebius, Syncellus), and what his thirty dynasties are and are not; the Greek and Roman observers (Herodotus book 2, Hecataeus of Abdera through Diodorus book 1, Strabo book 17, Plutarch), each with his date and what he could have known; the making of Egyptology in outline (the French expedition and the Description, decipherment, Mariette, Petrie and sequence dating, Reisner) and the colonial setting in which collections were formed; the scientific methods (radiocarbon, CT, ancient DNA, residue analysis) and their limits; where the open, reliable material is today (the UCLA Encyclopedia of Egyptology, the Thesaurus Linguae Aegyptiae, museum collection databases, mission pages). Then the method: the seven evidence grades of this course, each defined so that two readers would give the same grade, with one worked Egyptian example each. End the body with the main traps: Manetho's dynasties as real units, a royal inscription as reportage, Herodotus as eyewitness to the pyramids' building, Budge as a translation, and a press announcement as a result. Not here: decipherment in detail (EG53), chronology (EG3). Deliverable: the grade key as a one-page table (grade, definition, test, worked example), which every later module uses; then the Self-test and Carry forward.

Not here: the predynastic cultures (EG4). Deliverable: a north-to-south gazetteer table (site, ancient name, what is there, the modules that use it); then the Self-test and Carry forward.

Cover: the question stated exactly: about how many blocks, of what weights, to what heights, in how many years, with whose figures and how the rate is calculated; the evidence on the ground: ramp remains at Meidum, Lisht, Giza and the unfinished pyramids, and construction ramps at Karnak's first pylon; the proposals one by one, each with its author, the evidence for it and the objection to it: the single straight ramp, the spiral ramp, the zigzag ramp on one face, the internal ramp, levering and rocking, combinations by height; the heaviest blocks (the granite beams above the King's Chamber) as a separate problem; what experiments have shown (the NOVA pyramid, hauling trials) and their limits; what would settle it. No proposal is presented as established: each is graded. Classical accounts (Herodotus 2.125 on "machines"; Diodorus 1.63 on mounds) are quoted as later tradition. Deliverable: a comparison table of the ramp proposals (proponent, source, evidence for, objection, grade); then the Self-test and Carry forward.
