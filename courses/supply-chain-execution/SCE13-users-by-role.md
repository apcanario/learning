# SCE13 · Users by role

SCE5, SCE8 and SCE9 each wrote role cards for one system: the warehouse floor, the transport office, the order desk. This unit lays the cards side by side, groups the people on them by how their work reaches them, and names the conflicts that stand between them because each is measured on something different. Grades follow SCE1's key (section 10 there): **independent**, **analyst**, **vendor-reported**, **practice**. A line that rests on no opened source is a **working description**: the course's own account, graded nothing, never used as evidence. ## 2. Four working patterns, and why each role sits where it does

A **working pattern** in this unit is how work reaches a person and what sets its pace: who or what starts the next piece of work, where the person is when it arrives, and what they are judged against. The brief names four patterns. Each is defined here by that test, and every placement below is this unit's reading of the cards' fields 3 to 7.

**Task-driven on a device.** The system, or a counterpart's system, issues the next piece of work; the person confirms it, usually by a scan, a spoken check or a button; the pace is set by a standard or a deadline. This is the pattern SCE5 §3 describes for the handheld, voice and pick-to-light (working descriptions there), and the one Bartholdi and Hackman's picks per person-hour measures (§3.3, as SCE5 read it) [practice]. Mina is the plain case. Freya works the same way in a store: the order system sends a web order, and she packs it or declines it by the carrier's collection time (SCE9 card 2). The carrier representative is task-driven at a desk: each tender arrives as a piece of work to accept or refuse, an X12 204 answered by a 990, or the same exchange through a portal or by phone (SCE8 card 3, §5). Placing a desk role here is deliberate: the pattern is in how the work arrives, and the device is secondary.

**Exception-driven at a desk.** An **exception** is any case where the record and the world disagree, or where a step did not happen when it should: a short pick, a refused delivery, an invoice that does not match, an order that has not moved. The person's work is a queue of these, and the clean cases never reach them. Carla holds the role "whose job is the system being wrong" (SCE5 §1.3). Nuno takes the day's calls from drivers and customers (SCE8 card 2); Priya's invoices that match pass, and the ones that do not become her work (SCE8 card 4, §5's reading that a 210 can be matched automatically); Jonas's contacts follow every broken promise (SCE9 card 1); Lena works a list of stuck orders (SCE9 card 3); Olu answers clients who ask "before Tandric knows" (SCE8 card 5). Ilse is here too, at the floor's edge: she watches the route backlog and clears shorts (SCE5 card 2).

**Rule-setting in configuration.** **Configuration** is changing a system's behaviour through settings and rules the vendor supports, without changing its code (SCE6 §4). The person in this pattern changes the rules other people's work runs on, at intervals, and sees the effect only afterwards in others' measures. Oskar is the one carded case: he sets availability buffers, sourcing order and store caps before each season and before peak (SCE9 card 4 [working]); Gartner's definition of distributed order management has those rules set by "configuration of business rules in a hierarchical manner" (Gartner Peer Insights DOM page, as SCE9 read it) [analyst]. At Tandric, Luis configures the system for each new client [register]; his card is SCE14's.

**Watching and deciding as a manager.** The person reads others' measures in reports, and acts by moving people, money or rules. Marek is the carded case: he staffs the peak, sets standards and any bonus scheme, and approves workarounds (SCE5 card 5 [working]). Ilse watches and decides at shift level, and Oskar and Lena watch the effects of rules and exceptions.

**The planner fits none of the four well.** Sofia builds a plan once a day in a fixed window, between the late-afternoon cut-off and the evening start of picking, and then handles the changes to it (SCE5 card 4; SCE8 card 1). Building a plan is neither an issued task, an exception nor a rule. This unit marks it as its own column in the figure below and places her second pattern at the desk with the exceptions, where her re-plans belong. Priya, who plans each load before tendering it (SCE8 §3.1), takes the same mark. Whether the course should name planning as a fifth pattern is a question for the notes.

The figure draws the placements: "main" is the pattern that takes most of the person's day in the card's field 5, "also" a second pattern the card shows.

Three things recur, as this unit's reading of the table. The task-driven ask for fewer steps on the exception path, because their measure counts the clean path. The exception desks ask to see another system's state on their own screen: Sofia the warehouse's, Olu and Jonas the carrier's, Lena the location's reason. That is SCE10's seams seen from the desk; SCE10 §1 to §5 trace each handoff. The rule setters ask for a test before the change and a record after it.

**Inside one product, the same event means different things.** One short pick at Corvane shows it. When Mina reports a short on a web order, the order system can re-source the missing line to a store, where Freya ships it, and the customer receives two parcels (SCE4 §9, a worked chain, that unit's inference [register]); Jonas answers customers about items missing from web orders [register, SCE4]; Carla counts locations after short picks [register, SCE4 assumption]. Inside the warehouse system alone, the one transaction is lost time against Mina's standard, a discrepancy for Carla to count, and a line that misses Tobias's outbound shift; in the order system, it is a split for Lena's rate and a contact for Jonas's. Each card's field 2 decides what the same record is for that person.

## 4. The standing conflicts between roles

A **standing conflict** in this unit is a disagreement between two roles that recurs because each is measured on something the other's work moves: it is built into the measures, so it outlasts any one incident. Each is stated with what each side is measured on (the cards' field 2). How products commonly settle it is stated from the course's capability lists where one names the capability, and otherwise as a working description; no source opened measures how often any settlement is used or whether it works.

**K1 · Speed against control.** On one side, the people measured on throughput by a deadline: Mina on picks per person-hour, Ilse on routes picked complete by departure, Freya on web orders packed by collection time. On the other, the person measured on the record being right: Carla on inventory accuracy. The freight desk at Tandric shows the same shape on money: invoices are disputed when activity was not recorded [register], and Priya is measured on invoice match rate (card 8); K6 and K8 take that side up. Two experiments the filed units opened measured both speed and errors. The voice-picking one (101 participants) found that higher productivity "appears to be possible without sacrificing quality" (de Vries, de Koster & Stam, [2016, IJPR](https://doi.org/10.1080/00207543.2015.1064184), abstract, as SCE5 §3 read it) [independent: one experiment]; the incentive one found speed and errors moving apart in one picking method. In de Vries, de Koster and Stam's working paper, read in full by SCE5, zone pickers under a cooperation-based team bonus picked more order lines in the 10-minute recorded run than under a competition-based individual bonus (least-squares means of 47.58 against 35.37), and 31.6% of their orders had an error against 19.1% ([2015](https://repub.eur.nl/pub/78365/ERS-2015-009-LIS.pdf), as SCE5 §4 read it) [independent: 142 participants, a warehouse built for research]. The published article's abstract reports "No effects on order picking quality were identified" ([2016](https://doi.org/10.1111/poms.12547), abstract, as SCE5 read it) [independent], so the two versions disagree, and whether the published full text keeps the error rates was not checked (SCE5 unsure 2). A working site with full shifts is a different setting (SCE5 §4). On control, the internal-control standard SCE5 opened lists separating "the responsibilities for authorizing transactions, processing and recording them, reviewing the transactions" and physical control over inventories ([GAO-25-107721](https://www.gao.gov/products/gao-25-107721), para. 10.04, read by SCE5 in a third-party copy) [independent, for what the text says]; applying it to a warehouse is SCE5's inference. **How products commonly settle it:** cycle counting and adjustments are a warehouse capability (SCE6 W12: Bartholdi and Hackman [practice]; Gartner glossary [analyst]); approvals above a value or quantity limit, with every adjustment logged by user, are SCE4 §5's working arrangement, unsourced.

**K2 · The operator against the inventory controller.** The sharpest case of K1 is the short pick. Mina's rate counts the picks she completes, so time spent reporting a short or searching a mixed location is time against her standard (SCE5 card 1 [working]; the effect of hard search on pick times fell with experience in Batt and Gallino's study, [2019](https://doi.org/10.1287/mnsc.2018.3059), abstract, as SCE3 §9 read it) [independent]. Carla needs the short reported where it happened, while the error is fresh, and needs the adjustment that follows made by someone other than the picker (SCE5 card 3). The ECR report SCE4 read names manual adjustments as a cause of record error [practice: a list of causes] and, in one retailer's data, found discrepancies following them ([Rekik, Syntetos & Glock, 2019](https://orca.cardiff.ac.uk/142664/1/84486%20ECR%20Inventory%20Inaccuracy%20Report.pdf), as SCE4 §5 read it) [independent: one retailer's data]. **How products commonly settle it:** a short-pick entry that raises a count of the location (SCE4 §5's list of triggers a warehouse system can raise; that unit's inference, untested); Mina's card asks as well for a rate that counts the work done on shorts [working].

**K3 · The planner against the floor.** Sofia is measured on routes built by the time picking starts, vehicles used of the 40 and vehicle fill; Ilse on routes picked complete by departure (cards 4 and 2). A late order makes Sofia re-plan, and when she changes a route after picking has begun, Ilse sees it first at staging and moves cages (SCE10 deliverable B row 7 [register, working]). Sofia's change improves her fill and costs Ilse's completion. **How products commonly settle it:** Sofia's card asks for the warehouse's progress by route on her screen and a warning when a change hits a route already being picked [working]; no capability line in SCE6 or SCE8 names a cut-off past which a released route is frozen, so the course has no sourced settlement (working).

**K4 · The rule setter against the store.** A second form of the planner against the floor, across two products. Oskar is measured on outbound shipping cost per order, split rate and promise accuracy; Freya on web orders packed by collection time, store rejection rate and pickup ready time (cards 13 and 11). A rule that sends orders to the nearest or cheapest store sends work to a shop floor that is also serving customers. Hübner, Holzapfel and Kuhn found that store fulfilment adds handling effort and needs space and real-time store inventory (2016, p. 279, [full text](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40685-016-0034-7), as SCE9 read it) [independent: an exploratory study of 28 omni-channel retailers headquartered in German-speaking countries, non-food]. **How products commonly settle it:** the order of locations, set in the rule hierarchy Gartner's definition describes (as SCE9 read it) [analyst]; store caps, a working capability line (SCE9 O18) [working]; Freya's decline, which re-sources the order (SCE9 card 2) [working].

**K5 · The site against head office: Corvane.** Marek is measured on cost per unit handled, on-time and accurate shipments, injuries and staff turnover (card 5 [working]); Edda, vice-president supply chain, answers for the centre's cost and service and for the web promise (SCE11 buyer card 1 [working]). Two of the register's known troubles sit between them: web orders promised from stock the centre cannot find, and store staff asked to ship web orders with no process for it [register]. The promise is made by rules set away from the site (Oskar's, at Corvane), and the cost of a broken promise lands on the site's shorts and the stores' declines. **How products commonly settle it:** availability buffers on the promise (SCE9 card 4) [working]; no source opened describes how products divide this decision between a site and head office.

**K6 · The site against head office: Tandric.** At a logistics provider the head office that sets the rules is commercial. Karin signs client contracts and is accountable for their margin; Anke runs the one shared site, and her site records the activity clients are billed for (SCE11 Tandric buyer cards 1 and 2 [working]). Each client wants its own rules and reports, a new client is expected live within weeks of signature, and invoices are disputed when activity was not recorded [register]. Luis, who configures each new client [register], stands between them; his card is SCE14's. **How products commonly settle it:** billing clients by activity is a warehouse capability (SCE6 W26: Gartner 2022, "Third-party logistics (3PL) billing" [analyst]); configurability is another (W29: Bartholdi and Hackman [practice]); whether a product lets one client's rules be set without touching another's is not in any source opened (working).

**K7 · The dispatcher against the warehouse.** Nuno is measured on on-time delivery and refused goods per route; Ilse on routes complete by departure (cards 6 and 2). Substitutions are agreed by phone and not recorded, and drivers return with refused goods [register]; Ilse sends a short or a substitute and tells Nuno (SCE5 card 2 [working]). A substitute that keeps Ilse's route complete can become a refusal on Nuno's. **How products commonly settle it:** a substitution recorded at the pick and a refusal entered at the door (cards 2 and 6, field 8) [working]; dispatch and real-time routing are mandatory features of Gartner's vehicle routing market (as SCE8 §2 read it) [analyst], which says nothing about substitutions.

**K8 · The freight auditor against the carrier.** Priya is measured on invoice match rate and client invoices disputed; the carrier representative, by the party tendering, on acceptance and on-time pickup and delivery, and by the carrier on nothing any source states (cards 8 and 7). ## 5. Use that is not a choice: what the research on mandated technology says

**Mandated use** is use of a system the job requires, as against voluntary adoption (SCE11's glossary line). Every card in this unit is mandated use: Mina's picks come to her on the handheld, Priya's invoices reach her through the transport system, and the carrier representative answers each customer in the channel that customer tenders in (cards 1, 8 and 7; working). The research the filed units opened on it is small, old, and set outside logistics.

- **Brown, Massey, Montoya-Weiss and Burkman** note that technology acceptance research, which finds usefulness and ease of use the primary drivers of intentions to adopt, "has been conducted primarily in environments in which adoption was voluntary", argue that the relationships differ when use is mandated, and report one field study in the banking industry that found such differences ([2002](https://doi.org/10.1057/palgrave.ejis.3000438), abstract, as SCE11 §5 read it) [independent]. Setting: banking. The abstract gives no sample size and does not say which relationships differed (SCE11).
- **Venkatesh and Davis** tested an extended acceptance model on four systems at four organisations (N = 156), two with voluntary use and two with mandatory, measured before implementation and one and three months after; social influence and cognitive factors, among them job relevance, output quality and perceived ease of use, both significantly influenced acceptance ([2000](https://doi.org/10.1287/mnsc.46.2.186.11926), abstract, as SCE11 read it) [independent]. Setting: the abstract does not give the organisations' industries.
- **He and King's** meta-analysis of 82 empirical studies found user participation "minimally-to-moderately beneficial" to systems development, more for attitudes and behaviour than for productivity ([2008](https://jmis-web.org/articles/493), abstract, as SCE11 read it) [independent]. Setting: systems being developed; SCE11 notes that buying a package is a different case.
- **Alter's** theory treats workarounds as a process with its own causes, and the abstract says the theory can help explain compliance and non-compliance with mandates and inform systems analysis and design ([2014](https://doi.org/10.17705/1CAIS.03455), abstract, as SCE10 §11 read it) [independent]. A **workaround** is a way of getting the work done outside the prescribed process or system (SCE10's glossary line).
- **Van de Weerd, Vollers, Beerepoot and Fantinato** observed and interviewed 12 team managers in four stores of one Dutch supermarket chain from March to June 2018, analysed its prescribed processes, and validated the 29 workarounds they found with a survey of 292 team managers across the Netherlands. Among them: "Using a shadow administration for restocking planning" (159 respondents confirmed it), using another app to find the product number needed in the order app (236), "Still using the previous, outdated platform" (247), and "Needing private email to access the cargo tracker" (6) ([2019](https://aisel.aisnet.org/ecis2019_rp/183), full text in the Utrecht repository copy, pp. 5–6, as SCE10 §11 read it) [independent]. Setting: supermarket stores and their team managers.

**What follows for people who cannot decline the system**, as this unit's inference from these five and from the cards. A person who cannot decline still forms a view of the system, and the acceptance studies say that view works differently when use is required (Brown et al.). Where the work still has to be done, the retail study shows what people do beside the system: a second record kept beside the system, a second tool used to feed the first, the old system kept in use (van de Weerd et al.). For the thirteen cards, the case register already holds such records: substitutions agreed by phone and not recorded, paper delivery notes on some routes, client reports built by hand [register; SCE8 card 5]. They are the case's, and prove nothing about the world. What a product team can take from the research is a question to ask on site, which the closing section gives: where is the second record, and which of the person's measures does it protect? That the second record protects a measure is this unit's inference from section 4; no study opened tests it.

### 5.1 Common claim C14: "Users will work around any system they dislike"

**The claim as it circulates.** Users will work around any system they dislike (seed bibliography, common claims).

**What the opened sources show, and in which setting.**

- **That workarounds are common beside a prescribed system:** shown in one setting. The supermarket study found 29 workarounds in four stores of one chain and had them confirmed by up to 247 of 292 surveyed team managers (van de Weerd et al., as SCE10 read it) [independent]. The setting is retail stores in one country; the study as SCE10 reports it observed and surveyed team managers only, and it does not measure whether anyone disliked the systems.
- **That dislike causes workarounds:** not shown. No filed unit opened a study that measures users' view of a system and their workarounds together. Alter's theory gives workarounds causes of their own (abstract) [independent]; it is a theory, and the abstract as SCE10 reports it describes no test of it.
- **That mandated users' views shape how they use the system:** shown in banking and in four unnamed organisations, in abstracts only (Brown et al.; Venkatesh and Davis) [independent]. Neither abstract reports workarounds.
- **That it holds for "any" system, or in warehouses and transport:** no study in either setting was opened by any filed unit (flag F121), and the universal "any" has no support in anything opened.

**The course's verdict on C14: not tested in the setting.** The one workaround study opened is in supermarket stores; it shows workarounds widespread there and does not test dislike as their cause; the universal form is unsupported. A workaround study set in warehouses or in transport is not searched here and is a Need (notes). SCE1's C14 row, "Not searched here", is corrected by the notes' replacement.

*Grades in this section:* independent (Brown et al., Venkatesh and Davis and He and King, abstracts, as SCE11 read them; Alter, abstract, and van de Weerd et al., full text, as SCE10 read them); the inferences and the verdict are the course's, resting on those five.

## 7. What public peer reviews say, read by market and by reviewer role

A **peer-review site** publishes ratings and comments by people who say they use a product; a **review market** is the site's page for one kind of software, listing the products reviewed in it. The brief asks what reviews of this kind of software say by market and by reviewer role, with the number of reviews. No filed unit read a number of reviews for any market or any reviewer role, so this unit reports what such a count would rest on and says nothing about what reviewers say.

**What the filed units read on the market pages.** Gartner's Peer Insights warehouse market listed 140 products on 21 and 24 November 2026 and showed no total count of reviews on 21 November (SCE6 does not report the count) ([WMS market page](https://www.gartner.com/reviews/market/warehouse-management-systems?months=12), as SCE1 §7 and SCE6 §2 read it) [analyst]; its transport market listed 92 products on 27 November 2026, showed no total count of reviews, and carried a count of ratings on each product's listing ([TMS market page](https://www.gartner.com/reviews/market/transportation-management-systems), as SCE8 §9 read it) [analyst] (flag F10).

**How the reviewers come to be there.** Gartner lets vendors invite their own customers to review through a custom link, asks that any incentive be nominal (under $25) and offered to all invited customers, and itself gives the first 50 approved reviews a $25 gift card ([Review Sourcing](https://blogs.gartner.com/reviews-pages/tpt-faq/review-sourcing/), as SCE1 §7 read it) [analyst]. G2 requires at least 10 reviews in a category for a product to appear on its Grid and lets vendors ask their customers for reviews ([Research Scoring Methodologies](https://documentation.g2.com/docs/research-scoring-methodologies), as SCE12 §3 read it) [vendor-reported]. Because a vendor can choose whom to invite, the course grades what reviewers say vendor-reported (SCE1 §7).

**Where a count by market and by role would be read (flag F10).** On the pages the filed units read, no market page gives a total; a count by market would be the sum of the product listings' own counts on one date, and a count by role would need the site to show each reviewer's role, which SCE8 §9 says a market page gives only "where the page shows them". A writer with search on states the date, the page, how the count was made (summed by the writer, or shown by the site) and the role filter used; this unit's notes carry that as a Need. On 28 November 2026 the quality review read G2's warehouse management category (page 2, last updated 6 October 2026): it gave "Total Products under this Category: 680" under "Category Stats (Oct 2026)", and "9,700+ Authentic Reviews", which the page does not label as the category's total; breakdowns by company size and industry appeared only on each product's listing, and none by role ([G2, warehouse management category](https://www.g2.com/categories/warehouse-management?page=2)) [vendor-reported]. No count by reviewer role was found.

**What such a count could and could not show** (SCE8 §9; SCE1 §7). It could show what a counted number of reviewers of a stated kind say about products in a market. It could not show how a product performs, which buyer a product suits, or any ranking, and in this course it never names a product.
