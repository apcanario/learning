# SCE11 · The buying committee

SCE5 followed the people who work in a warehouse. This unit follows the people who decide which software those people will use. Grades follow SCE1's key (section 10 there): **independent**, **analyst**, **vendor-reported**, **practice**. As in SCE5, a line that rests on no opened source is called a **working description**: the course's own account, graded nothing, never used as evidence. Case facts are marked [register]; facts this unit adds to the case are in the notes.

Two limits hold through the unit. The research on organisational buying was built in the 1960s to 1980s on industrial purchases, not on software (section 1). And every study of software buying opened here was read in abstract only, except Olson et al. (2012), read in full for its report of an earlier study; what a study found beyond its abstract is not stated.

## 1. The buying centre: where the idea comes from, and how old it is

A **buying centre** (in US spelling, buying center) is the set of people in a customer organisation who take part in one purchase. In sales talk the same group is called the **buying committee** or **buying group**; this course uses "buying committee" for the people in one software selection.

**The roles.** A 2024 editorial in *Industrial Marketing Management* by Ehret, Johnston and Ritter states that "Buyers play a diverse set of specific roles" and names influencers, gatekeepers, buyers, users and decision-makers; it adds that external actors can complement or substitute for these roles ([Ehret, Johnston & Ritter, 2024](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.indmarman.2023.12.020), abstract, parts of the introduction and section snippets read) [practice: an editorial's account of the literature, with no measurement]. Glossed for this course:

- **Users**: the people who will work with what is bought.
- **Influencers**: people whose views shape the choice, often by setting or checking requirements.
- **Buyers**: the people with formal authority to deal with suppliers and sign the order; in most companies, the purchasing or procurement function.
- **Decision-makers** (deciders): the people who choose.
- **Gatekeepers**: the people who control what information and which suppliers reach the others.

The glosses are working descriptions; the editorial read names the roles without defining them in the part shown.

**The age.** The same editorial credits Robinson, Faris and Wind (1967) and Webster and Wind (1972) among the seminal works, says they were "published more than 45 years ago", and cites an earlier study of 1956 by Cyert, Simon and Trow on decision-making in a computer purchase (Ehret et al. 2024) [practice]. Webster and Wind's article, *A General Model for Understanding Organizational Buying Behavior* (*Journal of Marketing* 36(2), 1972), is, in this unit's working description, the usual source of the five roles; its publisher's page failed when this unit tried to open it on 28 November 2026, so nothing here rests on it directly.

**The setting.** The first measurements were of industrial purchases. Johnston and Bonoma's study of the buying centre examined group composition and interaction in purchases of capital equipment and of industrial services in 31 firms, treated the buying centre as a communications network, and found that the measures differed between equipment and service purchases and that organisational structure and the attributes of the purchase situation correlated with the dimensions of the buying group ([Johnston & Bonoma, 1981](https://doi.org/10.1177/002224298104500312), abstract) [independent: 31 firms; correlational; the abstract does not give the country or industries]. Thomas Bonoma's practitioner article of 1982, reissued by Harvard Business Review in 2006, argues, in its publisher's description, that "power does not correlate perfectly with organizational rank"; the same description says "All buyers act selfishly, but they sometimes miscalculate" ([Bonoma, "Major Sales: Who Really Does the Buying?", HBR Classic R0607P](https://store.hbr.org/product/major-sales-who-really-does-the-buying-hbr-classic/R0607P), publisher's description read) [practice].

**How it has aged.** The 2024 editorial argues that the concept needs updating: information technology has moved from being the object bought to a possible agent in buying (an AI theme this course does not treat), servitization (selling a service in place of a product) is changing the roles, especially that of users, and purchasing itself is increasingly outsourced (Ehret et al. 2024) [practice].

**What the transfer is.** Nothing in this section was measured on software, on supply chain execution, or in this decade. Using the five roles to describe the selection of a warehouse, transport or order system is **this unit's inference**, and so is every role card in the deliverable. The roles are useful as a checklist of who might be involved; they say nothing about who holds power in a given selection, which Bonoma's argument above warns against reading from the organisation chart.

## 2. How enterprise software is bought: what the studies opened show, with their samples

None of the studies below is about warehouse, transport or order software. This unit's searches on 28 November 2026 found no peer-reviewed study of a buying committee for execution software: no independent source found. The nearest evidence is on enterprise resource planning (ERP) systems and other packaged software, and on technology buying in general.

**Single and multiple case studies (ERP).** Verville and Halingten followed one ERP acquisition at a holding company for a gas and electric utility and a non-utility energy business; from initiation to completion it took about six months, and they describe it as six iterative, interrelated processes ([Verville & Halingten, 2002, *Management Decision*](https://doi.org/10.1108/00251740210420156), abstract) [independent: one case]. In a second study of four organisations that had acquired ERP, they used Webster and Wind's model to sort the influences on the decision and report that numerous influences emerged, "among the most notable of which was the influence of users" ([Verville & Halingten, 2002, *Qualitative Market Research*](https://doi.org/10.1108/13522750210432995), abstract) [independent: four cases; the abstract does not give their sectors or countries]. A field study of eleven cases of ERP selection, by authors at the University of Cape Town, proposes a selection process and a checklist of criteria and discusses the roles of the selection committee, consultants and vendors ([Das Neves, Fenn & Sulcas, 2004](https://doi.org/10.4102/sajbm.v35i1.651), abstract) [independent: eleven cases; the abstract gives no finding on the roles, and the full text could not be opened].

**What IT managers weigh (as reported).** Olson, Johansson and de Carvalho report Keil and Tiwana's (2006) conjoint study (respondents rate whole product profiles, and the weight of each attribute is inferred from their ratings; working definition), in which MIS managers (heads of management information systems) of large organisations rated software package profiles, with 126 completed responses: software reliability and functionality had the largest effects, then cost (negative), then ease of customisation, with ease of use a smaller effect, and vendor reputation and ease of implementation not significant ([Olson, Johansson & de Carvalho, 2012, p. 67](https://rd.springer.com/content/pdf/10.1007/978-3-642-28827-2_5.pdf)) [practice: Keil and Tiwana's article was not opened, so the origin is not traced and the effect sizes are not repeated here].

**Selection as a social process.** Howcroft and Light followed one small organisation's selection of packaged software over time and argue against the view that selection rationally produces the "best technology solution"; their framework shows how various actors shape the process ([Howcroft & Light, 2010](https://doi.org/10.17705/1jais.00224), abstract) [independent: one longitudinal case]. In an earlier study of a small management consultancy adopting a customer relationship management package, they describe "salesmanship", an activity "directed by the vendor/consultant and focussed on the interests of senior management", which marginalised users' needs and secured the purchase ([Howcroft & Light, 2008](https://doi.org/10.1108/17410390810911203), abstract) [independent: one case].

**Technology buying in general (analyst surveys).** Gartner's page on the business-to-business buying journey says the typical buying group for a complex solution "involves six to 10 decision makers", that buyers considering a purchase spend 17% of the time they give it meeting potential suppliers, and names six buying jobs: problem identification, solution exploration, requirements building, supplier selection, validation and consensus creation ([Gartner, "The B2B Buying Journey", undated, read 28 Nov 2026](https://www.gartner.com/en/sales/insights/b2b-buying-journey)) [analyst: the page states no sample or date]. A Gartner survey of 1,120 respondents at manager level or above, in organisations with at least $1 million in annual revenue in North America, Western Europe and Asia/Pacific, fielded in November and December 2021, found that 74% of technology purchases were funded at least partly by business units outside IT, 26% entirely by IT, that IT provided at least part of the funding in 70% of purchases, and that 75% of respondents using shared funding experienced delays in agreeing the budget ([Gartner press release, 13 April 2022](https://www.gartner.com/en/newsroom/press-releases/2022-03-13-gartner-survey-finds-rise-in-business-technologists-is-driving-funding-for-tech-purchases-outside-of-it)) [analyst]. Gartner's 2026 Tech Buying Behavior Study, published 27 July 2026, reports on AI purchases, which this course does not treat ([Gartner, 27 Jul 2026](https://www.gartner.com/en/articles/tech-buying-behavior-in-the-age-of-ai)) [analyst].

## 3. The people in a selection, one by one

For each role: what the person answers for, what a failed project costs them, and what kind of evidence they trust. The lines are working descriptions unless a source is cited; where a source is cited, the step from its setting to execution software is this unit's inference. SCE12 follows these people through the stages of the sale; SCE16 prices what they argue about.

### 3.1 The operations or supply chain executive

**Answers for.** The cost and service of the operation the system will run: cost per unit handled, orders shipped on time and complete, stock accuracy (the measures of SCE3 to SCE5). Working description.

**What a failed project costs.** The operation degrades at once, because execution software records physical events as they happen (SCE1 §1): if the warehouse system fails at go-live, orders stop leaving the building. Working description; no source opened measures the cost to the person.

**Trusts.** What peers running similar operations report, and what can be seen working on a floor. No source opened tests which evidence this role trusts. Working description.

### 3.2 The IT leader

**Answers for.** The systems estate: whether the new system can be run, secured, integrated with the ERP and the other execution systems (SCE10), and supported for its life. Working description; SCE10's deliverable gives Ravi, Corvane's (fictional) IT director, the order-to-warehouse interface (SCE10 deliverable B row 2).

**What a failed project costs.** The interfaces and the support load land on IT (SCE10 deliverable B places interface ownership with IT at Corvane). Working description.

**Trusts.** Technical evidence: architecture, interface specifications, the vendor's support record. In the conjoint study reported by Olson et al., MIS managers weighted reliability and functionality most and ease of use little (section 2) [practice, origin not traced]. Gartner's survey of late 2021 (published April 2022) found IT paying at least part of 70% of technology purchases (section 2) [analyst], so IT usually holds a share of the budget as well as the technical say.

### 3.3 Finance

**Answers for.** The case for spending: what the system costs over its life and what it returns. Working description; total cost of ownership is SCE6's term (SCE6 §12), and SCE16 breaks it down.

**What a failed project costs.** Money spent with no return, and a budget overrun. In Gartner's survey of late 2021 (published April 2022), 75% of respondents using shared funding reported delays in agreeing how the budget was split (section 2) [analyst]: the conversation finance chairs is where those delays sit (this unit's inference).

**Trusts.** Figures with a source: a cost model, contract terms, the vendor's financial standing. Working description.

### 3.4 Procurement

**Answers for.** The buying process and the contract: the formal request to vendors, the commercial terms, compliance with the company's purchasing rules. This is the "buyers" role of the buying centre (section 1). In one US state's guidance for IT procurements, the procurement lead "Leads the negotiation process" and prepares the contract, the business owner sets the negotiation objectives, and it recommends that business, technical, legal and financial expertise ("these four corners of expertise") be represented on the evaluation team ([VITA, 24.3.1](https://www.vita.virginia.gov/procurement/buy-it-manual/chapter-24---requests-for-proposals-and-competitive-negotiations/2431-putting-together-the-procurement-project-team-ppt-and-evaluation-team-et.html)) [practice: public-sector guidance]; New Zealand's government guidance gives an evaluation panel of 3–5 people ([NZ Government Procurement](https://procurement.govt.nz/procurement/guide-to-procurement/plan-your-procurement/setting-up-the-evaluation-panel)) [practice]. Their transfer to a private company's selection is an inference, drawn in SCE12 §4.

**What a failed project costs.** A contract that does not protect the company when the project goes wrong. Working description. Whether a given contract term is enforceable is a question for the company's legal counsel.

**Trusts.** Written, comparable answers: the same questions answered by every vendor. Working description. The 2024 editorial notes that purchasing itself is increasingly outsourced (section 1) [practice], so this role may sit with an outside firm.

### 3.5 The site managers who will live with it

**Answers for.** The building's cost, service, safety and people (SCE5 §1.5, working). Corvane's (fictional) site manager, Marek, prepares the warehouse-system replacement (SCE5 card 5, register note).

**What a failed project costs.** The go-live happens in their building, on their shift pattern, with their staff; a failed one falls on their shifts and their staff (working).

**Trusts.** Seeing the system run their own work: their order profile, their exceptions. Working description. In Verville and Halingten's four ERP cases, the influence of users was among the most notable (section 2) [independent]; a site manager is the user-side voice a committee is likely to include (this unit's inference; no source opened counts who sits on a committee, section 5).

### 3.6 The outside consultant or integrator

An **integrator** (systems integrator) is a firm that implements software for the customer, alongside or in place of the vendor's own services; a **selection consultant** helps the customer choose. Halvern Systems (fictional) implements through its own services team and through partners [register].

**Answers for.** The process it was hired to run, and, for an integrator, the delivery. Working description.

**What a failed project costs.** Its reputation and its next engagement. Working description.

**Trusts.** Its own experience of other projects. Working description. The research opened warns about the role's interests: in Howcroft and Light's 2008 case, salesmanship directed by the vendor or consultant at senior management's interests marginalised users' needs, and their practical implications note that external parties may have interests that differ from the organisation's (section 2) [independent: one case]. The 2024 editorial notes that external actors can complement or substitute for buying-centre roles (section 1) [practice].

### 3.7 The executive sponsor

The **executive sponsor** is the senior person who asks for the money, owns the decision in front of the board or the leadership team, and answers for the outcome. At Corvane (fictional), Edda, vice-president supply chain, sponsors the replacement; at Tandric Logistics (fictional), Karin, commercial director, signs client contracts and sponsors software purchases [register].

**Answers for.** The outcome the project was approved to deliver. Working description.

**What a failed project costs.** The sponsor's standing, because the sponsor's name is on the approval. Working description; no source opened measures it.

**Trusts.** The judgement of the people around them, and evidence from peers. Working description. Bonoma's argument that power does not follow rank (section 1) [practice] is the reason to ask who the sponsor listens to before assuming the sponsor decides.

## 4. How the room differs by kind of operation

Section 3 described the roles in general. The room changes with what the operation sells. Everything in this section is this unit's inference from the sources and units named; no study opened compares buying committees across these kinds of company.

**A retailer.** For a retailer the warehouse serves stores and a website, and the order system makes the promise to the customer (SCE9). E-commerce and store operations have a stake in a warehouse or order system as well as supply chain, so the room includes people whose measure is the customer's order (inference from SCE9's roles; Corvane's e-commerce operations role, Lena, is SCE9's case fact). Corvane (fictional) is deciding whether to replace its warehouse system and whether to buy order and transport systems from the same vendor [register]: that second question brings the order system's owners into a warehouse decision.

**A distributor.** A distributor such as Ostrel Foods (fictional), a food distributor with its own fleet of 40 vehicles [register], runs warehouse and transport as one evening-to-morning operation (SCE5 card 4, register note). The operations director, Aart at Ostrel [register], is likely to be both the operations executive and the sponsor, with fewer people in the room (inference from the register, whose Ostrel rows name an operations director and, from the planning course, a finance director, Joris, and no IT role).

**A manufacturer.** A manufacturer's warehouse may serve its own plants and customers from an ERP that already runs production and finance (working description; no source opened gives how often). Where the ERP vendor offers warehouse and transport as modules, as Varnby Software (fictional) does to manufacturers already on its ERP, saying of itself "No integration to build" [register], the ERP's owners in IT and finance carry more weight in the room (inference). The case register has no manufacturer, so no card is built for one.

**A logistics provider.** For a logistics provider, software is part of what it sells. Tandric (fictional) is paid by activity for warehousing and at a margin for freight, serves nine clients from one shared warehouse system, and must bring a new client live within weeks of signature [register]. SCE8 §4 sets out where a provider's needs part from a shipper's: rates on both sides, many clients inside one system, the client as a user through a portal, billing. So the commercial director sponsors the purchase [register], the onboarding lead who configures each client (Luis) has a direct stake, and the provider's clients are in the room in a sense: their contracts and reports set requirements, though they sign nothing with the software vendor (inference from SCE8 §4). SCE8 §4 also reports that Gartner's 2026 transport evaluation is written around shippers' needs [analyst], so a provider's committee reads analyst material written for another kind of buyer. DD-SCE1 develops the provider's business.

## 5. Who is not in the room

The people who will use the system every shift (the operators, supervisors, inventory controllers and planners of SCE5) are the "users" of the buying-centre roles (section 1). Gartner's page counts the buying group in decision makers (section 2) [analyst]; nothing opened says how often warehouse users are among them. In the studies opened, the users' place varies: in Verville and Halingten's four ERP cases their influence was among the most notable [independent]; in Howcroft and Light's 2008 case salesmanship marginalised their needs [independent]. Both are small qualitative studies, and neither is in a warehouse.

**What the research on mandated software says.** Use of a warehouse system is mandated: the operator scans because the job requires it (working description). Brown, Massey, Montoya-Weiss and Burkman note that technology acceptance research (which finds usefulness and ease of use the primary drivers of intentions to adopt) "has been conducted primarily in environments in which adoption was voluntary", argue that the relationships will differ when use is mandated, and report a field study in the banking industry that found such differences ([Brown et al., 2002](https://doi.org/10.1057/palgrave.ejis.3000438), abstract) [independent: one field study in banking; the abstract gives no sample size and does not say which relationships differed]. Venkatesh and Davis tested an extended model with data on four systems at four organisations (N = 156), two with voluntary and two with mandatory use, measured before implementation and one and three months after; social influence (subjective norm, voluntariness, image) and cognitive factors (job relevance, output quality, result demonstrability, perceived ease of use) both significantly influenced acceptance ([Venkatesh & Davis, 2000](https://doi.org/10.1287/mnsc.46.2.186.11926), abstract) [independent; the abstract does not give the organisations' industries].

**What the research on user participation says.** A meta-analysis of 82 empirical studies found that "user participation is minimally-to-moderately beneficial" to information systems development, with stronger effects on attitudes and behaviour than on productivity ([He & King, 2008](https://jmis-web.org/articles/493), abstract on the journal's editorial site) [independent: the abstract gives no effect sizes]. The studies concern systems being developed, not packages being bought.

**What follows, as this unit's inference.** Mandated use removes the users' choice to adopt; it does not remove what they think of the system, which the mandated-use research says changes how acceptance works. A committee that buys without the floor has its evidence from the demonstration and the reference customers, not from the people who will carry the device. Whether users then work around a system they dislike is common claim C14, which SCE13 tests; this unit makes no finding on it. SCE15 builds the two-sided case (the buyer's reason to pay against the user's reason to use).

## 6. What analysts' buying guides and published selection guides tell buyers

**One analyst's selection guide, read in full.** ARC Advisory Group's page "Warehouse Management System Selection" (undated; read 28 November 2026) tells groups responsible for purchasing to "properly define their organization's requirements, prioritize, and pay for what their organization requires most", recommends a cross-functional evaluation of requirements for a holistic view of needs, and a cost-benefit analysis; it lists criteria under business goals, product or solution functions and features (architecture, functionality, customer support, leadership, organisation), and service requirements (project and lifecycle), and names as things to check functionality, total cost of ownership, integration with business and material handling systems, usability, implementation support and training, and the supplier's long-term viability and vision; it ends by offering ARC's selection services ([ARC Advisory Group, "Warehouse Management System Selection"](https://www.arcweb.com/technology-evaluation-and-selection/warehouse-management-systems)) [analyst: the page also offers paid services; it names no vendor and no committee roles]. It does not mention demonstrations or reference checks.

**Two analyst abstracts.** Forrester's *Demystifying The Technical Functions Of Warehouse Management Systems* (Swerdlow, Kalra and Nagel, 12 September 2024) says, in its abstract's second sentence: "Vendors' marketing messages and a lack of standardization amplify the challenge" of identifying which solution solves a business problem, and applies Forrester's framework of standard functions per market category to WMSes ([Forrester, RES181465](https://www.forrester.com/report/demystifying-the-technical-functions-of-warehouse-management-systems/RES181465), abstract) [analyst]. Forrester's *Lessons Learned From The Forrester Wave: Order Management Systems, Q1 2025* (28 April 2025) says "Most reference customers we spoke with are happy with their vendor partners" ([Forrester, RES182379](https://www.forrester.com/report/lessons-learned-from-the-forrester-wave-tm-order-management-systems-q1-2025/RES182379), abstract) [analyst]; its abstract also covers generative AI use cases, which this course does not treat. Read with SCE1 §8, where vendors supply the reference customers Forrester interviews, the satisfaction is that of customers the vendors put forward (this unit's inference). Both reports were read in abstract on Forrester's site; a copy of a Forrester report on a vendor's site is one that vendor bought reprint rights to post (SCE1 §8) [analyst].

**Gartner.** SCE1 §8 read in abstract Gartner's 2024 note on how Magic Quadrants are evaluated, which advises using one to narrow a list of providers and "don't base your decision on this evaluation only" [analyst, as SCE1 read it]. Gartner's document pages failed when this unit tried them on 28 November 2026, so no Gartner selection guide or toolkit was read here, and none is described.

**Published selection guides.** The other selection guides and checklists this unit's searches found were published by software vendors, by integrators or by trade magazines; under the course's sourcing standard they are evidence of what their publishers claim and are not used here. SCE12 takes up published requirement templates and selection guides from named bodies.

What the guides read here tell a buyer to ask, put together (this unit's inference from the three analyst pages above): define and rank your own requirements before looking at products; evaluate across functions; ask for total cost, not price; check integration and the supplier's viability; and discount vendor language. None says who should be in the room by name of role.

## 7. What public sources cannot show about any real selection

- **Who was in the room.** No public source opened names the committee of any real selection of execution software. Vendor case studies are vendor-reported (SCE1 §9): what they show of a selection is what the vendor and its customer agreed to publish.
- **Why the losing vendors lost.** Nothing public records the scoring, the shortlist or the reasons, and no company opened publishes its win rate (the course's rule: no win rate of a private company unless it published it).
- **What was paid.** No public figure, unless a listed company discloses it in a filing.
- **Who vetoed.** A veto leaves no public trace; the "can veto" line of every card in the deliverable is a working description.
- **Whether the users agreed.** Peer-review sites hold what reviewers say, with vendor-invited reviewers among them (SCE1 §7); they are not a record of the floor's view.

So a buyer card for a real company is a hypothesis to be checked with people who were there: sales, services and the customer's own staff.

## On a site visit

This unit is not about an operation, so the questions are for colleagues in sales, services or product, about a selection they took part in.

- **Ask who was in the room at each stage, by role, and who joined late** (sections 1, 3). Set the answer against the seven roles of section 3.
- **Ask who could have stopped the deal, and whether anyone tried** (section 3; the "can veto" line).
- **Ask where the money came from: one budget or several** (section 2: Gartner's 2021 survey on shared funding and its delays).
- **Ask whether anyone who works a shift saw the system before signature, and how** (section 5).
- **Ask who supplied the reference customers, and what the buyer asked them** (section 6; SCE1 §8).
- **Ask whether an outside consultant or integrator ran the selection, and whom it worked for** (section 3.6).
- **At a logistics provider, ask which client's requirements drove the choice** (section 4).
- **Ask what the buyer read: which analyst report, guide or review site, and in what form** (section 6; SCE1 §8).

## Self-check

1. **How old is the buying-centre research, and in what setting was it built?**
   The seminal works date from 1967 and 1972, published more than 45 years ago (Ehret et al. 2024); Johnston and Bonoma's 1981 study measured purchases of capital equipment and industrial services in 31 firms. Its transfer to execution software is this unit's inference (section 1).
2. **Name the five roles as the 2024 editorial lists them.**
   Influencers, gatekeepers, buyers, users and decision-makers; external actors can complement or substitute for them (section 1).
3. **What did Verville and Halingten find in four ERP acquisitions?**
   Among the many influences, the influence of users was among the most notable; four cases, abstract only (section 2).
4. **What did the Gartner survey published in April 2022 find about funding, and on what sample?**
   74% of technology purchases funded at least partly outside IT, 26% by IT alone, IT paying part in 70%, and delays for 75% of those sharing funding; 1,120 respondents at manager level or above, three regions, late 2021 (section 2).
5. **Why does mandated use matter for warehouse software?**
   Acceptance research was built mostly where adoption was voluntary; Brown et al. found the relationships differ when use is mandated (one banking field study). An operator must use the warehouse system, so the voluntary findings cannot be assumed (section 5).
6. **What did Howcroft and Light find about consultants in one small firm's selection?**
   Salesmanship directed by the vendor or consultant at senior management's interests marginalised users' needs; one case (section 2, section 3.6).
7. **What does ARC's selection guide tell buyers, and what does it leave out?**
   Define and prioritise requirements, evaluate across functions, run a cost-benefit analysis, check total cost, integration, usability and supplier viability; it says nothing of demonstrations, reference checks or committee roles (section 6).
8. **Why is a logistics provider's committee different?**
   The provider sells what the software supports: the commercial director sponsors, the onboarding lead has a direct stake, and clients' contracts set requirements; analyst evaluations are written around shippers (section 4, from SCE8 §4).
